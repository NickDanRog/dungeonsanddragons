# The Crossing: A Player's Guide

*A note on this document: This guide describes the Perfect Isles and the world around them as a resident understands it. Some of what follows is incomplete. Some of it is wrong — not in error but by design: this is what people believe, not necessarily what is true. Your character does not know which is which.*

---

## Welcome to the Realm

### The World Between Worlds

The Perfect Isles exist because of a single act. Six generations ago, a group of exiles — dispossessed, pursued, with little left to them — used what remained of their magic to carve a sanctuary from the Middle Sea and conceal it from the world that had expelled them. The barrier they raised has held. The Isles they built have endured. This is the founding fact of the Archipelago, and it is known to every child who grows up here: the Isles were made by people who had nearly nothing, and what they built has lasted.

What they built was not a government. The founders had seen what a single power holding authority over all others does to the people beneath it; they had been on the receiving end of it. The Archipelago was shaped — if shaped is the right word for something built under duress — to resist that outcome. Thirty-odd factions hold the Isles between them, each with its own doctrine, its own practices, its own claim on the Archipelago's identity. None of them governs alone. All of them argue. Decisions are made, eventually, through the quarterly council meetings, through alliance and negotiation and occasional open conflict, through the accumulated pressure of people who cannot afford to let the whole arrangement collapse. The result is inefficient, contentious, and, by almost any measure, working.

The Isles are not a paradise. People here know the sanctuary is real but not permanent; they have always known it. The barrier could fail. The Unity, the great power that drove the founders out, does not know the Archipelago exists — yet. The two continents that bracket the Middle Sea are not static, and the Isles are not insulated from what happens on them, however much the cautious factions would prefer otherwise. The spirit halls and the markets at Dremma and the quarterly debates that never resolve cleanly are not in spite of that tension but because of it. People decided to build something here rather than simply wait.

Into all of this, the Oracle has spoken. Not a prophecy of war or ruin — something more specific, and more personal. You are the reason for it. The adventure that follows takes you out of the Archipelago and into the wider world: the Sacred Lands to the east, eventually the Unity to the west, and into contact with forces old enough that the Isles' six generations read as a recent development. The founders built something worth protecting. Whether it survives — whether the world the barrier has kept them hidden from can be met and changed rather than simply endured — is the question the Oracle believes your Circle is positioned to answer.

### Life in the Isles

Dremma is the closest thing the Archipelago has to a capital: the largest settlement, the main trading hub, the place where most factions maintain at least a presence. It sits on Kartos's Isle — the largest of the Archipelago's islands, known to most residents simply as the Big Island — which also holds Heida, the Wayfinders' headquarters and primary departure point for the inter-island routes, and Sanctuary, the Oracle's seat, at least a day's walk inland. Beyond Kartos's Isle, the Archipelago spreads across the Middle Sea in a scatter of islands ranging from farming communities to faction strongholds to outcroppings too small to chart that are known only to the Wayfinders who pass them. If you need to get somewhere, you find a Wayfinder.

Spirit contact is a part of ordinary life in the Isles, though it does not look the same everywhere. Most residents have encountered a spirit in some form — the small ones appear near streams, at old forest edges, at crossroads where travelers have stopped long enough to leave something behind. The larger ones, the named ones, are less visible and more present. Certain factions — the Greensingers, the Hidden Vale Guardians, and others — have spent generations developing their own traditions for approaching spirits: how to leave an offering that will be accepted, how to listen when a presence is near, how to read what a spirit is willing to give. These traditions are functional. They emerged independently across different factions and carry the character of whoever developed them, which means they are not identical and their practitioners are not always in agreement. What is consistent across all of them is the underlying fact: spirits are real, they can be reached by those who know how, and the factions who hold that knowledge are worth taking seriously.

The Isles feel, to someone who grew up in them, like home and hiding place at once — and the tension between those two things is not resolved, only lived with. There is warmth here: the closeness of a small world, the comfort of faction membership, the knowledge that the people around you are all, in the most literal sense, in the same position. There is also weight. The barrier has felt different in recent months, in ways that the practitioners who monitor it have not been able to account for to their own satisfaction. The spirits have been restless. The Oracle has spoken three times since the last quarterly meeting at Dremma. No one has agreed on what any of it means, which is a different situation from the usual one, where at least people disagree from established positions. Now some of them are not sure they have a position. They are watching and waiting and not quite saying so out loud.

## The World Around You

### The Perfect Isles

The Archipelago covers a considerable stretch of the Middle Sea, though it is scattered rather than arranged — clusters of islands interrupted by open water, isolated outcroppings that appear on no official charts, and a few larger islands that have served as natural anchors for the communities built on them. The Isles do not have a single geographic center; what organization they have is practical, organized around the Wayfinder routes that connect inhabited islands and the semi-regular Council meetings at the Council Site near Sanctuary on Kartos's Isle that provide the Archipelago its closest equivalent to a common political space.

The barrier that conceals the Archipelago from outside observation is understood, in broad terms, by every resident: it exists, it has held since the founding, and it is the reason six generations of Isles residents have lived without direct contact with the powers that would otherwise have found them. The specifics of how it functions, who maintains it, and what threatens its integrity are held primarily by the Seawatch, who treat that knowledge as the Archipelago's most sensitive operational asset and distribute it accordingly. What the general population knows is that the barrier requires active maintenance; that it is not self-sustaining; and that anything which might make the Isles visible to outside observers is treated as a collective threat rather than an individual choice.

The Archipelago does not have a government in any institutional sense. When the Council is called to session, it brings together representatives from across the recognized factions to negotiate, propose, and argue. Agreements reached there are binding only insofar as the parties to them feel accountable for honoring them, and factions that have declined to be bound by a given decision have done so with some regularity throughout Archipelago history. What prevents this from becoming complete dysfunction is the set of shared stakes that cannot be addressed by any single faction: barrier maintenance, Wayfinder infrastructure, collective defense against the possibility of discovery. On those matters, the record shows that the factions have consistently found their way to working arrangements. On everything else, the Council is a prolonged argument that nonetheless, eventually, produces results.

### The Two Continents

The Middle Sea sits between two landmasses, and the Archipelago knows both of them primarily through the people who arrived from them.

The Unity lies to the west. It is large, industrially capable, and possessed of the kind of organizational coherence that makes it dangerous: a single authority structure with significant military reach and an economy that requires resources it cannot produce in sufficient quantity within its own borders. Magic is outlawed in the Unity, as is formal worship of any kind. The founders' histories describe a place that had been moving in this direction for some time before the expulsion; by accounts from those who have made the crossing in recent years, the process has continued. The land in the Unity's core territories has been declining — less productive, less responsive — in ways the Unity's own authorities have not explained to their population's satisfaction. The Unity does not know the Archipelago exists. This has been the fundamental condition of the Isles since the founding, and it is the condition on which everything else depends.

The Sacred Lands lie to the east, larger than the Unity and considerably less consolidated. The peoples there include many who share ancestry with the Archipelago's founders; certain factions in the Isles maintain informal contact with communities in the Sacred Lands, and a steady if small number of people have made the crossing in both directions over the generations. The Sacred Lands have their own political complexity, their own conflicts, and their own relationship to the spirit world, which is by all available accounts more intact than anywhere else accessible to the Archipelago. The Unity has been expanding its presence in eastern territories in recent years. The Sacred Lands are not under Unity control. The direction of movement is not encouraging.

### The Quiet Change

Something has shifted in the past year, and the Archipelago has not found a satisfactory explanation for it. The spirits that practitioners have spent generations learning to approach have been behaving differently — more present in some cases, harder to read in others, occasionally responding in ways that contradict what previous experience would suggest. The Seawatch have described anomalies in the barrier's behavior while declining to characterize what those anomalies indicate. The Oracle has spoken three times since the last quarterly meeting, which is considerably more than her usual rate.

None of these developments, taken individually, constitutes evidence of anything specific. People are aware of that. They are also aware that several of them have occurred together over a short period, following a long stretch of relative stability, and that the practitioners best positioned to evaluate what is happening have not offered reassurance. The usual Archipelago debates continue. Beneath them, there is a quality of attention that was not present a year ago — a sense, distributed across factions that otherwise share little, that something is being waited for, and that no one has said clearly what it is.

## Society and Culture

### The Spirit Traditions

Spirits are real. In the Isles this is not a matter of faith but of accumulated experience: a practical observation documented across generations, as well-attested as the tides and considerably more variable in behavior. The Archipelago occupies a part of the Middle Sea where spirit presence is unusually strong, a fact the founders recognized and which every community that followed them has had to reckon with. Most Isles residents have encountered a spirit in some form. The small ones appear near water, at old forest edges, along paths that have been regularly traveled for long enough to accumulate the attention of something not quite human. They respond to notice, to offerings, to being known.

The named spirits — larger, more coherent, capable of something closer to genuine exchange — require more than casual attention. Several factions have built their traditions around understanding and approaching them. The Greensingers have developed a set of cultivation practices, rooted in land-tending and long observation, through which practitioners can enter sustained contact with a spirit willing to be found. The Hidden Vale Guardians hold a different tradition — older in form, more restricted in who may learn it, built around precise language and the principle of careful reciprocity. Other factions have their own methods, some formal, some pragmatic, some inherited from the founders' original practices in forms that have changed significantly over six generations of Isles conditions. None of these traditions are identical, and their practitioners do not always agree about what spirits are, what they want, or what the relationship between a practitioner and a named spirit should look like at its most developed. All of them agree that the work is real, that results are possible for those who learn their tradition well, and that doing it without proper preparation is noticed.

What the traditions share is the conviction that spirits are not objects of worship but presences to be known — that the relationship is one of extended acquaintance rather than devotion, built through offerings, attention, and demonstrated reliability over time.

### The Oracle

The Oracle is the Archipelago's oldest institution and its only fully neutral one. The Oracle, and the faction that supports her, occupy a pagoda just outside of Sanctuary, a small village at least a day's walk inland from Dremma. The Council site lies close by; both the Oracle's residence and the Archipelago's primary political forum occupy this quiet inland valley, which has its own logic — the Oracle's presence lends weight to what the Council decides, and the Council's proximity ensures her words have an immediate audience. The small community that maintains the pagoda and serves the Oracle works hard to provide her with insulation from faction politics. She does not belong to any faction and does not involve herself in their disputes. No faction has successfully claimed her endorsement, though several have tried to interpret her words as one. The community at Sanctuary is not forthcoming about the details of her life, her origins, or how the position of Oracle is transferred when it needs to be. The Isles do not know, and the Isles have learned not to press the question in ways that expect a response.

What the Oracle does is speak. Her pronouncements come without warning or obvious pattern and are delivered to whoever is present, recorded by the Sanctuary community, and distributed to the factions in the form of written copies. The Isles do not agree on how to interpret them; different factions maintain different reading traditions, and a body of Archipelago scholarship has accumulated around the question of what her words have historically meant versus what they were assumed to mean at the time of delivery. What the factions do agree on, despite themselves, is that her words are worth knowing. The documented record of events matching Oracle speech — sometimes in ways that required considerable interpretive effort to recognize — is long enough that dismissing her entirely has come to seem like a posture rather than a conclusion. She is currently speaking more than she has in living memory. The factions have their usual range of responses.

### The Faction Council

The Archipelago has no government. What it has is a Council, which meets only when called — by the Oracle, or by any three recognized factions acting together. When a session is called, each faction may send its standing representative; no faction is under formal obligation to attend, and a faction that does not participate is choosing not to be bound by the session's outcome. The meeting has no enforcement authority and no standing mechanism for compelling compliance beyond this. Agreements reached there are binding only insofar as the parties to them feel accountable for honoring them, and factions have declined to honor agreements with enough regularity that this is considered a normal feature of Archipelago political life rather than a crisis. The meetings usually dissolve in argument. Decisions emerge anyway, eventually, because the Archipelago's shared stakes are large enough that sustained non-cooperation costs more than the concessions required to reach a working arrangement.

The Wayfinders operate outside this political structure in a practical sense. They run the inter-island routes, maintain the craft that knit the Archipelago together, and have remained formally unaligned with any faction since the founding. Hiring a Wayfinder is not a political act, which is one of the reasons they are trusted across factional boundaries in ways that almost no other organization is. They do not make policy. They make things work, which in the Isles is a different and arguably more important function.

### The First Crossing

The Isles were built by people driven out of a homeland they did not leave voluntarily. The power that expelled them — the Unity, or the predecessor state that became the Unity, depending on which faction's historical account one accepts — did so with enough force to make return impossible by ordinary means and enough speed to make preparation incomplete. What the founders brought with them was their combined magical power — the accumulated traditions of dozens of separate backgrounds and teachings, wielded together under desperate circumstances.

That effort became the barrier. What it was in origin, what it cost to use, and what has sustained it over two centuries remain contested questions in Archipelago scholarship, which has had six generations to consider them and has not reached conclusions that all factions accept. What is agreed is that the founders understood the magic would require ongoing tending to remain functional, that they shaped the Archipelago's institutional structure with that requirement in mind, and that the Seawatch have held the relevant knowledge since. The Isles exists because people in desperate circumstances made a set of decisions under pressure and those decisions, by any measure, worked.

The people who make the crossing now — arriving from the Unity, from the Sacred Lands, finding the Archipelago by being found by the Wayfinders — arrive at something the founders could not have fully anticipated: a community that is, in the practical and social sense, functioning, but remains divided against itself. What it means to enter it as someone who remembers a different world, what one carries away from that world that the Isles does not have and cannot provide, and what it costs to belong to a place built entirely by people who understand what it means to have been removed from somewhere — these are questions the Archipelago has been answering, individually and collectively, for generations.

### The History of the Unity

The western continent spent approximately a thousand years at war with itself before a single figure ended it. **Sister Neema** was a genuine prophet — her divinatory gifts were real, her predictions unfailing throughout her lifetime. She founded the movement called the **One True Way** on two propositions: that the future is determined, and that mortal ingenuity, not divine power, would bring the world to its fulfillment. The exhaustion of centuries of accumulated violence made her promise irresistible, and the movement spread rapidly. After her death, her successors lost access to her gifts and continued through fraud — manufacturing the prophecies she would have made and arranging for their fulfillment by any means available. The deception was sustained across generations. In its name, the One True Way launched the **Last War**: a military campaign that unified the western continent under a single authority, outlawed magic and the worship of named deities, and suppressed or removed the peoples and traditions that would not convert.

Approximately three hundred years ago, senior members of the One True Way's own enforcement orders uncovered evidence of the long-running fraud. A half-orc named **Maakan** spent a decade carefully spreading that evidence before the civil war it triggered — the **Great Schism** — finally came. The war lasted a generation. Maakan's faction prevailed. They took the name the **Unity**, preserved what they understood as Neema's genuine teachings while rejecting the institution that had corrupted them, installed a system of direct democracy with firm checks on the concentration of power, and committed to the advancement of material knowledge and civic welfare as the founding purpose of the new state. The suppression of magic and divine worship was retained — not as the One True Way's continuation but as a deliberate rejection of what that world had been, and a commitment to a different kind of future.

The Unity that emerged is three centuries old and genuinely democratic: every citizen, regardless of race or gender, participates in governance through a cascading council system, and the social infrastructure it has built is real — no citizen goes unhoused, unfed, or without care if it can be prevented. Its technology outpaces anything in the Archipelago or the Sacred Lands substantially; its cities are gas-lit, its major routes carry steam-powered locomotives, and dirigibles move passengers and freight between cities in a manner that, by consistent testimony from those who have seen it, defies description before witnessing. The civic warmth of its people is not manufactured. The standard of living across most of the continent is, by any material measure, the highest in the world.

The land is failing nonetheless. Crops yield less with each generation; resources once plentiful grow scarce; the deterioration is uneven but consistent and worsening. The Unity attributes this to resource exhaustion and has responded by expanding into the Sacred Lands — a colonial project its citizens understand as necessity. The Isles' knowledge of current Unity conditions is assembled from the Keepers' intelligence network, the testimony of the Returned, and the accounts of recent arrivals; it is fuller than it was a generation ago and still substantially incomplete. What is consistent across all sources is the land's decline, the growing pressure on the eastern continent, and the character of Unity civic life — which those who have left it describe, uniformly, as something that cannot be conveyed accurately to someone who has not lived inside it.

The Isles' own existence traces to the Unity's first century. The suppression of magic did not end with the One True Way — the Unity retained it, as a deliberate rejection of what that world had been. Within a hundred years of the Unity's founding, a coalition of magical practitioners fleeing that continued suppression used their combined power to make the first crossing. What they built became the Archipelago.

## The Factions of the Archipelago

The Archipelago's thirty-three recognized factions represent the full range of traditions, philosophies, and practices that the original exiles carried with them from the Unity. No two are alike, and many are in active disagreement on matters they consider fundamental. What all of them share is the memory of the crossing, the knowledge of what drove them here, and the understanding that what the Isles has built is worth preserving.

### Faction Spotlights

The following factions represent some of the most natural starting points for a character entering this adventure. What follows are working introductions to each; full entries for all factions are maintained by the Pentad at the Nexus.

**The Godcallers.** The Godcallers are a monastic sect whose entire practice is organized around remembering what the Unity tried to make the world forget. They maintain detailed records of the deities worshiped across the western continent before the One True Way's purge — their names, their stories, their rites — and can often be heard chanting those names as they go about ordinary life. Their robes are black and tattered, sewn with white ribbons bearing the names of suppressed gods and fragments of scripture; the ribbons accumulate over a practitioner's lifetime, and an elder Godcaller's robes carry thousands. A character with the Godcallers carries a particular quality of awareness: a catalogued knowledge of loss, a commitment to the proposition that what was taken can still be restored, and a relationship to the Unity's suppression that is not theoretical. They are the Archipelago's primary institutional memory of what was erased, and that knowledge has a way of becoming directly relevant.

**The Blue Star Adepts.** The Blue Star Adepts combine druidic practice with significant arcane study, making them among the most technically literate factions in the Isles — and among the most comfortable with technology, which they use more readily than most, though carefully. Comparisons to the Unity carry a specific weight in the Archipelago, and the Adepts are aware of this. They serve the community as healers — the first faction called for serious injury or illness that simpler care has not addressed — and their study of astronomy grounds both their practical and spiritual work in ways other factions find difficult to replicate. A character from the Blue Star Adepts enters the adventure with skills that are immediately and broadly useful, and a breadth of knowledge across disciplines — druidic, arcane, medical, astronomical — that most practitioners have not tried to hold at once.

**The Wayfinders.** The Wayfinders are the most broadly respected faction in the Archipelago, and arguably the most indispensable. Their primary work — seeking out magical practitioners and others seeking escape from the Unity and bringing them safely to the Isles — is both necessary and dangerous, and their members are selected for it accordingly. They also work in close partnership with the Seawatch to manage access through the Barrier — the sending stones, route guidance, and return crossings that make travel to and from the outside world possible — giving them a structural role in the Isles' security that no other faction shares. Their headquarters at Heida on Kartos's Isle is perpetually busy; the work never stops. A character from the Wayfinders has already done the thing that most people in the Isles only discuss theoretically: gone out past the Barrier, into the world that does not know the Archipelago exists, and come back. They understand, practically rather than abstractly, what is on the other side.

**The Greensingers.** The Greensingers are a small, insular faction deeply attuned to the minor nature spirits of the islands. Their practice of approaching these spirits — what they call spirit singing — is incidental to daily life in their community rather than a formalized ritual; they maintain a genuine, informal relationship with the Isles' spiritual ecology that is unlike anything in the broader Archipelago. They know more about the native spirits of the Perfect Isles than any other faction, and their knowledge is built from the ground up, specific to these islands, developed over generations of living closely with them rather than adapted from the founders' western practices. A character from the Greensingers carries a quality of attention to the living world that most practitioners develop only after years of careful work, and an understanding of the Isles' spirit ecology that no other faction can match.

**The Troubadours.** The Troubadours — singers, musicians, artists, theatre companies — are one of the only genuine sources of shared cultural life across the Archipelago's many communities. They travel freely, are welcomed everywhere, and occupy a position simultaneously frivolous and essential: the keepers of a shared identity among people who agree on very little else. They are one of the few Isles factions to travel regularly to the Sacred Lands, where their performances are received with considerable enthusiasm by peoples who understand what it means to carry a living culture across an ocean. A character from the Troubadours has access of a particular kind: to people who would not open their doors to a stranger in most circumstances, and to stories that only move across social distances through music and performance.

**The Lodge of Perfection.** The Lodge of Perfection is a formal academy organized around the principle that the use of weapons can be elevated to an art form equivalent to any other. They offer training to anyone willing to commit to their rigorous standard, regardless of faction affiliation, and they are one of the few institutions in the Isles that openly advocates for military preparation against the Unity. The Lodge does not wait for that argument to be settled; it trains. What has grown from this is the Isles' most broadly respected martial institution and its single largest source of prepared fighters. A character from the Lodge enters the story with practical capability, connections that cut across factional lines, and membership in an institution that has been making the case — in the most concrete terms available — that the Archipelago will eventually need to fight.

**The Pentad.** The Pentad are researchers and scholars organized around five disciplines — Chronicles, Literature, Poetry, Preservation, and Natural History — and operate from the Nexus, a substantial library in the village of Candela on Santama. They are the Archipelago's primary institutional memory for the world outside the spirit traditions: they produce most of the Isles' written scholarship, hold its most comprehensive historical and cultural record, and are the place the rest of the Archipelago turns to when it needs documentation rather than memory. Their relationship with the Founders' Keep — the Isles' other major historical institution — is collegial rivalry maintained across generations. A character from the Pentad carries a specific kind of knowledge: what has been documented, where the archive's gaps are, and what the questions look like when enough of the record is assembled to see them clearly. They have access to the Nexus itself — the most complete written record the Isles possesses.

**The Returned.** The Returned are former Unity citizens who made the crossing and have not stopped arguing about what it means. Their position — that the Archipelago cannot sustain a permanent defensive posture, that liberation of those still under Unity governance is an obligation rather than a geopolitical stance — is not popular across the faction council, and they make it anyway. They are viewed with suspicion by many other factions, who question where loyalties formed inside the Unity actually lie; the Returned have been living with this suspicion since they organized. What they cannot be questioned on is knowledge: they hold the most current firsthand understanding of Unity society, culture, and institutions of any group on the Isles. A character from the Returned carries direct experience of the world the founders escaped and a clear-eyed sense of what it looks like from inside — not imagined, not inherited, but lived.

**The Seawatch.** The Seawatch patrol the Middle Sea along the Barrier's western edge, maintaining constant vigilance for anything approaching from Unity territory. Their methods rely on animals of sea and sky as advance scouts — a network of creature contacts that extends well beyond the range of any crewed vessel — and on the Barrier's own magic, which can be directed to turn approaching ships without betraying the Isles' location. They are the Archipelago's first line of defense against discovery and are treated by the Council with the seriousness that role requires. A character from the Seawatch has spent extended time in the space between the Isles and everything outside them, working the edge of the thing that makes the Archipelago possible. They understand, practically and in detail, what the Barrier actually feels like — and what it means when it behaves in ways it has not behaved before.

**The Greenwardens.** The Greenwardens have taken it upon themselves to protect the wild places of the Archipelago from the encroachment of the Isles' growing settlements — ensuring that the natural landscape the community was built within does not disappear under the civilization it shelters. They are combative by inclination and have made significant trouble for those they judge to be upsetting the natural balance. Most factions find them irritating on the subject. Nearly all acknowledge that without them, the islands' natural character would have been gone generations ago. A character from the Greenwardens is familiar with parts of the Archipelago that most residents never see — the unclaimed islands, the unmanaged stretches of coast and forest, the places no faction has organized — and carries a willingness to defend those places that does not stop at discomfort.

## Character Creation

### A Note on Character Origins

Two distinct starting points are equally valid for characters entering this adventure.

**Isles-born.** You grew up in the Archipelago — the product of generations of exile, or of a family that arrived long enough ago that the Isles are simply home. You know the factions because they are the fabric of ordinary life. You understand spirit contact as background reality rather than revelation. What you have is fluency: the cultural knowledge that comes from growing up inside something rather than arriving at it. What you may lack is perspective. People who have never seen the outside sometimes find, when they finally do, that they had assumptions about it they did not know they held.

**Returned from the Unity.** You made the crossing within living memory — rescued, escaped, or simply ran. The Isles is sanctuary; it may not yet feel like home. You carry something that Isles-born characters do not: direct knowledge of what the founders left behind, and an ingraned understanding that comes from having lived inside the world the Archipelago was built to hide from. What you understand about the Unity is not theoretical, its part of your lived experience. What you may struggle with is the Archipelago itself — the fractious, argument-heavy, faction-layered community that has built itself around the practice of living well in hiding, which is not always the same thing as living freely.

Both origins produce characters with something essential to contribute and something the adventure will test.

### Races

The Archipelago draws its population from the full diversity of peoples who have, over several generations, made their way to the Isles. Humans are the most common. The common races — half-elves, halflings, and gnomes — are well represented throughout the faction communities. Elves and dwarves are present but somewhat less numerous, their communities often self-contained and oriented around the preservation of cultural practices that arrived with the founding generation. The rare races — half-orcs, tieflings, aasimar, and genasi — are few enough that most people encounter them only occasionally, though the Isles' tradition of accepting all comers means they are met without hostility.

*Anthropomorphic peoples — Tortles, Harengons, and others — are inhabitants of the Sacred Lands. They are encountered in play but are not available as character races.*

#### Humans

Very common throughout the Archipelago. Humans make up the majority of the population in every major settlement and hold roles across all thirty-three factions without distinction. Their range is the point: a human character is unremarkable in the Isles, which means they can move through its communities without their race being the first thing anyone notices. Most of the founders were human; most of the Archipelago's governing tradition, such as it is, was shaped by human political instincts. The Pathist philosophy — the Archipelago's most prevalent — emerged from human spiritual traditions, though it has been adapted by every race that encountered it. To be human in the Isles is to be entirely ordinary, which is its own form of freedom.

#### Half-Elves

Common, and deeply woven into the Archipelago's social fabric. Half-elves have always occupied the space between communities in the Isles — not quite human, not quite elf, and therefore at ease moving between both. This has made them natural mediators, faction representatives, and travelers; a higher proportion of half-elves than any other race are members of the Wayfinders, the Messengers, or the Concordat. Half-elves in the Isles tend toward the practical and the relational — they have grown up learning how to be at home in a room that was designed for someone else.

#### Halflings

Common, and found most densely in the Isles' farming communities, coastal fishing villages, and the Provisioners' networks. Halflings have a cultural affinity for the ordinary work of living — growing things, feeding people, maintaining the infrastructure that makes community possible — and in the Archipelago this is considered quietly essential rather than mundane. They are not absent from the more dramatic factions, but they form the backbone of the civic and cultural organizations that hold the Isles together practically: Provisioners, Messengers, Healers' Circle. A halfling character is almost always known by reputation in their home community before they are known by their skills.

#### Gnomes

Common, with a particular concentration in arcane and scholarly factions. Gnomish intellectual energy found a natural home in the Archipelago's tradition of preserving and studying what the Unity suppressed; gnomes are heavily represented in the Eightfold Path, the Arcane Practitioners Association, the Pentad, and the Honored Forge. Their characteristic enthusiasm for understanding how things work — arcane, mechanical, historical, social — makes them indispensable in organizations that run on knowledge. Gnome communities in the Isles tend to be noisy, opinionated, and slightly chaotic in the way of places where everyone has several simultaneous projects and strong views about all of them.

#### Elves

Uncommon. The Isles' elven communities have maintained a degree of cultural cohesion that most other races in the Archipelago have not, and this has produced two distinct expressions of elven life — less a racial division than two different answers to the question of what it means to carry an old culture into new conditions.

**High Elves** are the product of elven families that settled in the Archipelago's townships — in Dremma, in Sanctuary, in the smaller communities that have grown up around particular factions. They are scholars, artisans, and practitioners, deeply integrated into the faction system and often among the most learned members of the communities they belong to. Their continuity with elven arcane and cultural traditions is sustained through practice rather than isolation.

**Wild Elves** are the product of elven families that chose the outer islands — the wilder reaches of the Archipelago, the unclaimed stretches between settled communities. They have developed a different kind of continuity: their relationship to the spirit world, maintained through proximity and attention rather than formal practice, is among the most direct of any population in the Isles. Wild elf characters from these communities carry knowledge of the Archipelago's less-visited places that no amount of study can replicate.

Both subraces use the standard Elf statistics. The distinction is cultural and carries no additional mechanical difference.

#### Dwarves

Uncommon. The Archipelago's dwarven communities are among the most internally cohesive in the Isles — not insular exactly, but oriented around the preservation of craft traditions and ancestral memory with a focus that most other races find admirable at a distance and occasionally overwhelming up close. Dwarves in the Isles are disproportionately represented in the Honored Forge, where their craft mastery is the point; the Founders' Keep, where their instinct for genealogical and ancestral memory is well used; and the Twilight Order, where the dwarven relationship to the dead is treated with particular seriousness. A dwarf character carries the weight of a tradition that survived the crossing intact — which is something, in the Archipelago, to be proud of.

**Hill Dwarves** are more common in the Isles' settled communities and faction organizations. **Mountain Dwarves** are somewhat rarer, their traditions more closely associated with the Sacred Lands and the mountains of that continent; a mountain dwarf character in the Isles often has family connections or ancestral ties to the eastern continent.

#### Rare Races

The following races exist in the Archipelago in small numbers. Their rarity does not make them unwelcome — the Isles' tradition is to receive anyone who arrives — but it means their cultural context is less established, their communities more dispersed, and their experience of the Archipelago more individual.

**Half-Orcs.** Found primarily among the Returned and among the families of those who made the crossing in unusual circumstances. Half-orcs in the Isles carry the particular quality of those for whom a welcoming community is not an abstraction: they have, in many cases, direct experience of what it looks like when it is absent.

**Tieflings.** Rare and often independent. Tiefling communities in the Isles are small and informal, their members more likely to maintain individual affiliations with a faction than to form a distinct cultural bloc. The Isles' suppressed-religion traditions — preserved by the Godcallers and others — include several faiths with particular significance to tiefling ancestry; a tiefling character with an interest in that heritage will find more here than they might expect.

**Aasimar.** Very rare, and often connected to the Sacred Lands. The Guardians of the Hidden Vale — whose sanctification ritual takes place at seven fey sites in the east — count several aasimar among their senior members. Whether this reflects coincidence or a deeper connection between aasimar nature and the fey spirit bonds the Guardians forge is not known, or at least not openly spoken of.

**Genasi.** Rare, and varied. The Isles' unusual density of spirit presence means elemental lineages surface here at slightly higher rates than they might elsewhere. Genasi characters in the Isles tend to be drawn toward the factions whose practices most closely align with their elemental nature: fire genasi to the Honored Forge or the Path of Virtue, water genasi to the Tidecallers or Seawatch, air genasi to the Wayfinders, earth genasi to the Lodge of Perfection or the Greenwardens.

### Classes

What follows describes how each class finds expression in the Perfect Isles — what practitioners do, what communities they come from, and what their role in Archipelago life looks like. These descriptions apply to all characters of that class regardless of subclass.

#### Barbarian

The Archipelago has no shortage of people who fight with a quality of intensity that goes beyond technique — people whose practice is rooted in something older and less formal than the schools of the Lodge of Perfection or the disciplined training of the Wayfinders. Barbarians in the Isles come most often from the communities that formed around the Archipelago's wilder islands: the Wild Hunt, the Wild Bunch, and  traditions within the Greensinger and Hidden Vale communities where connection to the spirit world can manifest as something closer to fury than contemplation.

A barbarian character in the Isles typically carries a practice that was not learned from a book or in a school — it was found, or inherited, or emerged in circumstances that made more measured responses impossible. They are not common in the Isles' faction system, but they are present, and the factions that value direct action over deliberation — the Avengers, the Returned — find them useful.

#### Bard

The Troubadours are the obvious home for a bard in the Archipelago, and the most direct one: the faction exists to do what bards do, which is carry culture across distances that would otherwise sever it. But bards in the Isles are not confined to the Troubadours. They appear in the Pentad's oral history work, in the Godcallers' practice of chanting suppressed divine names, in the community memory-keeping that every faction maintains in its own way. In the Isles, the power of a story told in the right room to the right people is understood practically, not theoretically — it is how culture has survived six generations of exile.

A bard character in the Isles is someone who understands that the most durable things are not the ones written down but the ones repeated, carried, and given away. They may be a performer with a traveling company, a community historian whose oral archive stretches back to the founding, or a factional representative whose skill at managing a room is their primary contribution to the work. What they share is the knowledge that in the Archipelago, how something is told matters as much as whether it is told at all.

#### Cleric

Divine magic presents a particular complexity in the Isles. The Unity's purge of religious life destroyed or suppressed the deities whose worship would traditionally support a cleric's practice; the Archipelago arrived at its founding with a fractured spiritual inheritance and has been working with the pieces ever since. Clerics in the Isles typically come from one of three backgrounds: the Godcallers, who have spent six generations preserving the names and rites of suppressed deities and whose practice carries real divine resonance; the Oracle's community at Sanctuary, which maintains its own relationship to divine power through channels it does not fully explain; or the traditions of the Sacred Lands, where divine worship was never interrupted and the Old Gods are present and accessible.

What a cleric's practice looks like in the Isles depends on what tradition they come from. A Godcaller-trained cleric is steeped in a catalogued knowledge of loss and preservation — they know the deities whose worship they sustain by name and story and rite, and what they draw on is real, if not always fully understood. A Sacred Lands-connected cleric carries a relationship to the spirit world that is more direct and more ancient. Either way, a cleric character in the Archipelago is someone whose power comes from a connection that the Unity spent centuries trying to sever — and that has survived.

#### Druid

The Archipelago's unusual density of spirit presence has made it a natural home for druidic practice, and several factions have developed traditions that align closely with the druid's work. The Greensingers are the most purely attuned — their spirit singing tradition is druidic in everything but name, built from the ground up in these specific islands with these specific spirits. The Wild Hunt practices a more formal druidic tradition centered on shapechanging and the hunt cycle. The Guardians of the Hidden Vale combine druidic attunement with fey-spirit bonds rooted in the Sacred Lands. The Blue Star Adepts blend druidic and arcane practice in ways that produce their particular combination of healing and astronomical knowledge.

A druid character in the Isles is rarely without a community — the druidic traditions here are faction-embedded, shaped by generations of practitioners working in a specific context. They carry a relationship to the Archipelago's living world that has accumulated rather than been acquired: knowledge of particular places, particular spirits, particular ways the land expresses what it needs. The change in the spirit world that the Isles has been feeling lately registers for druids before it registers for most others.

#### Fighter

The Lodge of Perfection is the primary institution of martial training in the Isles, and most fighters in the Archipelago have passed through it at some point — as full students, or as participants in one of the Lodge's open training programs. The Lodge's principle — that weapon use can be elevated to an art form — produces fighters with a characteristic quality: they take their craft seriously as a craft, not merely as a means. The Path of Virtue produces fighters of a different kind: deeply ethical, organized around seven cardinal virtues, the most morally earnest people you are likely to meet in an argument and among the most capable in a fight.

A fighter character in the Isles is someone who has made a deliberate practice of capability. They may have come to it through the Lodge, through one of the martial factions, through necessity in the Wayfinders' work, or through a life on the outer islands where personal capability was the only kind available. What distinguishes them is not raw strength — the Archipelago has plenty of that — but the decision to develop it deliberately and the discipline that follows.

#### Monk

The Isles' contemplative martial traditions are among the most internally diverse in the Archipelago. The Order of the Even Hand follows a path of extreme physical discipline aimed entirely inward — no spirit philosophy, no doctrine about the world, only the perfection of the self through the expansion of its physical limits. The Order of the Five Mountains takes the opposite approach: their practices seek understanding of the physical world and the spirits within it, enlightenment as the destination of a philosophical journey rather than an athletic one.

A monk character in the Isles carries a quality of depth that is not always visible on the surface. They have committed to something that requires sustained practice rather than talent — and the practice has changed them. Whether they come from the Even Hand's inward focus or the Five Mountains' outward one, they are people for whom the discipline itself is the point, not the outcomes it produces, which is a perspective the rest of the Archipelago finds quietly remarkable and occasionally baffling.

#### Paladin

Paladins present an interesting question in the Isles. The divine covenants that traditionally structure a paladin's oath are complicated by the Archipelago's fractured relationship to formal divinity: the deities whose names the Godcallers preserve are real but largely inaccessible; the Old Gods of the Sacred Lands are present and powerful but not the paladin tradition's native patrons; the belief-spirits of the Isles are real but not divine in the traditional sense.

Paladins in the Archipelago resolve this differently depending on their background. Some come from the Godcaller tradition — their oaths sworn to deities whose worship has been preserved in fragment and memory, drawing on a divine connection that is real but attenuated by what the Unity severed. Some come from contact with the Sacred Lands, where Old Gods are present enough to enter into genuine covenants. Some swear oaths to ideals rather than entities — Oath of Devotion paladins are not uncommon among the Path of Virtue's practitioners or the Wayfinders' most committed members. What all of them share is the quality of conviction that the Isles tends to produce in people who have thought carefully about what they are willing to stand for and why.

#### Ranger

The Wayfinders are the obvious home for a ranger in the Archipelago, and the match is close: their work demands navigation across the open sea and the Barrier's edge, the ability to operate in unfamiliar territory, and the capacity to bring people safely through situations that do not allow extended planning. Rangers from the Wayfinders carry that operational quality — they are people who have been out and come back and know what going out actually involves.

Rangers in the Isles are not limited to the Wayfinders, however. The Archipelago's outer islands produce natural navigators and trackers for whom skill emerged from living in places that required it. The Greenwardens field rangers who know the unclaimed islands in detail no map captures. A ranger character in the Isles is typically someone for whom the territory — sea, island, forest, barrier-edge — is not an obstacle but a medium they have learned to read.

#### Rogue

The Isles has several distinct contexts for roguish skills, and they produce different kinds of practitioners. The Keepers of the Veil's covert network — the intelligence operation running behind their vow of public silence — employs people skilled in observation, information-gathering, and movement without notice. The Messengers, whose neutrality and discretion make them effective anywhere, produce a related kind: the person who can be anywhere without being remembered. The Shadowed Disciples, to the extent they exist, represent a third kind entirely: specialists in the work that no faction officially does.

A rogue character in the Isles is someone who has developed capabilities that the Archipelago finds valuable but rarely discusses openly. They may have come to those capabilities through institutional training or through a life that required them without providing instruction. What they share is the understanding that the work they do happens in the space between what people notice and what they don't, which in a community built around the art of remaining unnoticed is, in some ways, the most natural thing in the world.

#### Sorcerer

The Truebloods are the Archipelago's primary institution of sorcerous practice, organized around the principle that the bloodlines through which raw magic passes are worth preserving and developing rather than treating as incidental. A Trueblood character carries a specific kind of pride — they are the thing the Unity spent centuries trying to erase, and they know it, and they have made the preservation of that inheritance a reason for pride rather than fear. The Discordians take a different approach: Wild Magic sorcerers who have made peace with their unpredictability and turned it into art. Their characteristic product, an irregular traveling festival of whimsy and living art, is spectacular, uncontrolled, and, by consistent testimony, worth experiencing.

A sorcerer character in the Isles is someone in whose body magic lives, which is a different thing from someone who studies or bargains for it. The Archipelago's relationship to innate magic is complicated by the fact that the Unity's suppression is the reason most of their ancestors are here — sorcerous heritage was one of the first things marked for removal in the west. A sorcerer in the Isles carries that history personally.

#### Warlock

Pact magic is practiced across several factions in the Archipelago, each with a different understanding of what is being bargained with. The Order of the Seven Stars makes pacts with entities it believes represent a higher spiritual reality; its practitioners are sincere, their patrons uncaring extraplanar beings, and the gap between these two facts is not something the Order speaks about openly to non-members. The Avengers make pacts with lower planar entities in explicit preparation for war — they are under no illusions about the nature of their patrons and have decided the arrangement is worth it. The older warlock traditions in the Isles — those that predate the founding and arrived with the original exiles — involve pacts with the spirit world itself: named spirits, entities that do not fit cleanly into any category, and powers that the Isles is only beginning to understand more fully. This practice is not formalized in any sense in the Isles today. 

A warlock character in the Isles carries a relationship with something larger than themselves that they did not enter into casually. The question of what the patron wants, whether the arrangement is sustainable, and what it means for who the warlock is becoming is not abstract in this setting — the spirit world is present and attentive, and pacts here are known to have consequences that play out over time.

#### Wizard

The Archipelago's wizardly traditions are among the most organized of any in the Isles' institutional life. The Eightfold Path treats the eight schools of magic as separate esoteric disciplines — each a path toward understanding, each requiring mastery before the next can be properly approached — and produces the deepest arcane scholars in the Archipelago. The Arcane Practitioners Association is of a more practical bent: a guild of independent wizards bound by the goal of improving the reputation of arcane practice through public works - they are approachable and broadly connected in ways opposite of the Eightfold Path. The Blue Star Adepts blend wizardly study with druidic practice in ways that most arcane scholars consider theoretically suspect and the Adepts consider foundational.

A wizard character in the Isles is someone who chose to learn magic rather than inherit it or bargain for it, which is its own statement in a community where all three are available. The act of choosing formalized arcane study — with its discipline, its theory, its accumulated knowledge — is not apolitical in the Archipelago. Magic here is one of the things the Unity tried to erase and failed to erase, and the wizards who practice it openly are making a point that does not always require words.

#### Unity Exile

*You came from the Unity — perhaps not entirely by choice. ### Character Hooks

The following questions are offered to help locate your character in the Isles at the start of the adventure. They carry no mechanical weight; they are starting points for thinking about who you are, what you believe, and where the events of the adventure will find you.

**Where do you come from?** Were you born in the Archipelago, or did you arrive here? If you arrived — how long ago, and what brought you? If you were born here — have you ever been past the Barrier? Do you want to be?

**What is your faction to you?** Do you belong to one of the Archipelago's thirty-three factions? If so, is it a community you were born into or one you chose? What does membership mean to you in practice — obligation, identity, work you believe in, or something else? If you have no faction, what has kept you outside the system?

**Have you ever met a named spirit?** Most people in the Isles have encountered the small ones — the presences near water and old paths. Not everyone has had contact with a named spirit. Have you? If so, what was the encounter, and did it change how you understand the Isles? If not — is that something you want, or something you have avoided?

**What do you make of the Oracle?** She has spoken three times since the last quarterly meeting at Dremma. Her words are circulating through the factions now, interpreted and reinterpreted. Have you heard what she said? What is your reaction — concern, skepticism, curiosity? Has anyone in your life changed their behavior because of what she said?

**Something is different.** Practitioners speak of the spirits behaving strangely. The Seawatch have reported anomalies in the Barrier. The season has an unusual quality to it, a sense that something is being waited for. Have you noticed? What first made you pay attention? Or are you one of the people who has been watching and waiting and not quite saying so out loud?

**What are you willing to lose?** The Isles was built by people who had already lost everything once. Many of them chose to lose it rather than become something they couldn't live with. What would you be willing to sacrifice? What is the thing you would not give up, even for something worth having?

## What Your Character Knows

### Common Knowledge

The following is understood by any resident of the Perfect Isles, regardless of faction or background. It is the baseline — what your character knows before the adventure begins.

- The Perfect Isles are hidden from the outside world by the Barrier — a piece of magic built by the original exiles and maintained by the Seawatch since the founding. Most people know it exists, that it requires upkeep, and that anything that might make the Isles visible to outside observers is treated as a collective threat.
- The Unity, to the west, outlawed the practice of magic and organized divine worship centuries ago. That suppression is the reason the Archipelago was built: the founders were among those removed.
- Spirits are real. Most people in the Isles have encountered one. The small ones are present near water, at old forest edges, along paths well-traveled enough to have accumulated something's attention. Certain factions have developed traditions for approaching the larger, named ones, and those traditions work.
- The Oracle exists and speaks truth — or something close enough to truth that dismissing her entirely has come to seem like a posture rather than a conclusion. Her words are recorded and distributed to the factions. Right now, she is speaking more than usual.
- The Sacred Lands lie to the east. People there still have a working relationship with the spirit world. The Unity has been expanding into the eastern continent in recent years. The direction of movement is not encouraging.
- The Archipelago's thirty-three factions hold the Isles between them. The Council, which meets only when called by the Oracle or by any three factions acting together, is the closest thing to a government the Isles has. Agreements made there are binding only to the degree the parties feel accountable — which is variable. The Isles works, after a fashion.
- The founders used their combined magical power to build the Barrier. What it was in origin, what it cost to use, and what sustains it are questions the Isles has spent six generations arguing about without reaching conclusions that all factions accept.
- Something has shifted in the past year. The spirits have been restless. The barrier has behaved in ways the practitioners who monitor it cannot fully account for. The Oracle has spoken three times since the last quarterly meeting. No one agrees on what any of it means, but people have begun paying attention in a way they were not before.
