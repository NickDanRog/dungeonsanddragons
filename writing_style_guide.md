# Campaign Writing Style Guide

This guide documents the voice, structure, and conventions to follow when writing DM Guides and Player's Guides for this project. It is derived from analysis of independently authored source materials: `artaxarta.md` and `Talumh_1.txt`.

---

## Voice and Register

Write from a position of complete knowledge. The text should read as though produced by someone who understands the subject thoroughly and is presenting it with authority.

The default register is **encyclopedic and formal** — comparable to a well-written historical reference work. The prose is confident without being dramatic, and precise without being dry. There is genuine interest in the subject; this is not bureaucratic writing.

Do not hedge on world facts. "The Artaxartoi were..." not "The Artaxartoi were perhaps..." or "In many ways, the Artaxartoi could be considered..." Hedging language is reserved for genuine in-world historical uncertainty ("Most scholars believe...", "Little is known of...") and should be deployed selectively.

When writing mythic history, cosmological origin, or deep legend, shift to a more **epic register**: shorter sentences, declarative statements, temporal sweep conveyed through accumulation rather than length. This register should feel distinct from the encyclopedic voice and signals to the reader that the material operates at a different scale. See the Ages of Talumh preface as the model.

---

## Sentence Structure

Complex, multi-clause sentences are the norm. The usual pattern is a main clause followed by one or more subordinate clauses that accumulate qualification and detail. Sentences build on each other within paragraphs.

Use short sentences intentionally, not habitually. A short sentence works as a pivot ("Not all were content, however."), a statement of consequence ("Within a few years the situation began to stabilize."), or a dramatic close to an extended passage. Overuse flattens the rhythm and reduces impact.

Preferred connective tissue: *however*, *though*, *while*, *as*, *though*, *thus*, *nonetheless*. These hold complex relationships inside a single sentence without resorting to semicolons, which tend to make prose feel listy. Do not overuse dashes.

Inverted syntax is acceptable for emphasis: "Two of their number they left as guardians." "Heedless of the danger their actions posed, these factions sought to settle their differences." Use it selectively — it signals elevation of subject matter.

Avoid:
- Sentence fragments (unless in mechanical/game text)
- Rhetorical questions in prose sections
- Sentences that begin with "It is important to note that..."
- Throat-clearing constructions ("As previously mentioned...")

---

## Paragraphs

Each paragraph develops one controlling idea, fully. A typical paragraph runs four to eight sentences. The opening sentence establishes the topic; subsequent sentences build on it with specifics, examples, context, or contrast; the closing sentence often pivots, looks forward, or closes with a consequence.

Do not pad. If an idea is sufficiently covered, move on. Do not add a sentence to smooth a transition if the transition does not need smoothing.

One-sentence paragraphs should be rare in prose sections. When they appear, they should land with weight — the reader should feel that they earned their own paragraph.

---

## Vocabulary

Use elevated but not archaic language. The vocabulary should feel comfortably above casual speech without tipping into affectation. A word like *countenance* or *surmount* is at home; a word like *verily* is not.

Use technical and in-world terminology naturally, without over-explanation on first use. Trust that context will carry it, or introduce it cleanly ("...a formation called a *kulak*, or company..."). Do not write "what they called a kulak" — either use it directly or gloss it once.

**Strong nouns and verbs over adjectives and adverbs.** If the facts make something clear, do not add an adjective to confirm it. "He amassed a large army" does not need "an impressively large army." Adverbs should be nearly absent; if a verb requires an adverb to work, consider replacing the verb.

Reserve the word *incredible* and its synonyms for situations that genuinely exceed expectation — and use them sparingly enough that they still have force when they appear.

---

## Tone

The prose does not editorialize or emote on behalf of the reader. Violence, tragedy, and achievement are treated with the same composure. Let the facts carry the weight.

Understated wit is appropriate and welcome. Dry observation delivered in a matter-of-fact register — "Sadly, the man who led the raid was killed in the fighting, but the Emperor, true to his word, made certain the money he promised was passed on to his descendants" — is characteristic of the voice and should be preserved when it arises naturally.

Do not editorialize with qualifiers like *terrifying*, *shocking*, *remarkable* when the underlying description is doing its job. Do not tell the reader how to feel.

---

## Structure and Formatting

**Headers** organize major sections and distinct sub-topics. Do not over-subdivide — a section that can be covered in two or three paragraphs does not need its own sub-headers. Headers should be descriptive, not clever.

**Prose over bullets** in all world, history, culture, and NPC content. Bullet points flatten information, strip rhythm, and signal a reference list rather than a document worth reading. The only appropriate uses for bullet points are:
- Mechanical game content (stats, traits, spell lists)
- Explicit reference tables that will be consulted repeatedly during play
- Quick-reference summaries in DM Guides (encounter conditions, DC tables)

Do not use bullets as a shortcut to avoid writing a paragraph.

Named figures are introduced with full context on first appearance, then referenced by shortened name thereafter. Specific numbers are integrated naturally into prose: not "there were many soldiers" but "the force numbered just over two hundred, organized into four cohorts."

## DM Guide Conventions

**Scene descriptions** are written in present tense, active voice, with sensory grounding. Describe what the players encounter as it is happening, not as a summary of what has been prepared.

**NPC descriptions** lead with behavior and disposition, not appearance. A physical detail should earn its place by telling the reader something useful. How an NPC moves, speaks, and responds under pressure is more valuable than their eye color.

**Encounter framing** states stakes before mechanics. The reader should understand what is at risk before they reach the stat block or DC.

**Avoid meta-language.** Write "Orestes does not offer this information freely" rather than "this encounter is designed to create tension." Write "if the players press him" rather than "players who engage with this scene may discover." Address the DM directly and practically.

---

## Player's Guide Conventions

**World and history sections** follow the encyclopedic register: third person, authoritative, descriptive. These sections read like the artaxarta.md reference material.

**Character creation guidance** addresses the player directly in second person where appropriate: "Every character answers six hooks during creation." Keep this clean and direct — do not over-explain mechanical choices or assume ignorance.

**Hooks and prompts** use second person and present tense to place the character in the world: "You have served in the Royal Guard for six years. You know the man whose signature is on your orders." Establish situation, not backstory.

**Mechanical sections** (racial traits, class features, background features) follow standard 5e formatting conventions: short, clear, explicit. Use direct language with no hedging. If a feature does something, say what it does.

---

## What to Avoid

- Bullet points in prose sections
- Adjective inflation ("an extraordinary, legendary, and celebrated order...")
- Hedging on world facts
- Meta-language about player experience or encounter design
- Exclamation points
- Passive voice as a default construction
- Adverbs modifying weak verbs
- Throat-clearing transitions ("As we have seen...", "It bears mentioning...")
- Summarizing what was just said
- Starting consecutive sentences with the same subject
