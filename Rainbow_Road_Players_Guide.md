# RAINBOW ROAD
## Player's Guide

*A Pathfinder 1st Edition adventure for characters of levels 1–20*
*Mythic Tiers 1–10 (Mythic Adventures)*

---

*This document reflects what a knowledgeable resident of Nexus knows or could discover. The Rainbow Road keeps its own counsel.*

# SECTION I: THE REALM OF NEXUS

## What Nexus Is

Nexus is a small continent suspended in the featureless mists of the Ethereal Plane — the center of the Nine Worlds system and the anchor point of the Rainbow Road. It was built by the celestial beings of Heaven as a hub through which all traffic between the worlds would pass. Five centuries ago, the Road went dark. The outer worlds fell into isolation, and Nexus was left alone. The landscape is temperate and varied in the manner of a world shaped by practical necessity rather than natural process — forests, plains, rivers, and farmland in proportions sufficient to sustain a human-scale population. There is nothing obviously otherworldly about standing in its fields. The strangeness comes from knowing what it was built for, and what it has been without.

## The City of Nexus

The great city occupies the continent's center. Its bones were laid by celestial architects — broad avenues radiating outward from the center, public spaces sized for a volume of traffic that has not existed in five centuries, infrastructure that assumes constant movement between worlds — and five hundred years of human habitation have grown over and around those bones in the organic, unplanned fashion of a population that was simply trying to survive. The result is a city of considerable contrast: ancient celestial stonework alongside wartime construction, fields of wildflowers growing between the foundations of districts razed in the demon war and never rebuilt, magitek fabrication halls producing arms and tools with the same reliability they maintained the day Casomir Zukra restored them.

Two full districts were demolished after the war and converted to farmland, an arrangement that has proven more than sufficient to feed the current population. The foundries of the western industrial quarter supply the city's defense, maintained by a small corps of trained artificers and producing everything from personal arms to the magitek devices on which daily life depends. An educated citizen of Nexus understands magitek roughly the way a traveler understands a good road — as a fact of the environment, useful and largely taken for granted, its underlying principles of interest mainly to those who build it. The militia, well equipped by foundry output and drilled from childhood, handles the demon raids that occur with rough predictability throughout the year. The raids are tactical in character: multiple simultaneous strikes against different districts, designed to stretch the defense thin. They succeed occasionally, and people die. The city absorbs these losses with the composure of a population that has been managing the same problem for five hundred years.

The full moon is a different matter. When the moon is full, the people of Nexus barricade their doors and do not emerge until morning. Beast Blavin hunts on these nights. The creature is massive and violent, resistant to every weapon that has been turned against it, and those who have organized hunting parties over the centuries have not returned. Casomir's court has officially prohibited such attempts. The barricades hold, most of the time, and the city cleans up after each visitation with the grim efficiency of long practice. Blavin also appears at other times, without the predictability of the lunar cycle, and these appearances are generally regarded as worse — the full moon at least gives the city time to prepare.

## The Gate Towns

Eight small settlements ring the continent's perimeter, each built around the terminus of one of the Rainbow Road's pathways to the outer worlds. The pathways themselves have been dark for five centuries, but the towns have not all shared the same fate. Some retain small populations — stubborn communities that refused to abandon their homes when the Road closed and have persisted in that refusal ever since. Others stand entirely empty, their buildings intact but unoccupied, their streets belonging to the wind. Whether a given gate town is inhabited or not, all share one peculiarity: the demons that regularly strike the city of Nexus leave the gate towns alone. No one has been able to explain this. The demons do not appear to avoid the towns out of weakness or inability; they simply do not go there. Scholars have proposed explanations ranging from residual celestial warding to some property of the gate structures themselves. None of the proposals has proven satisfactory.

The gate towns are not easily reached from the city. The journey on foot takes five to six days in good conditions, a distance that makes each town effectively its own world — isolated, self-reliant, and accustomed to receiving little attention from Casomir's court beyond the occasional maintenance expedition. A perfectly circular road of rainbow-colored tiles connects the towns to one another along the continent's perimeter, and straight spokes of the same material extend inward from each town toward the city. The tiles do not wear; centuries of use and neglect have left them in the same condition as the day they were laid.

## The Great Ancillary

At the very center of the city — older than the city itself, older perhaps than anything else on the continent — stands the Great Ancillary. It is a sealed temple of considerable size, its exterior architecture unlike anything built in the five centuries since the apocalypse, its entrance secured and guarded by Casomir's court. Civilians are not permitted inside. The court does not explain why. Most citizens understand, in the way that one understands a fact that has always been true and is unlikely to change, that the Ancillary exists, that it is important, and that it is not for them. Scholars who have studied its exterior have noted that the structure was clearly not designed for ordinary use — its proportions and arrangements suggest purpose rather than residence, though what that purpose might be has generated more speculation than consensus.

## The Sky Above

Heaven is visible to the naked eye above Nexus — not as a star, but as the world's sun, moving in its long circuit through the sky on a path that has remained unchanged for as long as records exist. The moon and stars appear at night, as they do on any world, though scholars of the celestial system have long noted certain irregularities in their movements that resist conventional astronomical explanation. Whether the celestials who inhabit Heaven are aware of the state of the worlds below is a question that has occupied the Host of Heaven faith for several centuries without producing a definitive answer.

The faith itself is real. The Host of Heaven — three races of celestial beings who built the Nine Worlds — genuinely exists, and the divine power they grant to their followers is genuine. Observance has nonetheless declined sharply in the present era. Five centuries of silence from a Heaven that has not answered a direct petition in living memory has a particular effect on institutional religion, and the Host of Heaven on Nexus today is sustained mainly by the oldest families and a small number of clerics who maintain its texts and rites out of conviction rather than cultural momentum. The celestials are real. They simply have not spoken. What one does with that fact is a question each citizen of Nexus settles for themselves.

# SECTION II: THE NINE WORLDS

## What the Nine Worlds Are

The Nine Worlds exist as interlinked pocket dimensions — each a self-contained domain roughly the size of a small continent, suspended within the Ethereal Plane and possessed of its own distinct terrain, climate, and character. They were built by the celestial beings of Heaven as a unified system, connected to one another and to the hub world Nexus by the interplanar pathways of the Rainbow Road. Each world developed its own race, culture, and history over the centuries of the system's operation. The celestial builders did not construct nine variations on a theme but nine genuinely distinct places, unified by the Road and by the shared light of Heaven passing overhead. No natural mechanism could have produced anything like them; every irregularity in their geography, every impossibility in their climate, is evidence of deliberate design.

## The Rainbow Road

In the centuries of its operation, the Rainbow Road was the connective tissue of the Nine Worlds — an interplanar network of pathways along which a common traveler could move freely between worlds, stepping through a gate terminus in one realm's gate town and emerging on another within moments. The trade and cultural exchange this enabled shaped every civilization in the Nine Worlds. Arlune's sandship captains carried cargo from Vatra's forges to Diaspola's markets. Candela's scholars corresponded with archivists on every other world. Nocturne's performers filled the great halls of Illium. The Road did not make the Nine Worlds one world, but it made each world richer for the connection, and the gate towns that served as its termini were among the most cosmopolitan places in existence.

The Road also served a purpose beyond commerce. From the earliest days of the Nine Worlds, the celestial system was designed to identify worthy mortal champions and imbue them with mythic power — a strength beyond the ordinary measure of heroism. Those chosen by the Host of Heaven would seek out the Nexian Oracle, receive confirmation of their calling, and then walk the Rainbow Road in sequence: visiting each of the outer worlds, undertaking trials of character and valor, and drawing on the places of power the celestials had seeded throughout the system. Those who completed the journey joined the Host of Heaven. For several centuries this process produced the great heroes of recorded legend, and their stories formed the bedrock of the Nine Worlds' understanding of what it meant to be worthy of Heaven's notice.

Throughout this same period, the people of the Nine Worlds contended with the influence of demons. Demonic corruption had troubled mortal civilization since the earliest generations — sponsoring cults, fomenting wars, degrading individual character through patient accumulation of vice. Many of the heroes who walked the Road fought against this influence directly. Over time, however, the corruption deepened. The channels through which it reached mortal minds widened, and by the century before the apocalypse scholars across the Nine Worlds were recording a marked and alarming increase in the scale and organization of demonic activity.

Five hundred years ago, a cohort of ten heroes walked the Road together — the greatest the Nine Worlds had produced in living memory — with the stated purpose of ending the demon threat permanently. They completed the mythic journey, received the full measure of power the celestial system could grant, and then constructed an artifact designed to open a portal to the Underworld and sever its connection to the Nine Worlds at the root. The portal opened. The severing did not follow. What came through instead was the full force of the Underworld, uncontained, in numbers that had never been seen in the Nine Worlds.

What followed is called the Apocalypse. The Nine Worlds were devastated. The demons attacked every realm they could reach, but their primary target was Heaven itself, and the battle fought there was the bloodiest engagement of the entire war. The celestial sphere sustained damage so severe that it has never fully recovered. The bulk of the demon forces were destroyed in that assault. The ten heroes led the surviving defenders in the fighting below, and together they held. When the war ended, the surviving heroes returned to the outer worlds to help their people rebuild.

The Rainbow Road was closed in the chaotic aftermath. The gate towns were evacuated as the heroes departed, the pathways were shut as a precaution against any further transit through systems that were no longer belived to be fully safe, and the work of reopening them fell to no one in particular. It was never done. The worlds fell into isolation, and five centuries of silence followed.

## The Outer Worlds

*The following descriptions are drawn from* A Survey of the Nine Worlds, *a geographic compendium compiled by the Nexian Society of Cartographers in the century before the apocalypse. They describe each world as it existed at the time of writing. No account of current conditions can be independently verified.*

**Diaspola —** Diaspola was a world of warm seas and low, green land, its territory divided roughly between ocean and the pastoral interior that the Halfling population called home. The Halflings of Diaspola were known throughout the Nine Worlds for their energy and commercial aptitude, and their network of river towns and market settlements had long served as a point of contact between traders from across the Road. At the heart of the land territory stood the Mother of Rivers, a great spring whose waters fed every river on the world and which the Halflings regarded as sacred. Of all the Nine Worlds, Diaspola was the most immediately hospitable to the traveler.

**Tanis —** Tanis existed as a vast spherical cavern open to the sky at its summit — a great orb of earth with the uppermost portion removed, exposing the interior to a few hours of direct sunlight each day. In the hours of darkness, the cavern floor was lit by bioluminescent flora of extraordinary variety, giving the forested interior a quality that travelers from other worlds consistently described as unlike anything encountered elsewhere. The Dwarven population built its cities partially underground while the Forest Gnome communities maintained a druidic tradition of unusual sophistication — a practice of working in harmony with the primal magic of the earth that had no equal in the Nine Worlds. The great ziggurat at the cavern's center was the seat of this tradition, and pilgrims traveled to it from every Gnomish settlement.

**Auzra —** Auzra was a world of open sky, its landmass distributed across dozens of floating islands arranged in three broad tiers. The Hawkmen, the world's native people, inhabited the smaller islands and the heights, their natural flight making them at home in the vertical landscape that defined Auzran life. The Grey Elves arrived on Auzra later in recorded history and settled the larger islands of the middle tier. They had developed in partnership with the Hawkmen a magitek tradition of considerable sophistication — advances in aerial transportation, arcane research, and scholarly instrument-making whose applications were felt across the entire Road. The world's universities were among the most respected in the Nine Worlds.

**Arlune —** Arlune was an endless desert, its shifting dunes illuminated by moonlight through the perpetual twilight that characterized the world's climate. Travel across the Twilight Sea, as the desert was known, was accomplished by sandship — sealed magitek vessels built to traverse dunes as a ship traverses open water. The population of Arlune was the most diverse in the Nine Worlds, its trade-centered culture having attracted settlers from every other world over the centuries, and its market towns were polyglot places where every language of the Road was spoken and nearly any commodity could be found.

**Vatra —** Vatra was a world of volcanic ranges and magitek forges, its circular territory organized in three concentric rings: an outer band of volcanic peaks whose eruptions fed channels of lava to the middle ring's forge-cities, the forge-cities themselves — enormous complexes of workshops and fabrication halls that produced the majority of the magitek equipment in use across the Nine Worlds — and an inner ring of ancient rainforest, protected from volcanic fumes by a great magitek dome and home to resort communities whose comfort stood in deliberate contrast to the industrial character of the world beyond their walls. The Tiefling population, whose natural resistance to heat and toxins made them ideally suited to their environment, had developed the manufacturing processes on which the whole Road depended.

**Illium —** Illium was a floating continent that received more direct sunlight than any other world in the Nine Worlds system — a function of its position within the system, as its scholars had long understood, and not an accident of cosmology. The Half-Elf population had built a chivalric tradition of unusual sincerity around the Church Ascendant, a faith oriented toward the Host of Heaven that treated honor, service, and sacrifice as practical virtues rather than abstract ideals. The knights of Illium were among the most celebrated warriors in the Nine Worlds, their reputation earned through centuries of genuine service rather than the inherited prestige of military institution.

**Nocturne —** Nocturne was a world of interconnected valleys in perpetual twilight — a landscape of gentle hills, still water, and diffuse indirect light that never brightened to full day and never darkened to full night. The Fetchling population, whose race was naturally attuned to conditions of shadow and dim illumination, had turned this environment into something remarkable: Nocturne was the artistic capital of the Nine Worlds. Its music, literature, and visual art — produced with a sensitivity to light, shadow, and the intervals between them that was characteristic of the Fetchling sensibility — were prized across the entire Road, and the great performance halls of Nocturne's principal valley attracted visitors from every other world.

**Candela —** Candela was the smallest and most unusual of the Nine Worlds — a collection of spherical constructs suspended in a void, each glowing with its own internal luminescence in the manner of a nearby star, the whole arrangement suggesting an artificial constellation. The Half-Orc population inhabited these spheres through engineering entirely of their own development, having adapted their architecture and daily life to a world without ground in the conventional sense. Candela was home to the Arcanium, the greatest university in the Nine Worlds, whose archives were said to contain the most comprehensive collection of arcane and historical knowledge in existence. To hold a credential from the Arcanium was, before the apocalypse, the most reliable mark of scholarly distinction the Nine Worlds offered.

## Heaven and the Underworld

Heaven is visible above Nexus as the world's sun — a vast artificial sphere of celestial construction, moving in its long circuit through the sky on a path that has remained unchanged for as long as records exist. It is not a natural star but an inhabited structure, the dwelling place of the three celestial races who built the Nine Worlds. Their collective power, when venerated together as the Host of Heaven, grants divine abilities to followers equal to those of any deity in the wider multiverse. In former times this faith was practiced in some form on every world. Its current state varies, though its underlying reality does not: Heaven is real, the celestials exist, and the divine power they grant is genuine.

Beneath the Nine Worlds, separated from them by layers of planar boundary, lies the Underworld — a prison formed by the celestials in the earliest days of the Nine Worlds' existence to contain a class of beings called Asuras. These entities are known as demons to the people of the Worlds, a name that well reflects their character. The Underworld was not part of the original design; its existence is a consequence of the Nine Worlds' construction, a solution to the problem of infiltration by these entities that presented itself before the mortal populations were fully established. The Asuras cannot leave the Underworld under their own power, though their influence can cross the planar boundary under certain conditions, and the raids that plague Nexus are evidence that some mechanism of transit exists even without the full power of the Apocalypse behind it. The nature of that mechanism is not fully understood by anyone living on Nexus.

# SECTION III: SOCIETY AND CULTURE

Five institutions define life in Nexus — not the only institutions that exist, but the ones any informed resident would know and any new arrival would encounter quickly. They are the court that governs, the faith that persists, the technology that sustains, the towns that wait, and the figure that answers.

## The Court of Nexus

Casomir Zukra has ruled Nexus for five hundred years. He was one of the ten heroes who walked the Rainbow Road before the apocalypse, received the full measure of mythic power the celestial system could grant, and then spent the remainder of the war fighting to hold what little remained. When it ended and his companions departed for their respective worlds, Casomir stayed. The work of rebuilding Nexus fell to him because no one else was present to take it, and five centuries later he has not relinquished it.

His reputation among the people he governs is not difficult to summarize: just, distant, and haunted. The justice is consistent and documented — five hundred years of court records, adjudications, and administrative decisions that historians study as much for their internal consistency as for their legal content. The distance is harder to characterize. Casomir is present in his court, available to his administrators, and visible enough that citizens do not doubt his continued existence. He is not, however, approachable in the way that human rulers occasionally are; there is something in his manner that makes ordinary people acutely aware of the difference between five centuries and a single lifetime. The haunted quality in his eyes is understood rather than discussed. He has outlived everyone he knew. The worlds he set out to save have been silent for as long as anyone living can remember.

The court itself is a functional apparatus. Administrators govern the city's districts, coordinate the militia, maintain the Foundry's operations, and handle the logistics of the gate town maintenance expeditions that go out twice a year. Casomir makes decisions where decisions are required and delegates whereever delegation is possible. The court is not corrupt in any conventional sense — corruption tends to require more active participation than an institution supervised by a five-century-old mythic hero generally receives. It is, however, slow to change, shaped by five hundred years of solving the same problems in the same ways.

## The Host of Heaven

The faith of the Host of Heaven is real in every material sense. Heaven itself is visible above Nexus — the world's sun, an artificial celestial sphere inhabited by the three divine races who built the Nine Worlds — and the divine power the celestials grant to their followers is genuine and measurable. The faith has its texts, its rites, its history, and a small corps of working clerics on Nexus who receive and channel that power as reliably today as they did before the apocalypse. What the faith does not have, and has not had in five centuries, is any direct communication from Heaven. The celestials have not spoken. Prayers are received — the divine power flows — but no word comes back. What one does with that silence is the central theological question of the faith, and the institution has not produced a consensus answer.

The practical consequence is that observance has declined sharply. The oldest families of Nexus maintain the faith as old families often maintain old practices — out of habit, conviction, and a sense that some things should not be allowed to disappear. A small number of clerics are devoted by genuine belief, finding in the celestials' continued power a sufficient basis for continued faith regardless of silence. The broader population understands that the Host of Heaven is real without feeling that this reality demands much of them personally. The faith's temples still stand. Its rites are still performed. It is simply no longer the cultural center it was when the Nine Worlds were open and the Road ran in both directions.

## The Magitek Tradition

Magitek is not an art confined to specialists or a secret held by guilds; here it is infrastructure. The transportation network that moves goods through Nexus runs on magitek. The Foundry that equips the militia runs on magitek. The lights in the administrative quarter, the pumping systems in the river districts, the maintenance mechanisms in the gate town road tiles — all of it runs on magitek. An educated citizen of Nexus understands these systems roughly the way any person understands the practical objects of their daily environment: enough to use them, not necessarily enough to repair them, and with the assumption that someone else will handle the latter.

The practitioners who do understand the underlying principles are trained at the Foundry's attached workshop — not a formal academy but a long-standing apprenticeship tradition that has produced every competent magitek artificer in Nexian history since the apocalypse. The tradition traces its origins to the celestial mechanisms already present in Nexus when Casomir assumed governance, and five centuries of accumulated knowledge have extended it considerably beyond those origins. The court's official position is that magitek is neither magic nor mere craft but something that partakes of both — a characterization that the artificers themselves tend to find accurate and the city's remaining scholars tend to find frustrating in its vagueness.

## The Gate Towns

Eight settlements ring the continent's perimeter, each built at the terminus of one of the Rainbow Road's pathways to the outer worlds. They were built for transit — large enough to handle significant through-traffic, arranged to receive travelers arriving from the Road and prepare those departing for it — and they have served that purpose for five centuries since the apocalypse. The Road has been dark — no one has come through, and no one has gone out.

What they have become in the absence of that purpose varies by town. Some retain small populations — communities that refused to leave when the Road closed and have persisted across generations through farming and whatever trade their isolation allows. Others stand empty, their buildings intact and maintained, their streets belonging to the wind. The court sends maintenance expeditions twice a year; the tiles of the Road and the gate structures themselves require little upkeep, and the expeditions spend most of their time on buildings. Gate town residents, where they exist, are technically citizens of Nexus, though the practical relationship between a city five to six days' journey distant and a community of a few hundred people is limited.

The towns share one peculiarity that has never been satisfactorily explained: demons do not attack them. Raids from the Underworld strike the city of Nexus with rough regularity throughout the year; they have never, in five centuries of recorded history, struck any gate town. The raids against the city are tactically sophisticated, which suggests that the absence from the gate towns reflects deliberate choice rather than physical constraint. Scholars have proposed residual celestial warding, properties inherent to the gate structures, and several other explanations. None has proven satisfactory. The people of the gate towns do not live under barricades or behind walls, something which people of the city find this fact remarkable.

## The Oracle's Sanctum

East of the city, across territory that the demons have made reliably hostile, lives the figure known as the Oracle. She has been there for as long as the oldest court records go back. Those who have reached her — envoys from Casomir's court, the occasional private petitioner who survived the journey — consistently report that her answers are genuine: not vague or evasive in the manner of legend, but precise, accurate, and frequently disconcerting. What those answers have meant in practical terms has varied considerably. The court's relationship with the Oracle is one of the least-documented aspects of Nexian governance.

The journey to the Sanctum takes several days under conditions that virtually guarantee combat. The court does not facilitate private access, but does not prevent it. Those who attempt the journey without preparation do not generally return. Those who prepare adequately and survive report that the Sanctum itself is well-fortified, that the Oracle is fully aware of who has come and why before the first word is spoken, and that she does not offer more than was asked.

# SECTION IV: CHARACTER CREATION

## Races

Nexus is the most diverse settlement in the Nine Worlds. Every race present on the continent at the time of the apocalypse has left descendants here, and five centuries of isolated coexistence have produced a population of extraordinary variety — one in which unusual ancestry is common enough to require no explanation. Any race documented in the Pathfinder system may be found on Nexus. Players may select any core, featured, or uncommon Pathfinder race for their characters.

Two races warrant specific treatment because of their narrative significance to the campaign.

---

### Hawkmen

The Hawkmen are the native people of Auzra — an avian race whose communities occupied the floating island chains of that world's upper tiers, moving between them on wings that serve as naturally for daily travel as for the occasional migration. Their presence on Nexus dates to the pre-apocalypse era of open trade: traders, scholars, and diplomatic envoys were among those present in the city when the Road closed, and their descendants have maintained a distinct community in the upper districts ever since. Five centuries of residence have not made them inconspicuous. A Hawkman is identifiable by wingspan alone; folded, the wings rise a foot above the head and require adjusted doorframes, wider corridors, and more personal space than most Nexian architecture was designed to accommodate.

Their features are severe in the manner common to avian peoples: pronounced cheekbones, large hooked noses, pointed ears, and pale eyes that track movement with the involuntary precision of a predator's gaze. Their plumage ranges from tawny to dark brown across individuals, reflected in hair coloration, and their wingspan averages twenty feet fully extended. They are somewhat taller and thinner than humans — averaging six feet and one hundred thirty pounds — reach full maturity around twenty-five years of age, and may live to one hundred fifty or two hundred. Their stillness in conversation is frequently read as coldness by those unfamiliar with them. It is not coldness, it is the attention of a people accustomed to watching the horizon.

**Hawkman (Strix)**

*+2 Dexterity, −2 Charisma.* Hawkmen are swift and perceptive but tend toward directness that others sometimes find abrasive.

*Medium.* Hawkmen are Medium creatures with no size bonuses or penalties.

*Normal Speed.* Hawkmen have a base speed of 30 feet.

*Fly Speed.* Hawkmen have a fly speed of 60 feet (average maneuverability).

*Darkvision.* Hawkmen see in the dark up to 60 feet.

*Natural Attacks.* Hawkmen have two talon attacks, each dealing 1d3 points of damage.

*Suspicious.* Hawkmen receive a +2 racial bonus on Sense Motive checks.

*Languages.* Hawkmen begin play speaking Common and Strix. Hawkmen with high Intelligence scores may choose from Auran, Dwarven, Gnome, Sylvan, and Undercommon.

**Alternate Racial Traits**

*Auzran Sight (replaces Suspicious):* You gain a +2 racial bonus on Perception checks. This bonus increases to +4 when the target is at a distance of 60 feet or more.

*Magitek Heritage (replaces one talon attack):* The Hawkmen of Auzra developed an extensive magitek tradition in partnership with the Grey Elves of that world. You gain a +2 racial bonus on Craft (Magitek) and Knowledge (arcana) checks.

---

### Fetchlings

The Fetchlings, or Kayal as they call themselves, are Nocturne's native people — a race shaped over generations by life in perpetual twilight, their features pale and shadowed, their senses calibrated to the intervals between light and dark that constitute all of Nocturne's illumination. Those present on Nexus are the descendants of individuals who were traveling the Road when the apocalypse struck; they did not return to Nocturne in time to be sealed within it, or chose not to. Their community is small, concentrated in the quieter precincts of the city, and their reputation is the one that artistic peoples generally acquire in practical societies: admired in the abstract, somewhat difficult to know personally. Fetchling artisans and performers have contributed to Nexian cultural life out of proportion to their numbers, which has done nothing to make them easier to understand.

**Fetchling (Kayal)**

*+2 Dexterity, +2 Charisma, −2 Wisdom.* Fetchlings are swift and perceptive in social matters but are not always attentive to immediate practical dangers.

*Medium.* Fetchlings are Medium creatures with no size bonuses or penalties.

*Normal Speed.* Fetchlings have a base speed of 30 feet.

*Darkvision.* Fetchlings see in the dark up to 60 feet.

*Low-Light Vision.* Fetchlings see twice as far as humans in conditions of dim light.

*Skilled.* Fetchlings gain a +2 racial bonus on Knowledge (planes) and Stealth checks.

*Shadow Blending (Su).* Attacks against a Fetchling in dim light have a 50% miss chance rather than the normal 20%. This trait does not grant total concealment; the attacker must simply defeat the miss chance.

*Shadowy Resistance.* Fetchlings have cold resistance 5 and electricity resistance 5.

*Spell-Like Ability.* A Fetchling may use *disguise self* once per day as a spell-like ability. Caster level equals character level.

*Languages.* Fetchlings begin play speaking Common and D'ziriak. Fetchlings with high Intelligence scores may choose from Aklo, Draconic, Infernal, and Undercommon.

## Classes

All standard Pathfinder classes are available in the Rainbow Road campaign. The following paragraphs situate each class within the culture of Nexus and the broader context of the Nine Worlds; they describe how each tradition exists in this setting, not restrictions on how individual characters are built. Custom archetypes follow.

**Barbarian.** The tradition Nexians call barbarian is, on examination, a collection of distinct fighting disciplines from across the Nine Worlds that have blended imperfectly over five hundred years of isolation. Some trace their lineage to Tanis, where communities adapted their combat practices to confined terrain. Others originate in the outer ring of Vatra, where working the forge-cities required a tolerance for sustained physical exertion that eventually formalized into a fighting style. On Nexus, the barbarian tradition is most strongly associated with the militia's shock units — the fighters who bear the brunt of demon raids and have learned that what the situation requires, refinement rarely provides.

**Bard.** The Song of Heaven is not merely a metaphor in the Nine Worlds — it was, before the apocalypse, a literal phenomenon: a sequence of resonant frequencies embedded in the Rainbow Road's structure that mythic champions could hear and draw upon. The bard tradition on Nexus preserves echoes of this understanding, whether or not its practitioners are aware of the connection. Bards in Nexus are the city's historians, its working musicians, its storytellers, and its information brokers — people who understand that knowledge travels, and that those who know how to carry it hold a particular kind of power.

**Cleric.** The Host of Heaven grants genuine divine power; the clerics who serve it can demonstrate this as a matter of practical fact. What they cannot report is a word from Heaven in return. The clerics of Nexus maintain the faith's rites and texts with the conviction of people who believe that the silence does not constitute abandonment — only absence — and who find in the continued flow of divine power sufficient evidence that the celestials remain engaged even if they will not speak. They are not numerous, and the city treats them with a respect calibrated precisely to the distance between acknowledgment and genuine devotion.

**Druid.** Nexus is not a world that has generated a strong druidic tradition of its own — the continent is managed, its terrain shaped by celestial architects rather than natural process. The druidic practitioners who exist here tend to trace their lineage to Tanis, whose Forest Gnome communities developed a relationship with primal magic that had no equal in the Nine Worlds. Others are simply individuals who found in the wild margins of the continent a connection that the city's magitek infrastructure could not provide. They are not numerous. The demon-haunted land beyond the walls has given them more work than any druid would prefer.

**Fighter.** The Nexian militia is the city's primary defense against demon raids, and the fighter tradition is its backbone. Five hundred years of continuous low-grade warfare against a tactically sophisticated enemy has produced a military culture that is neither glamorous nor sentimental about its role. Nexian fighters train from childhood, rotate through militia service as adults, and carry the institutional memory of five centuries of raids in their doctrine and their instincts. The best of them are distinguished not by strength or technique in isolation but by the ability to hold a position when the line is stretched thin — which is, in practice, most of the time.

**Gunslinger.** The magitek firearm tradition in Nexus is approximately two centuries old, developed at the Foundry from principles derived from pre-war artifacts recovered from Vatra and Auzra. Gunslingers are elite within the militia — not merely for the damage their weapons produce but for the training required to operate and maintain them. The weapons are not simple. The gunslingers who master them understand the relevant principles of magitek well enough to diagnose a malfunction in the field and the patience to accept that the weapon will occasionally disagree with them.

**Inquisitor.** The Host of Heaven has, alongside its clerics, a smaller tradition of inquisitors — practitioners who draw on divine power not primarily for healing or ceremony but for pursuit and investigation. On Nexus, inquisitors have historically focused on identifying and rooting out Asura influence: the infiltrators, the cult recruiters, the agents who operate in the spaces between raids. The tradition is old enough to have developed its own body of doctrine, most of which concerns identifying corruption before the signs become obvious. The work is respected in the way that necessary things are respected: quietly, and by people who understand what would happen if it were not done.

**Magus.** The magus tradition — blending spellcasting with martial technique — occupies an unusual position on Nexus: too rare to constitute a formal institution, too useful to the militia to be neglected. A handful of practitioners train through informal apprenticeship, learning the fundamentals of spellwork and blade-craft in whatever combination their mentor emphasizes. Those who also develop an aptitude for magitek are particularly valued; the ability to channel arcane energy through an engineered device as readily as through a blade is rare and practically irreplaceable in a city whose defense depends on both.

**Monk.** The contemplative martial traditions that Nexians group under the label of monk arrived with the populations of several different worlds, each with its own philosophical framework and its own relationship to discipline, suffering, and transcendence. They have not merged; a student must choose a lineage, and the lineages do not agree on fundamental questions. What they share is the commitment to mastery of the self as a prerequisite to anything else — a position that five centuries of demon raids have validated more consistently than their founders could have anticipated.

**Oracle.** Practitioners whose divine gifts arrive unbidden and unclear in their ultimate purpose are treated in Nexus with a mixture of practical respect and personal caution. The existence of the actual Oracle east of the city gives the class a particular resonance; individuals who manifest divine revelation they did not seek and cannot fully control are assumed by many to be responding to the same underlying force, whatever that force is. Nexian oracles learn early to be precise about what they know, careful about what they claim, and patient with people who want both to consult them and to keep them at arm's length.

**Paladin.** The Host of Heaven has always had its knights. In the pre-war era, the Illian tradition of the Church Ascendant produced the most celebrated paladins in the Nine Worlds — warriors who treated honor, service, and sacrifice as practical commitments rather than aspirational ideals. On Nexus, that tradition has been maintained in reduced form by the oldest families of the faith. The Knights of the Host are modest in number and substantial in conviction. They are not the militia's shock troops; they are the people who show up where the militia cannot hold and stay until the situation resolves.

**Ranger.** The Nexian ranger tradition developed for a specific purpose: monitoring demon activity beyond the city walls, maintaining the observation post network that gives the militia its advance warning of raids, and recovering those who have become lost in hostile terrain. It is unglamorous work in demanding conditions. The rangers who do it tend toward a practical taciturnity that outsiders occasionally mistake for unfriendliness. They are not unfriendly. They are people who have spent significant time in terrain where unnecessary noise is dangerous, and the habit persists.

**Rogue.** The city of Nexus has enough institutional complexity — enough opacity, enough competing interests, enough information that someone always wants and someone else wants to keep — to sustain a rogue tradition ranging from legitimate information brokerage to outright crime. Five centuries of self-contained existence have produced a shadow economy as layered as any city's, and the individuals who navigate it most successfully are those who understand that in a city this old, the relationships between people and institutions are always more complicated than they appear.

**Sorcerer.** Sorcerers on Nexus carry bloodlines established before the apocalypse and maintained since by the ordinary mechanisms of heredity — imperfectly and without institutional support. The full range of bloodlines documented in the Pathfinder system exists somewhere in Nexus's population. One bloodline carries particular significance in this campaign: the bloodline of the mythic heroes who walked the Rainbow Road. Descendants of those ten figures occasionally manifest powers that scholars associate with the celestial system itself. A custom bloodline is detailed in the archetypes section below.

**Witch.** The witch tradition on Nexus is old enough to have acquired the reputation that witches generally acquire in cities under sustained siege: useful, distrusted, and never entirely understood even by the people relying on them. Nexian witches draw their power from patrons whose identities they typically decline to specify, and the city has learned not to ask too pointedly. The militia has employed them during difficult raids. The court consults them occasionally. Neither institution has found a satisfactory framework for what they are.

**Wizard.** The scholarly tradition on Nexus is concentrated in the Foundry's research wing and a smaller collection of private academies that have maintained libraries through five centuries of periodic crisis. Nexian wizards tend toward specialization in areas of immediate practical value — combat magic, divination, and the arcane theory underlying magitek — because those are the areas that have attracted funding and produced practitioners willing to teach. The purely theoretical tradition is smaller but real, sustained by scholars who believe that understanding how the Nine Worlds were built is more important than any immediate application of that understanding. They are probably right. They are also difficult to fund.

### Mythic Hero Bloodline (Sorcerer)

You are descended from one of the many heroes who walked the Rainbow Road before the apocalypse — mythic champions who received the full measure of celestial power the system could grant and then joined the Host of Heaven. The Song of Heaven runs in your blood. You did not choose this, it simply is.

**Bloodline Powers:**

*Song Fragment (Sp):* At 1st level, you can emit a 30-foot burst of harmonic energy as a standard action a number of times per day equal to 3 + your Charisma modifier. Allies within the area gain fast healing 1. Asuras within the area are dazzled for as long as they remain within it.

*Celestial Resonance (Su):* At 15th level, you gain damage reduction 5/evil and immunity to fear. Once per day as an immediate action, when you or an ally within 30 feet would be reduced to 0 or fewer hit points, you may grant that creature temporary hit points equal to your sorcerer level. These temporary hit points last 1 minute.

*Avatar of the Road (Su):* At 20th level, you become immune to sonic and light effects. Your spells and spell-like abilities with the sonic or light descriptor ignore 10 points of energy resistance. ### Magitek Engineer (Wizard)

Where most wizards distinguish sharply between arcane magic and engineered devices, the Magitek Engineer treats both as expressions of a single underlying principle: the structured application of energy toward a predictable effect. The distinction between a spell and a magitek item is, in her view, primarily one of medium.

*Replaces:* Arcane School (all school powers and bonus spells).

**Magitek Interface (Ex):** At 1st level, the Magitek Engineer gains Craft (Magitek) as a class skill and Magitek Apprentice as a bonus feat, ignoring prerequisites. She may identify magitek items using a Spellcraft check (DC 15 + the item's effective spell level).

**Charge Tap (Su):** As a standard action, the Magitek Engineer may drain charges from a held magitek item to recover a prepared spell slot. Each charge drained restores one spell level (minimum 1 charge per level recovered). The maximum slot level recoverable equals half the wizard's level (minimum 1st). This ability may be used a number of times per day equal to 3 + Intelligence modifier.

**Overclock (Su):** At 5th level, when activating a held magitek item, the Magitek Engineer may expend 2 additional charges to increase the save DC of the effect by 1. This may be applied up to 3 times per activation (maximum +3 to save DC, 6 additional charges expended).

**Magitek Attunement (Ex):** At 10th level, the Magitek Engineer may spend 1 hour attuning to a single magitek item. While attuned, that item regains 1 charge per day automatically. The engineer may be attuned to only one item at a time; attuning to a new item ends the previous attunement.

### Magitek Warrior (Magus)

### Demon Hunter (Vampire Hunter)

Five centuries of contact with demon infiltrators has produced a tradition of specialists who have studied the enemy closely enough to use its own nature against it. **Asura Focus (Su):** At 1st level, the Demon Hunter selects one Asura ability type — fear aura, energy drain, damage reduction penetration, or spell resistance reduction. **Purified Strike (Su):** Any weapon the Demon Hunter wields functions as cold iron and good-aligned for the purpose of overcoming Asura damage reduction.

**Demon Tracker (Ex):** At 7th level, the Demon Hunter can track an Asura in any form — gaseous, incorporeal, or dimension-shifted. The base DC for such tracking is 20.

**Demon Bane (Su):** At 11th level, the Demon Hunter can present a holy symbol of the Host of Heaven as a standard action. All Asuras within 30 feet must succeed on a Will save (DC 10 + ½ class level + Charisma modifier) or be repelled, as per *turn undead* applied to Asuras.

**Purge Corruption (Su):** At 13th level, the Demon Hunter can spend 10 minutes and expend a flask of holy water to cleanse the site of a slain Asura's manifestation, preventing its taint from lingering and blocking a destroyed Asura from possessing a new host at that location.

**Master Demon Hunter (Ex):** At 20th level, on a successful attack against an Asura, the Demon Hunter may choose to destroy it, pin it in place, or paralyze it — the same mechanics as Master Vampire Hunter, applied to Asuras.

## Mythic Paths

Mythic power in the Rainbow Road campaign is not an achievement. It is not earned through training, accumulated through experience, or granted as a reward for virtue. The celestial system was designed from its earliest days to identify mortal champions capable of carrying the weight of the Nine Worlds and to grant them, at the right moment, a strength beyond the ordinary measure of heroism. The system is still running. It has been waiting five hundred years.

Your character will be endowned with mythic power as part of this campaign. Prior to this, you should select a mythic path for your character to follow during character creation. The six paths each represent a different resonance between a champion's nature and the celestial power they carry. The paths are not assigned; they arise from who the character already is, amplified.

**Archmage.** The Song resonates through spellwork. For the Archmage, mythic ascension is the recognition that every spell she has ever cast has been an imperfect attempt at something the Song expresses perfectly. Magic becomes not merely technique but attunement — an increasingly precise instrument for articulating what the celestial system has always been trying to say through her.

**Champion.** The Song resonates through the body. Combat, for the mythic Champion, becomes something other than violence — a form of expression that the celestial system has always understood as one of the Nine Worlds' primary languages. The Champion does not become stronger in the way that training makes someone stronger. She becomes stronger in the way that a word spoken at exactly the right moment carries more weight than the same word at any other time.

**Guardian.** The Song resonates through endurance. The Guardian holds. Whatever the cost, whatever the circumstance, however thin the line has been stretched, she holds. The celestial system did not design this path for those who fight well. It designed it for those who understand that the point is not to win every engagement but to ensure that what matters is still standing when it is over.

**Hierophant.** The Song resonates through faith. For the Hierophant, mythic ascension is confirmation of something she already believed without being able to prove: that the Host of Heaven has not abandoned the Nine Worlds, that the silence was not indifference, and that the power of what the celestials built can still be accessed by those who understand what it was built for. What the Host of Heaven believed becomes, in her hands, real.

**Marshal.** The Song resonates through connection. The Warlocks — the corrupted former heroes who have ruled the outer worlds for five centuries — were not defeated by superior force. They were reached by the people who refused to stop seeing them as people. The Marshal's mythic power is the recognition that the war the Nine Worlds have been fighting is not, at its root, a military problem. It is a problem of isolation, and the solution is the same thing the Rainbow Road was always for.

**Trickster.** The Song resonates through adaptability. Every world on the Rainbow Road is new — new terrain, new rules, new dangers, new possibilities. The celestial system designed the journey to require a particular kind of intelligence: the willingness to arrive without assumptions and learn what the situation actually is before deciding what to do about it. The Trickster's mythic power is the recognition that this flexibility is not a compromise. It is a strength the Road was specifically built to reward.

**Special Ability - The Vessel of the Song.** Mythic power in this campaign is not lost permanently with the character who carries it. If a character dies before reaching Mythic Tier 9, the power seeks a new vessel on the current world. A new character enters play at the same level and tier as the character who fell, carrying inherited memories of their predecessor's journey — fragmented, emotional rather than precise, but sufficient to understand what has come before. Death at Tier 9 or above triggers the Immortal ability: the character returns to life after twenty-four hours, fully restored.

## Magitek Items

Magitek items are a category of equipment unique to the Nine Worlds — produced primarily in Nexus and, before the apocalypse, in the forge-cities of Vatra. They function by drawing on a charged magitek battery rather than on ambient magic or the user's own power; the effect is produced when charges are expended through activation, and the item becomes inert when its charges are exhausted. An uncharged magitek item does nothing, but its charges can be later restored. 

Magitek items can be recharged by a practitioner with the appropriate training, making them more economical over time than equivalent standard magic items, at the cost of ongoing maintenance.

**Basic magitek items** hold up to 10 charges and replicate the effects of spells of 3rd level or lower. Each item produces a single effect, activated by expending charges as specified in the item's description.

**Advanced magitek items** hold up to 25 charges and replicate the effects of spells of up to 5th level. An advanced item may be constructed with multiple effects, each operating independently from the same charge pool.

### Using Magitek Items

Activating a magitek item expends one or more charges and produces the item's designated effect. Any character may attempt to use a magitek item, but those without proper training must succeed on a Use Magic Device check, as they would for any unfamiliar magic item. Characters with the Magitek Apprentice feat may use basic magitek items without a check. Characters with the Magitek Adept feat may use advanced magitek items without a check.

### Recharging Magitek Items

Restoring charges to a depleted magitek item requires the Magitek Journeyman feat and the ability to cast arcane spells. The character expends prepared spell slots in any combination, restoring 1 charge per spell level expended. A character with the Magitek Mastery feat may forgo charges entirely, expending a prepared spell to power a magitek item's effect for a single activation; save DCs for effects powered this way increase by 2.

### Using Magitek Weapons and Armor

Activating a magitek weapon or armor requires expending 1 charge as a swift action. Once activated, the item's enchantment bonus and special powers are available for 1 hour.

Recharging a magitek weapon or armor follows the same process as recharging a standard magitek item but at a significantly higher cost, reflecting the sustained enchantment these items maintain rather than the discrete activations of standard magitek devices. To restore 1 charge to a magitek weapon or armor, a character must expend a number of spell levels equal to the item's effective enchantment bonus multiplied by 5. A magitek weapon with a total effective bonus of +3 requires 15 spell levels per charge restored; one with a +5 bonus requires 25.

### New Skill: Craft (Magitek)

A specialized application of the Craft skill that allows the character to construct and modify magitek items and magitek-enhanced equipment. Crafting costs are as follows:

*Magitek items (basic or advanced):* 100 gp per spell level of each effect included in the item, paid at creation.

*Magitek weapons and armor:* 150 gp per point of effective enchantment bonus placed on the item.

## Character Hooks

The following six questions are answered during character creation and shared with the Game Master. They are not backstory questions; they are questions about who your character is now, at the moment the campaign begins. The answers will become relevant during play.

Every character answers all six.

1. You have lived in Nexus for some or all of your life. What is the one thing about the city — one institution, one practice, one person — that you genuinely love?

2. You have had at least one encounter with a demon. Not a battle — an encounter. What happened, and what did it leave behind?

3. The Gate Towns are a peculiar fact of Nexian life — eight settlements built for a road that went dark before anyone living can remember, maintained for five centuries by a king who has outlived everyone who knew the original purpose firsthand. What do you they mean to you?

4. Casomir Zukra has ruled Nexus for five hundred years. What is your honest opinion of him?

5. The Host of Heaven is real — Heaven is visible in the daytime sky, the celestials are documented fact, and the faith has its texts and its history. What does a person do with that knowledge?

6. If a greater power told you that saving the Nine Worlds would cost you something you considered essential to who you are — your memory of someone, a relationship, a part of yourself — what would you do?
