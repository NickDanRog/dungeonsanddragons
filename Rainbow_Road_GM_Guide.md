# RAINBOW ROAD
## Game Master's Guide

*A Pathfinder 1st Edition adventure for characters of levels 1–20*
*Mythic Tiers 1–10 (Mythic Adventures)*

---

# INTRODUCTION

## About This Adventure

**Rainbow Road** is a mythic-scale campaign adventure set in the Nine Worlds — pocket dimensions suspended in the Ethereal Plane, linked by a network of planar pathways called the Rainbow Road. The adventure spans twenty levels and ten mythic tiers, following the player characters from ordinary residents of the hub world Nexus through a journey across eight outer worlds, each ruled by a corrupted hero called a Warlock, to a final descent into the Underworld to destroy the source of the corruption that has held the Nine Worlds in stasis for five centuries.

The central secret of the campaign is the Nine Worlds' forgotten purpose. The worlds, the Rainbow Road, and the celestial construct called Heaven were built by three races of celestial beings as a system for finding and empowering mortal champions of good — imbuing them with mythic power through a progressive journey of heroic deeds. Five hundred years ago, the system produced its greatest cohort: ten heroes who attempted to destroy demonic influence permanently by severing the connection to the Underworld using a powerful magitek artifact. The plan backfired catastrophically, allowing demons to physically enter the worlds. The heroes won the war they had caused, but at devastating cost — while over ninety-five percent of the demon population was destroyed, Heaven itself was nearly shattered in the assault and each of the Nine Worlds were left in ruins. In the aftermath, each hero returned to one of the worlds of their own choice, carrying the mythic power they had accumulated. The corruption found them one by one, through the very virtues that had made them heroes. The Rainbow Road was shut down. The worlds fell into isolation. The champions became tyrants.

The player characters are the system's second attempt. They walk the same path their predecessors walked, receive the same mythic power from the same places of power, and face the same fundamental question the original heroes failed: whether the accumulation of power must inevitably produce corruption, or whether it is possible to carry it and remain whole. The adventure's answer depends entirely on what the players do. Each Warlock can be turned — restored through the presentation of a specific object, person, or truth that reconnects them to who they were before the corruption took hold — or killed. The ratio of turnings to killings accumulates across eight worlds into a corruption score that determines the power of the final antagonist, the strength of the allied forces available for the campaign's climactic battle, and the shape of the ending the party reaches. Four distinct endings are possible.

## How to Use This Guide

This book is organized as follows:

- **The Realm** covers the hub world Nexus, the Nine Worlds in overview, and the key NPCs the party will encounter before their journey begins. For extended world detail — geography, history, culture, and population — refer to the companion document `nine_worlds.md`. Each world chapter in this guide contains the adventure-specific content for that world; the reference document provides the deeper setting context.
- **Running the Adventure** covers all mechanical and narrative systems: milestone leveling, the Song of Heaven progression, mythic tier advancement, the Corruption Mechanic, the Warlock Turning Framework, the Ally-Tracking System, the Battle of Nexus, and the Multiple Endings Framework. Read this section before Chapter 1 and keep it accessible throughout.
- **Chapters 1–13** present the adventure in three phases. Phase 1 (Chapters 1–3) covers the Nexus prologue. Phase 2 (Chapters 4–11) covers the eight outer worlds in sequence. Phase 3 (Chapters 12–13) covers the endgame. Each chapter begins with a summary and GM guidance, followed by scene-by-scene descriptions with read-aloud text, GM notes, encounter information, and skill check DCs.
- **Appendices A–C** contain stat block references, magic items, and random tables. Full PF1e stat blocks for key NPCs and creatures are provided in a separate stat block appendix document.

**Read-aloud text** is presented in blockquote format:

> *This text is meant to be read aloud or paraphrased to your players. It describes what characters see, hear, and experience — not what they conclude.*

**GM notes** appear in italics and address pacing, likely player divergences, and conditional outcomes. Where the text says *"if the party does X,"* treat that as conditional logic, not prescription.

## Adventure Synopsis

**Phase 1: Nexus (Chapters 1–3, Levels 1–4, Mythic Tier 1)**
The characters begin as residents of Nexus, the hub world, drawn into the service of its king — **Casomir Zukra**, the leader of the original ten heroes and the only one who has maintained his sanity through alchemical suppression of the corruption that is slowly killing him. Two assignments prove the party's worth and bring them before the **Nexian Oracle**, an ancient being whose cryptic message confirms what Casomir already suspects: there is no separating him from the Beast, the monstrous manifestation of his corruption. Casomir engineers a controlled confrontation that forces the party to kill him. His death opens the passage to the **Great Ancillary**, a sealed temple at the heart of Nexus where the party meets celestial representatives, receives their first mythic tier, and is granted the first fragment of the **Song of Heaven** — the melodic key that opens the Rainbow Road to the first of the eight outer worlds.

**Phase 2: The Eight Worlds (Chapters 4–11, Levels 5–19, Mythic Tiers 2–9)**
The party travels the Rainbow Road through eight worlds in fixed sequence — Diaspola, Tanis, Auzra, Arlune, Vatra, Illium, Nocturne, Candela — confronting the Warlock who rules each one. Every world presents a unique crisis shaped by its Warlock's specific corruption, a hidden lever that can restore the Warlock to their former self, and a place of power that grants the next mythic tier and the next fragment of the Song of Heaven. The choice to turn or kill each Warlock is the campaign's central recurring decision. Turning is always harder than killing and always produces a better outcome — but the party may not find the lever, may not have the prerequisite knowledge, or may face a Warlock whose turning depends on choices made on a previous world. Throughout Phase 2, demon forces on Nexus grow as the villain **Tornsoul** exploits the progressive reopening of the Rainbow Road to move larger groups through prepared ritual sites. The party becomes aware of this escalation gradually.

**Phase 3: The Endgame (Chapters 12–13, Level 20, Mythic Tier 10)**
After completing their tasks among the eight worlds, the party returns to Nexus to find a demon army massing for assault. They travel the now-open Rainbow Road to rally allies from each liberated world — turned Warlocks contribute their full strength; worlds where Warlocks were killed contribute less or nothing. The **Battle of Nexus** determines whether the city survives and awards the final character level. The party then returns to the Great Ancillary with the complete Song of Heaven, meets the celestial representatives a second and final time, receives mythic tier 10, and descends through a portal into the **Underworld** to face Tornsoul — whose power is scaled by the corruption score accumulated across the entire campaign. Death in the Underworld is permanent. The four possible endings are determined by the corruption score, the strength of the allied forces, whether the party survives the Descent, and the state of the worlds they left behind.

## Themes and Tone

**Rainbow Road** is mythic heroic fantasy with tragic undertones. The PCs are genuine chosen heroes on a grand quest — this is not an ironic or subversive take on the form. The weight comes from the Warlocks' stories and from the stakes, not from horror or grit. Each Warlock was once among the best the Nine Worlds produced: brave, brilliant, selfless, and strong enough to defeat a demonic incursion that nearly destroyed everything. The corruption found each of them through their defining virtue — the witch's love of her people became paranoid isolation, the druid's compassion became an obsession with healing himself that poisoned the earth, the cleric's faith became zealotry steered by a demon whispering in the voice of the gods. These are not villains. They are warnings. The party is walking the same path, accumulating the same power, and the question of whether they can succeed where their predecessors failed is the campaign's thematic spine.

Redemption is the adventure's animating hope, not its guarantee. Turning a Warlock should feel earned — a moment of genuine connection across five centuries of corruption, achieved because the party cared enough to find the right key. Killing a Warlock should feel like a failure, even when it was the only option available. The accumulation of these outcomes across eight worlds is what gives the campaign its emotional shape. Aim for grandeur in the settings, compassion in the character work, and restraint in the prose. Let the facts of the story and its inherent emotion carry weight.

## Adjusting for Your Party

**Rainbow Road** is designed for a party of four to five characters. For other party sizes:

- **Three characters:** Reduce enemy hit points by 25% and lower encounter DCs by 1–2. The Battle of Nexus should emphasize PC spotlight encounters with stronger allied support. Warlock confrontations should include fewer minions. The Underworld Descent should reduce the number of intermediate demon encounters but preserve the Tornsoul fight at full difficulty.
- **Six characters:** Add hit points and additional minions to Warlock confrontations. The Battle of Nexus should include additional encounter waves. Tornsoul should have legendary-equivalent actions that scale with the larger party.

**If the party misses a lever:** This is expected to happen at least once across eight worlds. The adventure is designed to function whether the party turns every Warlock, none of them, or any combination in between. Missing a lever means a harder fight against the Warlock, a weaker allied contribution from that world, and a higher corruption score — but it does not break the adventure. Do not rescue parties from missed levers. The consequences are the point.

**If the party refuses to kill Casomir:** Casomir has planned for this. The Beast will emerge regardless — the conversation in the sealed arena is the trigger, not an optional path. If the party hesitates, Casomir will provoke the transformation himself. The scene must end with his death. What the party can control is whether it feels like a mercy or a tragedy.

**If the party wants to skip a world:** The Song of Heaven mechanic prevents this — each fragment opens the path to the next world in sequence, and only to the next world. The party cannot skip ahead. If a party is eager to move on from a world they find less engaging, shorten the investigation phase and move to the Warlock confrontation sooner, but do not skip the place of power scene or the Song fragment.

**Pacing across eight worlds:** Some groups will spend three sessions on a world; others will spend six. The per-world chapter template is designed to scale. Scenes 2 and 3 (investigation and complication) can be expanded with additional side content or compressed to a single session if the party is driving toward the Warlock confrontation. The arrival, the Warlock scene, and the place of power scene should always be given their full weight.

# THE REALM

## Nexus — The Heart of the Nine Worlds

Nexus is a small continent surrounded by the featureless mists of the Ethereal Plane, temperate in climate and varied in terrain. It contains forests, plains, hills, and rivers in proportions that would be familiar to any traveler of the prime material worlds — a landscape that reads as unremarkable until one understands what it was built for. At the center of the realm stands the great city of Nexus, the largest settlement in the Nine Worlds and the place from which all journeys along the Rainbow Road once began.

Near the edges of the continent, equidistant from one another, stand the eight gate towns. Each is built around the Rainbow Road terminus to one of the outer worlds, and the terrain surrounding each gate town subtly shifts to reflect the character of the realm it connects to — the approach to Auzra's terminus rises into rocky outcroppings swept by persistent winds, while the land near Diaspola's gate grows marshy and low, threaded with mist. These reflections are never dangerous or otherworldly, merely evocative. A perfectly circular road of rainbow-colored tiles connects each gate town to those on either side, forming a great ring around the continent's perimeter. Those possessed of mythic power find their speed upon this road increased to an extraordinary degree, shortening what would otherwise be a journey of days to a matter of minutes. A third set of rainbow-tiled roads extends inward from each gate town along straight spokes, connecting the ring to the city at the continent's center. The gate towns have stood empty since the apocalypse.

The city itself was once surrounded by a broad belt of farmlands and villages. These were destroyed in the demon war and have not been rebuilt. The demons that poured into the Nine Worlds during the apocalypse struck Nexus first and hardest, and their remnants troubled the realm long after the initial fighting ended. Under Casomir Zukra's leadership, the survivors organized themselves and fought off several major concentrations over the course of years. When the immediate threat subsided, researchers determined that the demons were either reproducing or continuing to arrive in small numbers from the Underworld. Attacks have continued in the centuries since, though in smaller and more dispersed groups — multiple simultaneous raids across the city designed to keep the defenders off balance. The people of Nexus tore down two full districts and converted them to farmland, an arrangement that has proven more than sufficient to feed the now much-reduced population. Magitek foundries that existed before the war, restored and maintained by Casomir, keep the populace well armed, and they generally handle demon raids without significant difficulty.

In ancient times, Nexus also contained portals to other planes throughout the multiverse, allowing travel and trade with the wider cosmos. All of these portals went dark during the apocalypse. The Nine Worlds are effectively severed from the rest of the planes until the Rainbow Road is fully restored.

### The People of Nexus

The primary race of Nexus is human, though the city's long history as the crossroads of the Nine Worlds has left it with a population of considerable diversity. Members of every race found on the other eight worlds live here, along with representatives of other races from across the planes who were trapped in the realm when the portals closed. Humans make up the majority, and their customs and language form the baseline of the city's culture, but the streets of Nexus hold more variety in a single district than most worlds contain across their entire territory.

### Key Locations

**The City of Nexus** sprawls across the continent's center in a pattern that reflects centuries of organic growth layered atop the original planned construction. The city was designed by the celestial builders as a hub for travelers passing through the Rainbow Road, and its bones still show this intent — broad avenues radiate outward from the center, public spaces are oversized for the current population, and the infrastructure assumes a flow of visitors that has not existed for five hundred years. What has grown up around and over those bones is messier and more human: residential districts that accumulated by need, market streets that follow foot traffic rather than any plan, defensive fortifications erected during the demon war that have since been absorbed into the cityscape. Two districts were torn down entirely and converted to farmland, and the fields within the city walls are among the stranger sights Nexus offers — rows of grain growing between the foundations of former houses, tended by farmers who live in the adjacent blocks.

**The Great Ancillary** lies in an ancient temple at the very center of Nexus, making it the central point of the Rainbow Road and the place where the mythic journey both begins and ends. The temple is heavily fortified and tightly sealed, accessible only by means of a specific key. Inside stands a great magitek machine in the form of an orrery: eight spheres, each representing one of the outer worlds, orbit a central sphere representing Nexus, while a tenth sphere above the mechanism represents Heaven, casting light down upon the arrangement. A great diagram of powerful arcane runes is inscribed on the floor beneath the central sphere, glowing with persistent light. When a bearer of the key enters the chamber, the orrery activates of its own accord. Those who step into the runic circle are taken outside of space and time to meet representatives of the three celestial races of Heaven. It is here that the mythic journey begins in earnest, and here that it ends.

**Casomir's Citadel** occupies the high ground at the northern edge of the city, a fortified palace that served as the administrative center of Nexus before the apocalypse and now serves as the seat of Casomir's reluctant kingship. The citadel contains his private quarters, his alchemical laboratory, his council chambers, and — in a heavily reinforced basement accessible through a sequence of locked and warded doors — the purpose-built prison where he confines himself when the Beast's transformation approaches. The prison has failed to contain the Beast on multiple occasions, a fact of which Casomir is painfully aware.

**The Oracle's Sanctum** lies some distance east of the city, a structure of uncertain age set into the landscape at the edge of the settled farmlands where the terrain begins its long, empty run toward the Diaspola gate town. It is partially overgrown and largely forgotten by the general populace. The Nexian Oracle resides here. Few seek her counsel in the present day, and those who do are generally turned away without explanation. The surrounding area has become a concentration point for demon activity — the land between the city walls and the sanctum is among the most dangerous territory on the continent, and reaching the Oracle requires crossing it. This is not a coincidence, though the nature of the connection is not widely understood.

**The Magitek Foundries** are concentrated in the western industrial quarter, a complex of workshops and fabrication halls that produce the arms, tools, and devices on which the city's defense and daily life depend. Casomir restored these foundries after the war and has maintained them through a combination of personal expertise and a small corps of trained artificers. The foundries are the city's most strategically valuable assets after the Ancillary itself, and they are defended accordingly.

**The Gate Towns** ring the continent's perimeter, eight small settlements built around the Rainbow Road termini to the outer worlds. All have been abandoned since the Road closed. Their buildings stand empty, their streets silent, their gate structures dark. As the party progresses through the campaign and the Road reopens world by world, the gate towns reactivate — their structures hum with energy, their roads glow with color, and in some cases, populations begin to trickle back. The state of each gate town is a visible measure of the party's progress.

### Daily Life

Life in Nexus is shaped by two facts: the magitek infrastructure that sustains the city, and the demon raids that periodically threaten it. The foundries produce everything from weapons and armor to the tools used in the converted farmland districts, and their output is sufficient to keep the population fed, armed, and functional. Demon raids are a fact of life — citizens drill for them, maintain personal barricades, and accept them with a resigned pragmatism that would strike outsiders as remarkable. The raids are smaller and more tactical than they once were, multiple simultaneous strikes across different districts designed to stretch the defense force thin, but the populace has adapted.

The full moon is another matter. When the moon is full, the people of Nexus barricade their homes and do not emerge until morning. Beast Blavin hunts on these nights, and the creature's passage through the city leaves destruction and death in its wake. Some citizens have organized hunting parties to track and kill the Beast despite Casomir's express prohibition. All have died. The barricades hold, most of the time, and the city cleans up after each visitation with the grim efficiency of long practice. Certain researchers have noted that demon attacks tend to increase in the days following the Beast's appearances, though no one has proposed a satisfactory explanation for the correlation.

## The Power Structure

### Casomir Zukra — King of Nexus

Casomir Zukra was a human fighter and alchemist, a specialist in magitek equipment, and the leader of the ten heroes who last walked the Rainbow Road. He returned to Nexus after the war to find the city under siege by remnants of the demon army. He rallied the defenders, organized the counterattack, and held the city together through the worst of the crisis. The people proclaimed him king in the aftermath — an office he did not seek but has tried to honor with as little ceremony as possible.

Casomir is the only Warlock who has maintained enough of his original self to understand what has happened to him and to act on that understanding. He is aware of the corruption affecting all of the former heroes and took proactive steps to deal with it through alchemy. His efforts partially succeeded, but the side effect was catastrophic: his corruption manifests as the Beast Blavin, a gigantic mutated creature. The transformation is triggered under two circumstances. The first is the accumulation of magical residue — each time Casomir uses his personal magic or mythic abilities, the residue builds, and when it reaches a critical threshold the Beast emerges. The second is the full moon, which triggers the transformation reliably regardless of how much magic Casomir has used. The guaranteed lunar transformation is the reason the people of Nexus barricade themselves on the night of the full moon, though the Beast is known by the people of Nexus to appear at other times. Casomir takes elaborate precautions — supposed hunting trips, the reinforced prison beneath his citadel — but the Beast knows everything Casomir knows and always finds a way out. It always kills.

Casomir governs through a small council of advisors and a corps of administrators who manage the city's daily operations. He is respected by the populace as a protector and feared as the source of Blavin, though no one speaks the connection aloud. His governance is competent rather than inspired — he maintains the foundries, organizes the defense, and ensures the food supply, but the city has not grown or improved under his rule. He is holding the line, and he knows that the line will not hold forever.

### The Dark Secret (GM Only)

The truth about Beast Blavin is worse than anyone suspects. The Beast is not merely a destructive byproduct of Casomir's failed alchemical containment — it is an active agent of the demonic corruption, drawing on Casomir's mythic power to gate demons into Nexus through the remnant planar connections that the apocalypse left behind. The Beast's gating capacity is limited, which has prevented the demon numbers from becoming overwhelming, but the demons have been exploiting this channel for centuries. They have changed their tactics accordingly: the smaller, simultaneous raids that now characterize demon attacks on the city are designed to keep the defenders off balance while concealing the true extent of the growing demon population outside the city walls. They are amassing forces for a massive assault — the largest since the end of the war.

Casomir knows about the gating. He has known for years. It is the reason he has resolved to die. He cannot stop the Beast without stopping himself, and every day he lives is another day demons pour into the world through the creature that wears his body. The two quests he assigns to the party in Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 are not idle errands — they are a test, designed to confirm that the PCs are the heroes the Oracle's prophecy describes. When the confirmation comes, Casomir engineers his own death.

The demon army gathering outside Nexus is led — at a distance — by **Tornsoul**, the tenth Warlock, who has been directing the gating operation from the Underworld. Each time the party opens a new segment of the Rainbow Road during Phase 2, Tornsoul exploits the reopened pathway to move larger groups of demons through prepared ritual sites in the wilderness beyond the city. The party is unaware of this escalation until most of the way through the campaign, when reports from the liberated worlds and Nexus itself begin to paint a picture of the growing threat. By the time the party returns to Nexus after Candela, the demon army is ready to strike.

## The Nine Worlds — Overview

The Nine Worlds exist as interlinked pocket dimensions contained together as a single realm within the Ethereal Plane. Each world is a self-contained domain roughly the size of a small continent, possessed of its own terrain, climate, and character. At the edges of each, the land fades into the featureless mists of the Ethereal. Though separate in form, the worlds were built as a unified system — a mechanism to identify worthy mortal champions of good and imbue them with mythic power — and the connections between them are as fundamental to their nature as the land and sky within each one. No one now remembers this purpose except the Warlocks who once walked the Road.

The following entries provide a brief overview of each outer world. For extended detail — geography, history, culture, and population — refer to the companion document `nine_worlds.md`. Each world chapter in this guide contains the full adventure-specific content for that world.

**Diaspola — The World of Water** *(Chapter 4)*
A circular world divided between land and sea, once pastoral and beautiful, now frozen solid under ice and snow. The Halfling population struggles to survive under the de facto rule of Khaga, a corrupted Nixie witch who manipulates the Warlock — Sona Brightspring, the Snow Queen — through an elaborate web of deception. The mythic place of power is the Mother of Rivers, a great magical spring at the center of the land side, now frozen and cursed by the corrupted Fey.

**Tanis — The Realm of Earth** *(Chapter 5)*
A great orb-shaped cavern with the top sliced open, lit by a few hours of sunlight each day. The majority of the cavern floor is covered by the Contamination — a spreading zone of twisted plants, mutated creatures, and infectious fungi emanating from the ziggurat where the Warlock resides. The Dwarven population has retreated to vertical cities drilled into the cavern walls. The Warlock is Garl the Even-Handed, the Howler, a Forest Gnome druid driven to madness by an unhealable wound. The mythic place of power is Gaia's Navel, a golden pool in a cavern beneath the ziggurat.

**Auzra — The Realm of Air** *(Chapter 6)*
Three tiers of floating islands suspended in open sky — the lowest shrouded in toxic gases, the middle wrapped in mist, the highest rising into perpetual twilight. The Grey Elf population lives under a police state administered by the Directorate, a bureaucracy whose agents are bound by magical compulsion. The Hawkmen, the world's original inhabitants, resist from tribal bands in the mist. The Warlock is Tana White-Lightning, the Gray Eminence, an Aasimar wizard who can no longer memorize spells and hoards all knowledge obsessively. The mythic place of power is the Overlook, a clifftop within the Warlock's private garden at the apex of Castle Perilous.

**Arlune — The Realm of the Moon** *(Chapter 7)*
An endless desert called the Twilight Sea, lit only by moonlight, where survival depends on magitek infrastructure. Seven criminal cartels hold the realm in a state of fragile equilibrium, manipulated from the shadows by the Warlock — Garm the Honest, the Trademaster, a Dwarf rogue who is unique among the Warlocks in that he has not been fully corrupted and genuinely wants to turn. The Gnome-majority population trades via magitek sandships. The mythic place of power is the Moonstone, a massive opalescent monolith in a hidden desert valley.

**Vatra — The Realm of Fire** *(Chapter 8)*
A circular world of three concentric rings — volcanic mountains, ruined forge-cities, and a burned rainforest haunted by incorporeal undead. The Tiefling population toils in near-starvation under a construct hierarchy controlled by the Overmind, a hive-mind intelligence made from enslaved souls. The Warlock is Kerlin Whitemane, Blackfire, a Tiefling magus who killed his lover and fed his apprentices to a construct-making machine. The realm's day-to-day administration falls to Haplo the Prophet, who maintains control through a false religion that forbids all mention of the past. The mythic place of power is the Flame Imperishable, a sourceless white flame in an ancient temple within the forges.

**Illium — The Realm of Light** *(Chapter 9)*
A floating continent blessed with more sunlight than any other world, ruled by the Church Ascendant — a theocracy whose entire adult population is bound by the geas of the Crown of Ascension. The Rebellion holds the fortified northwestern island. The Warlock is Tamaron the Just, a Half-Elf cleric possessed by the demon lord Tekticula the Unraveler, who speaks to him in the voice of the gods. Turning Tamaron is critical — it is the prerequisite for turning Baselia on Nocturne. The mythic place of power is the Well of Light, a massive stained glass window in the Cathedral of Light.

**Nocturne — The Realm of Darkness** *(Chapter 10)*
Interconnected valleys in perpetual twilight, once the artistic capital of the Nine Worlds, now cursed. The Fetchling population exists in a state between life and death called the Dream — they do not eat, sleep, or die permanently, and they cannot create anything new. The five valleys are severed by broken light bridges whose power orbs are held by malicious Cabal spirits. The Warlock is Baselia the Undaunted, Lady Sorrow, a Half-Elf anti-paladin and Tamaron's former lover. Her lever is Tamaron himself — she can only be turned if Tamaron was turned on Illium. The mythic place of power is the Womb of Darkness, an orb of supernatural darkness beneath the Citadel of Lament.

**Candela — The Realm of the Stars** *(Chapter 11)*
The smallest and most unusual world — sphere-like constructs suspended in a void, glowing like stars. Once the site of the Arcanium, the greatest university in the Nine Worlds, now completely depopulated except for the Warlock. Ourania Lachos, the Overmind, is a Half-Orc arcanist who has been fused to the Arcanium's magitek control network for five hundred years and is thoroughly insane. She perceives all intruders through the magitek systems and escalates defenses as they approach. Her turning requires the most foreknowledge of any Warlock — a volume of poetry, a key from Casomir's study, and information that only previously turned Warlocks can provide. The mythic place of power is the Stellar Oracle, a magitek machine that taps into the collective unconscious of all beings across the Nine Worlds.

## Heaven and the Underworld

### Heaven — The Artificial Sun

Heaven is a vast sphere of clockwork and magitek construction that moves between the Nine Worlds in sequence, providing light to each realm as it passes overhead. It is the dwelling place of three races of celestial beings — Archons, Angels, and Agathions — who collectively created the Nine Worlds. These beings are not true gods in the conventional sense, but their collective power is such that when worshiped together as the Host of Heaven, they grant divine abilities to their followers equal to those bestowed by a deity. The practice of venerating the Host goes by different names on each world, though its character is broadly the same everywhere. It was the dominant faith across most of the realms before the apocalypse. It has since fallen away in most places, weakened by the waning of celestial power, the general decline of civilization, the influence of the Warlocks, and a widespread belief among the people that the gods have abandoned them.

Heaven was heavily damaged during the demon war. The assault on the celestial sphere was the bloodiest engagement of the entire conflict — the vast majority of demon casualties occurred there — and the damage has never been fully repaired. The celestials endure, diminished but unbroken.

The party never visits Heaven directly. They meet representatives of the three celestial races twice during the campaign, both times at the Great Ancillary on Nexus. The first meeting occurs in Chapter 3, when the party steps into the runic circle after Casomir's death — the celestials confirm their mission and grant the first mythic tier. The final meeting occurs in Chapter 13, after the complete reopening of the Rainbow Road, when the celestials grant mythic tier 10 and offer to open a portal to the Underworld.

### The Underworld — Prison of the Asuras

The Underworld is a vast, generally featureless grey plain that stretches without apparent limit. It was not part of the original design of the Nine Worlds. When the three celestial races were constructing the realms, creatures of hate and ruin — exiles from Hell — infiltrated the work while it was still in progress. The inherent goodness of the mortal populations rejected these entities upon the worlds' completion, and the celestials formed a prison to contain them. The prison held, but it could not sever the influence the intruders had already planted in the fabric of the realms. The people of the Nine Worlds came to call these imprisoned entities demons (though they are technically creatures called Asuras). New demons are formed whenever a mortal willingly corrupted by their influence dies, their soul descending to the Underworld rather than passing to a chosen afterlife.

The most powerful demons — the demon lords (or Asura Ranas) — can shape the terrain to their will, raising bleak mountain ranges and serrated formations that serve as their strongholds. The lesser demons exist in the spaces between these domains, scrabbling and warring in an existence defined by cruelty and want.

The party's visit to the Underworld is brief and intense. After the Battle of Nexus, the celestials open a portal, and the party descends to end Tornsoul's reign. They must fight through progressively more powerful groups of demons to reach her stronghold, where the final encounter of the campaign awaits. Death in the Underworld is permanent — no resurrection magic functions in this place. This is true for Tornsoul and for the PCs alike.

## Key NPCs

### Casomir Zukra — Beast Blavin
*Human Magitek Knight. King of Nexus. Leader of the original ten heroes.*

**Appearance:** A man in his apparent late forties — he is far older than that, sustained by alchemy and mythic power, though the cost is visible. He carries himself with the careful economy of someone who measures every expenditure of energy against the risk of what it might trigger. His hands are scarred from decades of alchemical work, and his eyes hold the particular quality of attention common to people who have been solving the same problem for a very long time but know that a solution is impossible. He dresses practically and without ceremony.

**Personality:** Casomir is tired. Not in the theatrical sense of a man performing exhaustion for sympathy, but in the deep and genuine way of someone who has been fighting a war on two fronts — one against the demons outside his walls and one against the monster inside his body — for five hundred years. He is competent, honest, and fundamentally decent. He does not self-pity. He governs because it needs doing, fights because there is no one else, and has arrived at the conclusion that his death is the most useful thing he has left to offer with the same methodical clarity he has applied to every other problem.

His relationship with the party is that of a commanding officer who knows he is sending his replacements on the mission he failed. He will test them, he will equip them, and when he is satisfied that they are what the Oracle says they are, he will ask them to kill him. This request is not a dramatic gesture — it is a tactical decision. Every day he lives is another day the Beast gates demons into Nexus. He has done the math.

**Role in the adventure:** The Phase 1 anchor. Everything the party does in Chapters 1 through 3 flows through Casomir — his assignments, his revelation, his death. The party's relationship with him, however brief, sets the emotional tone for the entire campaign. If the players feel the weight of killing a good man who cannot be saved, the question of whether the other Warlocks can be saved will carry genuine force across the eight worlds that follow.

**Secret:** He knows about the gating. He has known for years. He has also left a series of contingency instructions — sealed letters, the key to the Great Ancillary, and provisions for the journey — to be discovered by his seneschal, Maranthe Soldati, after his death. Maranthe does not know the full plan. She suspects a connection between Casomir and the Beast but has never pressed the question out of loyalty. Casomir has prepared a letter for her explaining everything, timed to be found after the arena doors seal. He has planned his own death with the same thoroughness he brings to everything else — and he has deliberately excluded the one person closest to him from the decision, because he knows she would try to stop him.

### The Nexian Oracle
*Ancient being. Arbiter of worthiness.*

**Appearance:** The Oracle's form is deliberately left to the GM. The source material describes her as an ancient being of uncertain nature — she may be a celestial remnant, a bound elemental intelligence, or something older. What matters is the impression: age beyond reckoning, authority that does not require explanation, and a quality of attention that makes the person being observed feel as though they are being read at a level they did not know existed.

**Personality:** The Oracle does not explain her judgments. She does not engage in conversation in the ordinary sense. When she speaks, her words are cryptic but not meaningless — they are compressed, layered with significance that only becomes clear in retrospect. She has served as the arbiter of worthiness since before recorded history, and the passage of five hundred years since the last heroes walked the Road has not diminished her patience or her standards.

**Role in the adventure:** The Oracle appears in Chapter 2, when the party reaches her sanctum. Her message to the party confirms what Casomir already suspects — that these are the heroes the system was designed to produce. Her cryptic utterance also contains information that will become relevant across the campaign, seeded early and paid off later. The Oracle does not appear again after the Nexus phase, though her words may be recalled at critical moments.

*GM note: The Oracle's message should be written to contain at minimum three pieces of information that are not immediately interpretable — one relevant to the Casomir death scene, one relevant to a specific Warlock turning, and one relevant to the endgame. The party should not be able to decode these on first hearing. They should have the experience of remembering her words at the exact moment one of them suddenly makes sense.*

### The Celestial Representatives
*Three beings — one Archon, one Angel, one Agathion — who speak for the Host of Heaven.*

The celestial representatives are not characters in the traditional sense. They are presences — vast, radiant, and deeply sorrowful — who appear to the party twice at the Great Ancillary. They do not have names that mortal tongues can pronounce, though they may offer titles or epithets if asked. They are the last functioning connection between the people of the Nine Worlds and the beings who created them.

**First Meeting (Chapter 3):** After the party steps into the runic circle following Casomir's death. The celestials confirm the party's mission, explain the nature of the mythic power they are about to receive, and grant mythic tier 1. They speak of the Rainbow Road, the places of power, and the Song of Heaven. They do not explain the Warlocks' origin or the true purpose of the Nine Worlds — that revelation comes later, from other sources. They are honest about what they can and cannot do: Heaven is damaged, their power diminished, and the party must walk the Road themselves. The celestials cannot intervene directly.

**Final Meeting (Chapter 13):** After the complete reopening of the Rainbow Road and the Battle of Nexus. The celestials grant mythic tier 10 — the full measure of mythic power the system was designed to bestow — and offer to open a portal to the Underworld. This meeting is heavier than the first. The celestials know what the party has done across eight worlds, and their response reflects it. If many Warlocks were turned, the celestials speak with something approaching hope. If many were killed, their sorrow is deeper and their warnings more pointed.

### Tornsoul — Sarena Tal
*Human Summoner. Warden of the Underworld. The tenth Warlock. Final antagonist.*

**Before the fall:** Sarena Tal was a summoner whose bond with her Eidolon was among the strongest ever recorded. She was the only one of the ten heroes who died during the apocalypse — her death was part of the plan. She cast the spell that opened the portal to the Underworld and then descended into it as its Warden, merging her physical form with her Eidolon and carrying a portion of the mythic power willingly granted by each of the other nine heroes. The reservoir of strength was meant to sustain and protect her.

**The corruption:** The demons could not match Sarena's power directly, so they worked at the edges. Over the course of a century, they spiritually wore at the Eidolon's soul, creating an opening through which corruption could seep. Sarena felt the corruption taking hold and responded by turning on her charges — torturing demons endlessly, consuming their soul essence to maintain her fading mythic abilities. The consumption deepened the corruption rather than slowing it. Each century saw her sink further into madness and hate, until nothing remained of the woman who had willingly given her life.

**Present state:** Tornsoul is a being of pure vengeance and ruin, possessed of untold mythic power drawn from her own abilities, the expanded strength of her Eidolon, and centuries of consumed demonic essence. She has sworn to return to the Nine Worlds at the head of a demon army to destroy the realms and Heaven alike. She cannot be turned. She cannot be meaningfully reasoned with — the PCs may attempt it, but the woman they would need to reach no longer exists. The only way to defeat her is destruction.

**Role in the adventure:** Tornsoul is a background presence for most of the campaign. The party learns about her gradually — first as a name, then as a threat, then as the architect of the demon army gathering on Nexus. She is the final encounter in Chapter 13, and her difficulty is directly scaled by the corruption score accumulated across the eight worlds. Each Warlock the party turned reclaimed their portion of the mythic power they originally granted to Sarena; each Warlock killed left their portion with Tornsoul. A party that turned every Warlock faces a diminished Tornsoul. A party that killed them all faces her at full strength.

# RUNNING THE ADVENTURE

## The Song of Heaven

The Song of Heaven is the campaign's structural backbone. It is a melodic key — a sequence of musical phrases that, when performed in full, opens the Rainbow Road in its entirety and unlocks the gate to Heaven. The Song is assembled progressively across the campaign, one fragment at a time, beginning at the Great Ancillary and concluding on Candela.

### How Fragments Are Granted

Each time the party accesses a mythic place of power, the power itself imparts the next fragment of the Song. This is not a physical object — the melody inscribes itself directly into the mythic consciousness of the party members, and they know it as surely as they know their own names. The experience is visceral: the fragment arrives as sound, light, and emotion simultaneously, and each successive fragment builds on the last, extending the melody and deepening its complexity. By the time the Song is complete, the party carries a piece of music that has been accumulating meaning and power across nine worlds.

### Performance

The Song has a performative component. To open a new segment of the Rainbow Road, the party must travel to the appropriate gate town on Nexus and perform the accumulated melody — singing, playing instruments, or simply willing the Song into audible form through their mythic connection to it. The Rainbow Road responds: the rainbow tiles glow, the gate terminus activates, and the pathway to the next world opens. This performance is not a skill check — it succeeds automatically. The purpose is narrative and ceremonial, a moment that marks the transition between worlds and gives the party ownership of their progress.

### The Complete Song

After Candela, the party holds all nine fragments. The complete Song of Heaven is performed at the Great Ancillary in Chapter 13, after the Battle of Nexus, as part of the final celestial meeting. Playing the complete Song is what opens the gate to Heaven and triggers the celestials' appearance to grant mythic tier 10. The Song then carries the party home from the Underworld after Tornsoul's defeat — or, in the worst ending, falls silent forever.

### Fragments Cannot Be Lost

The Song is mythic knowledge, not a physical possession. It cannot be stolen, destroyed, or forgotten. If a party member who carries fragments dies and is resurrected (outside the Underworld, where death is permanent), the fragments persist. If the entire party is somehow incapacitated, the fragments endure in their mythic essence and reassert themselves when consciousness returns.

### Mythic Path Selection

Players choose their mythic path when they receive mythic tier 1 at the Great Ancillary. All six PF1e mythic paths are available: Archmage, Champion, Guardian, Hierophant, Marshal, and Trickster. The choice is locked at tier 1 and cannot be changed thereafter. The celestials present the paths as aspects of the mythic power the system was designed to bestow — the party is not choosing a class so much as discovering which facet of the power resonates with their nature.

### Interaction with the Song

The Song of Heaven and the mythic tier grants are thematically linked but mechanically independent. The Song opens the Road; the place of power grants the tier. Both happen at the same location and in the same scene, but a party that somehow accessed the place of power without the Song fragment would still receive the tier. This situation should not arise in normal play — it is noted here for completeness.

## Vessel of the Song

Death in **Rainbow Road** is permanent. There is no resurrection magic that can restore a fallen PC to life. The mythic power the party carries is too deeply woven into the fabric of the celestial system to permit the ordinary reversal of death — the system was designed to produce champions who face genuine mortal risk, and the power respects that design.

However, the system also cannot afford to lose a champion mid-journey at this point of crisis, and the Host of Heaven will intervene in an unexpected way. When a PC dies, the mythic power they carried — including the Song of Heaven fragments inscribed in their consciousness — seeks a new vessel. Within hours of the PC's death, that power kindles in a mortal somewhere on the world the party currently occupies. A new champion is born.

### What Happens at the Table

When a PC dies, the player creates a new character. The current encounter should be resolved before the new character is introduced. After the encounter, the surviving party members become aware that the fallen PC's mythic power has manifested in someone nearby — they sense a direction and a rough distance, an instinctive pull along the same mythic connection that binds them to the Song of Heaven and to each other.

The GM should create a brief narrative scene in which the party tracks down the new character. This person is a native of the current world — a Halfling farmer on Diaspola, a Dwarven wall-city engineer on Tanis, a Grey Elf scholar on Auzra — who has been suddenly and overwhelmingly suffused with mythic power they do not understand. The party finds them and recognizes what has happened, and the new character joins the group.

If circumstances prevent the party from breaking away to find their new companion — if they are imprisoned, in the middle of a dungeon, or otherwise unable to travel — the GM should use whatever means are appropriate to bring the new character to them, up to and including direct divine intervention by the celestials. The Song must continue and the system will ensure that it does.

### The New Character

The new character is built from scratch. The player chooses any race drawn from the current world's population, any character class or multiclass combination, and their own mythic path and advancement. The new character enters play at the same character level and mythic tier as the rest of the party.

The new character's background, personality, and life are entirely their own. They were a person before the mythic power found them, and they remain that person — augmented, changed, but not overwritten. This also means the new character can provide specific knowledge about their home world and culture that the party would not otherwise have access to, which may prove valuable to the current chapter's investigation and turning efforts.

Any surviving gear belonging to the fallen character may be used by the new character with the permission of the other PCs. Alternatively, items from the fallen character's inventory may be converted to similar items suitable for the new character's build, provided the replacement does not exceed the gold piece value of the original item.

### Inherited Memories

The new character carries the memories of the fallen PC — and of every prior incarnation, if the power has passed through multiple vessels during the campaign. These memories are not the new character's own. They are accessible but not automatic: they surface when triggered by something familiar or significant. A loved one's face, a favorite meal, a battlefield the original PC fought on, the sound of a specific Song fragment — these moments open a window into the previous life. The new character understands that what they are experiencing belongs to someone else. They can choose to open themselves to the memories or hold them at a distance.

The memories do not overwrite or merge with the new character's own experiences. The new character remembers their childhood, their family, their life before the power arrived. They also have access to the full knowledge of their predecessor — tactical information, relationships with NPCs, the layout of places previously visited, and critically, the Song of Heaven fragments. The Song persists across vessels because it is mythic knowledge, not a personal memory, but the inherited memories provide the context that gives the Song meaning.

If a player's character has been replaced multiple times, the current vessel carries the layered memories of every previous incarnation. Each set of memories is distinct — the new character can distinguish between them — and each is triggered independently by its own associations. The effect is cumulative but not overwhelming; the mythic power that enables the transfer also provides the capacity to bear it.

### Mythic Immortality at Higher Tiers

This mechanic is designed for PCs below mythic tier 9. At tier 9, PCs gain the Immortal mythic ability: if killed, they return to life 24 hours later regardless of the condition of their body or the means of death, unless killed by a coup de grace or critical hit from a mythic creature or a weapon capable of bypassing epic damage reduction. At tier 10, PCs can only be killed by a coup de grace or critical hit made with an artifact. Characters at these tiers do not need the Vessel of the Song — they are effectively immortal by the power of the mythic system itself.

The exception, as always, is the Underworld. Death in the Underworld is permanent for all characters regardless of tier. The Immortal ability does not function there. The Vessel of the Song does not function there. The Underworld was built as a prison, and its walls do not permit escape — not for the demons, and not for the champions who enter to destroy them. This is the final risk the campaign demands, and no mechanic softens it.

### The Coda

After the final battle, if the party has survived the Descent, the celestials can separate the souls that share a vessel. This is offered during the epilogue as a gift — the gods acknowledge what was given and what was carried, and they undo the binding. The original character's soul passes on, carrying the mythic power with it. The new character — every new character, if there were multiple incarnations — returns to their own life as they were before the power found them, retaining the shared memories of their time as a vessel. From that point forward, every being who participated in the chain remembers what the others experienced, in the same triggered, associative manner described above. They are separate people who carry pieces of each other's lives.

This separation is for narrative closure only. By the time it occurs, the campaign is over and mechanical considerations are moot. What matters is the story: the farmer from Diaspola who carried a hero's memories for six worlds, the scholar from Auzra who picked up the Song mid-verse and sang it to the end, the original champion whose death on Tanis was not the end of their journey but the beginning of someone else's.

## The Corruption Mechanic

The corruption score is a hidden value tracked by the GM. It measures the cumulative cost of the party's choices across the eight outer worlds and determines two critical outcomes: the power of Tornsoul in the final encounter and the ending the campaign reaches.

### How It Works

The score starts at 0 and increases each time a Warlock is killed rather than turned. Casomir's death does not affect the score — his death is required, not a choice. Tornsoul's destruction is the campaign's objective and does not affect the score. The score is calculated from the eight outer-world Warlocks only.

Not all Warlocks carry equal weight. The corruption score uses a weighted scale that reflects the difficulty of each turning, the narrative consequences of failure, and the cascading dependencies between worlds.

### Corruption Values

| Warlock | World | Killed | Turned | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sona (Snow Queen) | Diaspola | +1 | 0 | First world — lever is accessible, turning is straightforward |
| Garl (Howler) | Tanis | +1 | 0 | Turning results in mercy killing — Garl dies either way |
| Tana (Gray Eminence) | Auzra | +2 | 0 | Spellbook is difficult to find; Tana's knowledge is valuable to later worlds |
| Garm (Trademaster) | Arlune | +1 | 0 | Garm is not fully corrupted; killing him is harder to justify |
| Kerlin (Blackfire) | Vatra | +2 | 0 | Renara's Blade is well hidden; Vatra's population is desperate |
| Tamaron (the Just) | Illium | +3 | 0 | Requires mindscape battle; failure cascades to Nocturne |
| Baselia (Lady Sorrow) | Nocturne | +2 | 0 | Requires Tamaron turned; unturnable if Tamaron killed |
| Ourania (Overmind) | Candela | +2 | 0 | Most knowledge-dependent turning; requires insanity cure |

**Maximum corruption:** 14 (all eight killed).
**Minimum corruption:** 0 (all eight turned).

### Cascading Failures

The Illium-Nocturne dependency is the most significant cascade in the campaign. If Tamaron is killed on Illium, Baselia cannot be turned on Nocturne — the party must fight her. This produces a combined corruption of +5 from two worlds (Tamaron's +3 plus Baselia's +2), the single largest swing possible from a single decision. The GM should not telegraph this dependency overtly, but clues should be available on Illium for observant parties — Tamaron's connection to Baselia is mentioned in rebel archives, in his own ramblings, and in the lore available from turned Warlocks on earlier worlds.

Candela's turning is similarly dependent on accumulated knowledge. Ourania's lever requires knowing where her personal sphere is (information available from any previously turned Warlock), possessing Casomir's key (recoverable from his study on Nexus, though he cannot tell the party about it directly), and curing her insanity after breaking the interface. A party that has turned no prior Warlocks will find Ourania's turning extremely difficult, though not impossible — the information exists in other forms for those who search thoroughly.

### Corruption Thresholds

| Corruption | Ending Available | Tornsoul's Power |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | Ending 1: The Worlds Reborn | Significantly diminished — stripped of most borrowed mythic portions |
| 4–7 | Ending 2: The Long Dawn | Weakened but dangerous — retains some borrowed strength |
| 8–11 | Ending 3: The Price of Victory | Near full strength — a brutal, costly fight |
| 12–14 | Ending 4: The Silence | Full power — TPK is a real possibility |

### Mechanical Translation

Tornsoul's stat block in the Stat Block Appendix is presented at full power (corruption 14). For each point of corruption below 14, reduce her as follows:

- **Hit points:** Reduce maximum HP by 5% per point below 14 (e.g., corruption 0 = 30% fewer HP than full power).
- **Mythic abilities:** For every 3 full points below 14, remove one of her mythic abilities from the encounter, starting with the most powerful. At corruption 0, she loses four abilities; at corruption 14, she retains all of them.
- **Eidolon strength:** The merged Eidolon's damage output scales with corruption. At corruption 0, the Eidolon deals half damage. At corruption 14, full damage.
- **Legendary actions:** At corruption 8 or below, reduce her legendary actions by 1. At corruption 4 or below, reduce by 2.

These reductions should be applied before the session. The party should never see the full-power stat block unless they have earned it.

## The Warlock Turning Framework

Turning a Warlock follows a universal structure, though the specifics vary dramatically from world to world. Every turning requires three elements: **discovery** of the lever, **acquisition** of the lever, and **presentation** of the lever to the Warlock at the right moment.

### Discovery

The party must learn that the lever exists and what it is. On some worlds, this information is available locally — NPCs, historical records, environmental clues. On others, it requires foreknowledge from earlier worlds. Turned Warlocks are the richest source of cross-world information: each knows details about the other heroes that can point the party toward levers on subsequent worlds. The GM should track which Warlocks have been turned and what information each can provide if asked.

### Acquisition

The party must physically obtain or arrange access to the lever. This is the most variable element — the Ivory Music Box is hidden on a remote island guarded by fey, the Rerox figurine is buried in a ruined childhood home in the Contamination zone, Renara's Blade is locked in a vault guarded by a dragon construct. Acquisition is typically the most dangerous part of the turning process and is where most of the per-world adventure content concentrates.

### Presentation

The party must bring the lever to the Warlock and present it in a way that allows the lever to work. For most Warlocks, this means a direct confrontation — approaching the Warlock with the lever in hand and creating the conditions for the emotional or spiritual breakthrough that the lever enables. For Tamaron, presentation involves a mindscape battle against the demon possessing him. For Garm, it involves earning his trust over the course of multiple tasks before the coin is even relevant. For Ourania, it involves reading poetry aloud to a woman fused to a machine.

### What Turning Looks Like

When a Warlock is turned, the corruption breaks. The specific manifestation varies — Sona's ice shatters, Garl finds peace and asks for death, Tana remembers who they were — but the mechanical result is the same: the Warlock renounces their mythic power, and that power transfers to the world's place of power, where it becomes available to the party. The Warlock survives the turning (except Garl, whose wound kills him) and begins the work of rebuilding their world. Turned Warlocks are available as allies in the Battle of Nexus.

### What Killing Looks Like

When the party kills a Warlock, the Warlock's mythic power dissipates rather than transferring cleanly. A portion of it feeds Tornsoul in the Underworld, strengthening her for the final encounter. The place of power still grants the party their mythic tier, but the world suffers — without the Warlock's knowledge and authority, the local population must rebuild from a deeper deficit, and the world's contribution to the allied forces in Phase 3 is diminished or eliminated.

## The Ally-Tracking System

Each of the eight outer worlds can contribute allied forces to the Battle of Nexus. The strength of each contribution is measured on a 0–3 point scale, producing a maximum possible total of 24 ally points. The GM tracks this total on a simple tally as the party completes each world.

### Point Awards Per World

| Points | Condition |
|---|---|
| 3 | Warlock turned + world-specific side quest completed |
| 2 | Warlock turned |
| 1 | Warlock killed, world left stable |
| 0 | Warlock killed, world left destabilized |

**Turned** means the Warlock survived and is actively rebuilding. The turned Warlock contributes their personal strength, their followers, and whatever military resources the world can muster.

**Killed, stable** means the Warlock was destroyed but the party's other actions on the world left the population in a position to organize — key NPCs survived, infrastructure was preserved, or the party took deliberate steps to ensure stability before departing.

**Killed, destabilized** means the Warlock's death left a power vacuum that the world cannot recover from in the time available. This is the default outcome when a Warlock is killed without significant effort to support the local population. The world sends nothing to the Battle of Nexus.

### World-Specific Side Quests

Each world contains one significant side quest beyond the Warlock confrontation. Completing the side quest adds 1 point to that world's contribution if the Warlock was also turned (for a total of 3). If the Warlock was killed, the side quest does not add a point — the population cannot organize effectively without the Warlock's leadership, regardless of what else the party accomplished. Side quests are identified in each world chapter.

### Ally Strength Tiers

| Total Points | Tier | Battle of Nexus Impact |
|---|---|---|
| 19–24 | Full Strength | Decisive allied advantage. The PCs fight spotlight encounters while allies hold the line. Multiple turned Warlocks fight alongside the party. The city survives with minimal damage. |
| 13–18 | Strong | Hard-fought victory. Allies hold most positions but need PC intervention at critical moments. Some losses. The city survives but is damaged. |
| 7–12 | Weak | Desperate battle. Allies are stretched thin. The PCs must handle multiple crises simultaneously. Significant losses. The city's survival is uncertain. |
| 0–6 | Minimal | Near-hopeless. Few or no allies. The PCs bear the full weight. Mass casualties. The city may fall. |

### Rally the Allies

In Chapter 12, before the Battle of Nexus begins, the party has the opportunity to travel the now-open Rainbow Road to each liberated world and request allied support. This is a narrative scene, not a mechanical one — the party visits the worlds they saved, sees the consequences of their choices, and gathers their forces. Worlds where the Warlock was turned respond immediately and with strength. Worlds where the Warlock was killed may offer token support, resistance, or nothing at all. The rally sequence is the party's final accounting of their decisions across eight worlds, and it should be given appropriate weight at the table.

---

## The Battle of Nexus

The Battle of Nexus is the campaign's penultimate confrontation — a demon army assaulting the city while the party and their allies attempt to hold it. The battle uses an abstracted narrative system rather than mass combat rules. The PCs fight a series of dramatic encounters within the larger battle; the ally strength tier determines the conditions, scale, and consequences of those encounters.

### Structure

The battle is divided into three phases, each containing one or more PC-scale encounters set against the backdrop of the larger conflict.

**Phase 1: The Walls.** The demon army strikes the city's outer defenses. The PCs must hold a critical section of the wall — a gate, a breach, or a stretch of fortification that the allied forces cannot spare troops to defend. The encounter scales with ally strength: at Full Strength, the PCs face a manageable assault wave with allied support on their flanks; at Minimal, they face an overwhelming force alone while the walls crumble around them.

**Phase 2: The Crisis.** A demon general — a powerful Asura Rana — leads a strike force into the city's interior, targeting the Ancillary, the foundries, or another strategic location. The PCs must intercept and defeat this force. Ally strength determines whether the PCs have support (turned Warlocks, allied troops) or fight alone. The demon general's stat block is fixed — this encounter tests the party's combat ability at its current power level.

**Phase 3: The Turning Point.** The battle reaches its climax. At Full Strength or Strong, this is a decisive counterattack — the PCs lead a charge that breaks the demon line, supported by turned Warlocks and allied forces. At Weak or Minimal, this is a desperate last stand — the PCs must protect the civilian population while the remaining defenses collapse, buying time for an evacuation that may or may not succeed.

### Narrative Framing

Between encounters, the GM narrates the state of the larger battle. Allied forces engage demon formations in the streets. Buildings burn. The sky is dark with demonic flight. The PCs hear reports — this district has fallen, that allied force has broken through, the foundries are holding. The texture of these reports is determined by the ally strength tier. At Full Strength, the reports are encouraging with occasional setbacks. At Minimal, the reports are a litany of loss.

### Aftermath

The Battle of Nexus ends when the demon army is broken or the city falls. At all ally tiers except Minimal, the city survives — damaged, but standing. At Minimal, the city may fall, and the PCs must fight their way to the Ancillary through a ruined cityscape to begin the Descent. Level 20 is awarded on completion of the Battle regardless of outcome.

### Turned Warlocks in Battle

Each turned Warlock who participates in the battle is a significant combatant — a mythic-tier NPC fighting alongside the party. Their presence is felt narratively and mechanically: they anchor allied formations, contribute to key encounters, and provide the party with tactical options that are not available otherwise. A party that turned six or more Warlocks should feel the weight of those decisions paying off. The specific combat contributions of each turned Warlock are noted in their world chapters.

---

## Managing Multiple Endings

The campaign produces one of four endings, determined by the interaction of four axes. Three of these axes — corruption score, ally strength, and world restoration — are largely set by the time the party enters Chapter 13. The fourth — PC survival — is determined by what happens in the Underworld.

### The Four Axes

**Corruption Score (0–14).** Tracked throughout Phase 2. Determines Tornsoul's power and the primary ending threshold.

**Ally Strength (0–24 points).** Tracked throughout Phase 2 and finalized in Chapter 12. Determines the Battle of Nexus outcome and the state of the allied forces.

**World Restoration.** A qualitative assessment of the eight outer worlds' states after Phase 2. Worlds with turned Warlocks are rebuilding. Worlds with killed Warlocks are struggling. This axis does not have a numeric score — it is a narrative condition that shapes the epilogue.

**PC Survival.** Determined in the Underworld. Death in the Underworld is permanent — no resurrection. Whether all, some, or none of the PCs survive the Descent determines the emotional register of the ending.

### Ending Conditions

**Ending 1: The Worlds Reborn.** Corruption 0–3. Battle of Nexus won decisively (ally tier Full Strength or Strong). Most or all of the PCs survive the Underworld. Most or all worlds are rebuilding under turned Warlocks. The system works and the champions it was built to produce have arrived at last.

**Ending 2: The Long Dawn.** Corruption 4–7. Battle of Nexus won (any tier above Minimal). Tornsoul defeated. The threat is ended. The cost was real. Some worlds are rebuilding; others are leaderless and recovery will take generations.

**Ending 3: The Price of Victory.** Corruption 8–11. Battle of Nexus won at great cost. Tornsoul is defeated, possibly with multiple PC deaths. Most of the worlds are shattered. Victory is a fact, not a feeling.

**Ending 4: The Silence.** Corruption 12–14. Battle of Nexus barely survived or possibly lost, with the PCs falling back to the Ancilarry as a final gambit. With Tornsoul at full or near-full power a total party kill is a real possibility. If the PCs win, it costs them everything. If the PCs fall, the Nine Worlds fall with them, and the Song of Heaven is silenced.

### Writing the Epilogue

Each ending includes a full epilogue in Chapter 13 covering the fate of the Nine Worlds, the Rainbow Road, Heaven, the Underworld, and the surviving characters. The epilogue for each ending should be read or paraphrased to the players after the final encounter resolves. The GM should also address the specific state of each world the party visited, referencing the choices made and their consequences. A party that turned Sona and Tamaron but killed Kerlin and Baselia should hear about the frozen sea thawing on Diaspola and the church reforming on Illium, but also about the power vacuum on Vatra and the unbroken curse on Nocturne. A coda for each character may be appropriate.

## Managing the Revelation of Secrets

The campaign contains several truths that the party does not learn at the outset but must understand by the end. The pacing of these revelations is critical to the adventure's emotional arc. Reveal too much too early and the stakes flatten; reveal too little or too late and the party makes uninformed decisions on worlds where that information matters.

### The Major Secrets

**The true purpose of the Nine Worlds.** The worlds, the Rainbow Road, and the places of power were built as a system to find and empower mortal champions of good. No one now remembers this except the Warlocks. The party learns this progressively — hints from the celestials at the first meeting, fragments from turned Warlocks on the outer worlds, and with full clarity by the time they reach Candela or the final meeting with the gods.

**The Warlocks' origin.** The ten Warlocks were the last heroes to walk the Rainbow Road — the system's greatest success and its greatest failure — the party should learn this no later than the third or fourth world. Turned Warlocks are the primary source for this information. The Oracle's cryptic message in Chapter 2 contains a seed of this truth, though it will not be interpretable until later.

**Tornsoul and the demon army.** Tornsoul is directing the demon gating operation from the Underworld, and the progressive reopening of the Rainbow Road is enabling her to escalate. The party should become aware of the escalation by the fifth or sixth world (reports from Nexus, observations at the gate towns, intelligence from turned Warlocks). The full picture — that a demon army is massing for an assault on Nexus — should crystallize by the end of the Candela arc.

**The corruption's nature.** The mythic power the party carries is the same power that corrupted the Warlocks. The party is walking the same path. Whether this knowledge is delivered explicitly or left for the players to infer is a GM judgment call, but the parallel should be unmistakable by mid-campaign. Turned Warlocks will speak to it directly if asked.

### Pacing Guidance

Do not dump all secrets at once. The first celestial meeting should raise many questions, questions which cannont be formulated to ask or fully answered. Turned Warlocks should each contribute a piece of the puzzle — Sona knows about Khaga's manipulation and demonic cpooruption but not about Tornsoul; Garl knows the Warlocks' origin but not the system's purpose; Tamaron, if turned, understands the demon's long game. The party assembles the full picture from fragments, and the experience of that assembly is part of the campaign's reward.

If the party turns no Warlocks, they will have fewer sources of information. The Oracle's message, the celestials' oblique references, and environmental clues on each world must then carry more weight. The adventure does not break without turned Warlocks as informants — the information exists in other forms — but the party will arrive at the endgame with a less than complete understanding of what they are facing.

# CHAPTERS

The adventure is presented in thirteen chapters across three phases. Phase 1 (Chapters 1–3) takes place on Nexus and covers levels 1 through 4 and the first mythic tier. Phase 2 (Chapters 4–11) covers the eight outer worlds in fixed sequence, levels 5 through 19, and mythic tiers 2 through 9. Phase 3 (Chapters 12–13) covers the endgame — the Battle of Nexus, the final meeting with the celestials, and the Descent into the Underworld.

Each chapter begins with a summary and GM guidance, followed by scene-by-scene descriptions with read-aloud text, GM notes, encounter information, and skill check DCs. Read-aloud text is presented in blockquote format and describes what the characters perceive, not what they conclude. GM notes are presented in italics and address pacing, likely player divergences, and conditional outcomes.

---

## Phase 1: Nexus

*In which the PCs are drawn into the service of a dying king, discover the truth behind the demon raids, and are set on a path that will take them across every world in the system.*

The campaign opens on Nexus. The PCs are residents of the city — its largest and most diverse population center, sustained by magitek foundries and converted farmland districts, defended by Casomir Zukra's forces against the demon raids that have plagued the hub world for centuries. The PCs begin at level 1 and advance through three milestone events before leaving Nexus at level 4 with their first mythic tier. Phase 1 establishes the campaign's core elements: the magitek infrastructure, the demon threat, the Rainbow Road, Casomir Zukra as a leader at the end of his resources, and the mythic system that will define the party's journey through the eight worlds that follow.

---

## Chapter 1: The Foundry Crisis *(Levels 1–2)*

### Chapter Summary

A magitek foundry critical to the city's survival is malfunctioning. Casomir's defense forces are stretched thin dealing with the latest round of demon raids, and he needs capable people to investigate what his soldiers cannot spare the numbers to handle. The PCs are recruited — whether through direct assignment, recommendation, or circumstance — and discover that demons have infiltrated the foundry, sabotaging the machinery that keeps the city armed and fed. The chapter introduces the campaign's core elements in microcosm: the magitek infrastructure on which civilization depends, the demon threat that has been grinding it down for centuries, and Casomir Zukra as a ruler who is stretched past his limits. The PCs' success brings them to Casomir's direct attention. Level 2 is awarded on completion.

### GM Guidance

This chapter is an introduction. Its purpose is to orient the players in the world — the feel of Nexus, the rhythms of daily life, the presence of demons as a persistent rather than apocalyptic threat, and the centrality of magitek to everything. Don't rush it — the party should leave this chapter understanding that this is a city that works, held together by infrastructure and will, and that the demons are eroding both. The foundry is one extremely important piece of that infrastructure. Its loss would be a problem, the pattern its loss represents represents an ongoing catastrophe.

The chapter is designed for level 1 characters and should be played accordingly. The encounters are calibrated for a party with no mythic power and limited resources. The demons in the foundry are minor — individually manageable, dangerous in numbers, and evidence of a larger pattern the party does not yet understand.

*If the party is entirely new to Pathfinder or to the campaign setting, extend Scene 1 to allow more time in the city before the foundry assignment. Use the briefing scene to establish relationships with NPCs who will recur — Maranthe Soldati (Casomir's seneschal), the foundry foreman, members of the city watch — and give the players time to inhabit the world before the adventure demands that they act in it.*

### Scene 1: The City of Nexus

The chapter opens with the party in the city. How the PCs know each other and why they are available for Casomir's assignment is left to the GM and the players — the party may be members of the city watch, mercenaries looking for work, skilled tradespeople whose expertise is relevant to magitek, or simply residents in the right place at the right time. The campaign does not prescribe a single hook.

> *The city of Nexus wakes to the sound of hammers. The foundry quarter, three blocks west, is already at work — the dull rhythm of metal on metal carries over the rooftops and settles into the background of every morning like a second heartbeat. The streets fill as they always do: farmers heading to the converted agricultural districts with their tools, watchmen changing shifts at the district gates and on the walls, a cluster of children being hustled toward a schoolhouse by a woman who has clearly done this before and has opinions about the pace. The air smells of bread from the bakery on the corner and, underneath it, the faint chemical tang of magitek fabrication that never quite leaves the western quarter. A patrol of the king's soldiers passes at a measured clip, armored and armed, the gleaming magitek rifles standing out in the morning sun, heading toward the outer wall. No one looks twice. This is a city that has been at war for longer than anyone alive can remember, and the war has become furniture.*

*Let the players spend time here. If they want to explore, the city offers what a hub world's capital should: markets where goods from the converted farmlands trade alongside salvaged pre-war artifacts, taverns where the regular topic of conversation is the last demon raid and the next one, a shrine to the Host of Heaven that is more habit than devotion for most of its visitors. The players should feel the city as a functional place before the adventure asks them to defend it.*

The assignment arrives through whatever channel the GM has established — a summons from the watch captain, a direct approach from Casomir's seneschal, a posting on the mercenary board outside the foundry quarter. The message is the same regardless of delivery: Foundry Seven, the largest magitek fabrication facility in the western quarter, has been producing defective output for three days. The foreman reports equipment malfunctions that his people cannot explain. Casomir's soldiers are deployed on the outer wall handling demon activity and cannot be spared. Someone needs to investigate.

*GM note: Foundry Seven produces the alchemical ammunition and magitek weapon components that the city's defense force depends on. Three days of defective output has not yet created a crisis, but two more will. The foreman's report is accurate as far as it goes — the equipment is malfunctioning — but the cause is not mechanical.*

### Scene 2: Foundry Seven

The party arrives at Foundry Seven to find a facility in visible distress. The foreman — a veteran magitek engineer named **Tolun** — meets them at the main gate and walks them through the problem.

> *Foundry Seven is a large, low building of reinforced stone, its roof studded with ventilation stacks that pulse with the blue-white glow of active magitek. The main floor is a controlled chaos of workstations, conveyor systems, and fabrication chambers connected by an overhead network of pipes and conduits that carries alchemical reagents between stages of the production process. Half the stations are dark. The workers at the remaining stations move with the tense efficiency of people who know something is wrong and are doing their best to work around it.*
>
> *Tolun is a broad, square-built man whose hands are stained with reagent residue to the wrist. He does not waste time on pleasantries. "Three days ago the calibration systems on the eastern wing started drifting," he says, leading you through the main floor. "Thought it was a coolant leak — happens sometimes when the conduits age. Replaced the conduits. Drift got worse. Yesterday the primary forge in Chamber Four shut down entirely. I've had my best people on it. They can't find the fault." He stops at the entrance to the eastern wing. The lights here flicker at irregular intervals. "This morning two of my workers reported hearing sounds in the sub-basement. Scratching. I don't send people into places that scratch."*

*Let the party investigate. Foundry Seven has three areas of interest:*

**The Eastern Wing** contains the malfunctioning calibration systems. A successful Knowledge (Engineering) or Knowledge (Arcana) check (DC 14) reveals that the malfunctions are not mechanical — the magitek circuitry has been physically interfered with, connections loosened and reagent lines redirected in ways that degrade output without producing obvious visible damage. Someone — or something — has been sabotaging the foundry from inside.

**Chamber Four** is the primary forge, currently dark. Entering the chamber reveals scorch marks on the interior walls that are inconsistent with normal forge operation — they radiate outward from a point near the floor, not from the forge itself. A Perception check (DC 12) identifies claw marks on the underside of the forge housing. Something came up from below.

**The Sub-Basement** is accessible through a maintenance hatch in Chamber Four. The sub-basement is a low-ceilinged crawlspace that runs beneath the foundry's eastern wing, housing the conduit network that connects the calibration systems to the main reagent supply. It is dark, tight, and designed for maintenance workers, not combat.

*GM note: The sub-basement is where the encounter happens. The party should arrive here having established through investigation that the foundry's problems are caused by deliberate sabotage from below. How much of that they piece together before descending depends on their skill checks and investigation approach — do not block progress behind checks. If the party fails every check, Tolun can provide the key observation: "Whatever is wrong, it's coming from underneath."*

### Scene 3: The Demons Below

The sub-basement contains two types of asura. A handful of **phankhonvrani** — the lowest-tier combat asura, known colloquially as **demonites** — have nested in the access tunnel near the fissure, drawn by the residual magical charge in the conduit network. They are fast, wiry creatures with mottled gray skin, a blade grafted to one arm, and the ability to mimic non-speech sounds — they have been reproducing the groan of shifting pipes and the whine of overloaded conduits to mask their movements. Above them, closer to the foundry machinery, a small group of **tripurasuras** — tiny, shapeshifting asuras — have been conducting the actual sabotage, methodically degrading the magitek systems from within. The tripurasuras entered through a crack in the sub-basement floor where the stone has been weakened by centuries of magitek resonance. The crack is not a portal; it is a physical fissure that the asuras have widened over the course of several days, tunneling up from a natural cavity beneath the city. The phankhonvrani followed them up. As with all the creatures the people of the Nine Worlds call demons, these are technically asuras — fiends born of divine error, drawn to the works of the gods and their servants in order to unmake them. The tripurasuras' innate shapeshifting and nondetection aura allowed them to operate unnoticed for days; the phankhonvrani, less subtle, stayed deeper in the tunnel and fed on scraps of magical residue.

The people of Nexus have a colloquial name for these small saboteur-demons: **gremlins**. The term is old — it predates the apocalypse — and is used loosely for any small demon that gets into the machinery and breaks things. Foundry workers treat gremlin infestations the way a farmer treats rats: a persistent nuisance, occasionally dangerous, and a sign that something larger may be wrong. The term is imprecise and covers several distinct types of minor asura, but the tripurasura is the most common.

> *The maintenance hatch opens onto a ladder descending eight feet into a crawlspace barely tall enough to stand in. The air is different down here — warm and wet, carrying a sulfurous undertone that is not part of the foundry's normal chemical palette. The conduit network runs along the ceiling, a lattice of pipes and cable bundles that hum with magitek energy. Several conduits have been pulled loose from their housings, their exposed ends sparking intermittently and casting brief flashes of blue-white light across the space. In the far corner, where the stone floor has cracked and buckled upward, something moves — small, quick, and wrong in a way that registers before the eye can fully resolve it.*

**Encounter: Gremlin Infestation**

This encounter plays out in two stages as the party pushes deeper into the sub-basement.

**Stage 1: The Demonites**

As the party moves through the access tunnel toward the sub-basement proper, they encounter the phankhonvrani. The creatures are using Distress Mimicry to reproduce the sounds of groaning pipes and sparking conduits — a Perception check (DC 12) notices that the sounds are coming from the wrong locations, slightly off-rhythm with the actual machinery. If the party approaches without detecting the mimicry, the phankhonvrani attack from ambush.

- **Enemies:** 3 **phankhonvrani** (CR 1 each; see Stat Block Appendix). Small outsiders (asura, evil, extraplanar, lawful). AC 15, 13 HP each, fast healing 1, DR 2/cold iron or good. Speed 40 ft. Blade arm +5 (1d6+1/19–20), bite +0 (1d4).
- **Tactics:** The phankhonvrani fight as a pack. They attempt to remain within 30 ft. of each other to maintain their Cacophony ability (Will DC 12 or shaken for 1d4 rounds). They use their speed to dart in, strike with their blade arms, and withdraw — they are not strong enough to stand and trade blows with armed adventurers. If two are killed, the survivor flees toward the fissure, shrieking — this alerts the tripurasuras in the sub-basement proper, who prepare an ambush.
- **GM note:** The phankhonvrani are the party's first combat encounter with asuras and should feel like a scrappy, chaotic fight against fast-moving vermin. The Cacophony ability — if it triggers — introduces the idea that demons become more dangerous in groups, a principle that scales throughout the campaign. The shaken condition is minor but meaningful for level 1 characters about to face a harder fight in the next room.

**Stage 2: The Gremlins**

Beyond the access tunnel, the sub-basement opens into the crawlspace beneath the foundry's eastern wing. This is where the tripurasuras have been conducting the actual sabotage.

- **Enemies:** 4 **tripurasuras** (CR 2 each; Pathfinder RPG Bestiary 3). Tiny outsiders (asura, evil, extraplanar, lawful). AC 16, 19 HP each, fast healing 2, DR 5/cold iron or good. Their elusive ability grants a constant *nondetection* effect (CL check DC 14 to penetrate). In the confined space of the sub-basement, the tripurasuras use the conduit network as a three-dimensional fighting space — they are Tiny and can move freely through gaps that Medium creatures cannot, climbing pipes and dropping behind the party.
- **Terrain:** The sub-basement is 5 feet high, 40 feet long, and 20 feet wide. Medium creatures fight without penalty; Large creatures cannot enter. The conduit network along the ceiling provides partial cover (+2 AC) to creatures adjacent to it. The exposed conduit ends deal 1d4 electricity damage to any creature that touches them (Reflex DC 12 to avoid when moving through the space). The tripurasuras are Tiny and treat the conduit network as open terrain, moving through it without penalty.
- **Tactics:** The tripurasuras are cunning and coordinated, communicating via telepathy. They use their small size and the conduit network to stay out of melee reach, striking with their poisoned stingers (1d4-1 plus Wisdom-damaging poison, Fort DC 12) before retreating into gaps between pipes. If cornered, they use *change shape* to assume the appearance of small humanoids — a gnome child, a halfling — and attempt to talk their way out, feigning innocence or pleading. This deception is their nature: tripurasuras are infiltrators and corruptors, and they will lie as naturally as they breathe. If two are killed, the survivors attempt to flee through the fissure. If the phankhonvrani alerted them, the tripurasuras have positioned themselves in the conduit network above the entrance and attack with surprise (Stealth +14 vs. party Perception) when the party enters.
- **The Fissure:** A jagged crack in the stone floor roughly 3 feet wide and 10 feet long. It descends into a natural cavity below the city's foundations. The tripurasuras came up through this opening, and the phankhonvrani followed. It is too narrow for most Medium creatures to descend without squeezing (Tiny creatures pass freely), and the cavity below connects to a network of natural tunnels that extend toward the outer wall. The party cannot pursue the asuras through this network at their current level — this is noted for completeness and for GMs who want to foreshadow the larger demon infrastructure beneath the city.
- **Treasure:** The tripurasuras have accumulated a small cache of stolen components in a nook near the fissure — magitek parts worth 50 gp total if returned to Tolun, plus a single *potion of cure light wounds* that one of the asuras was hoarding.

*GM note: The two-stage encounter introduces the party to two distinct asura types and teaches them that demons operate at different levels of sophistication. The phankhonvrani are pack hunters — fast, vicious, and dangerous in numbers but limited in cunning. The tripurasuras are infiltrators — physically weaker but far more intelligent, using shapeshifting and deception as naturally as breathing. This contrast establishes a pattern that scales throughout the campaign: the asura hierarchy is real, and the more dangerous creatures are not necessarily the biggest ones. After the encounter, the party should have enough information to report back to Casomir's people. The foundry was sabotaged by gremlins who entered through a natural fissure beneath the building, supported by demonites that nested in the access tunnel. The fissure suggests a larger subterranean network — a detail that should concern whoever receives the report, because it means the demons have been probing the city's foundations. This is not a one-time infiltration; it is a pattern.*

### Scene 4: Reporting to Casomir

The party's success at Foundry Seven earns them a direct audience with **Casomir Zukra**. This is the first time the PCs meet the king, and the scene should establish him as the person described in his NPC profile: competent, honest, tired in a way that goes beyond the physical, and carrying something he does not intend to share.

> *The citadel's working chamber is not what the word "throne room" conjures. It is a practical space — stone walls lined with maps, a broad table covered in reports and logistics documents, chairs that have been sat in hard and often. The man standing behind the table looks up when you enter and gives you his full attention in a way that is both flattering and slightly unsettling, as though he is reading something in you that you did not know was written there.*
>
> *Casomir Zukra is not what five hundred years of kingship should look like. He is vital, sharp-eyed, and clearly present — but there is a quality to his stillness that suggests it is maintained rather than natural, a man who is very carefully not doing something. "You cleared the foundry," he says. It is not a question. "Tell me what you found."*

*Let the party deliver their report. Casomir listens carefully and asks precise follow-up questions — he is interested in the fissure, in the demons' behavior, in whether the party observed any coordination or simply instinctive sabotage. He is not testing them; he is gathering intelligence. When the report is complete, he thanks them directly and without ceremony.*

*If the party asks about the subterranean network or whether similar infiltrations have happened before: Casomir confirms that demon probing of the city's foundations has been reported before, but never this deep inside the walls. The fissure beneath Foundry Seven represents an escalation. He is concerned. He does not explain why — the connection to Beast Blavin's gating is not something he shares with anyone.*

*If the party asks about Casomir himself — his age, how he has ruled for five centuries, the nature of the Beast: he answers the first two questions honestly and deflects the third. He is aware of Beast Blavin. He has it under control. The subject is closed. Players who press (Sense Motive DC 20) detect that the deflection is practiced and the man behind it is deeply uncomfortable, but they cannot determine why.*

**What Casomir is actually doing in this scene:** He is evaluating the PCs. The foundry assignment was not random — Casomir has been watching for capable individuals who might be the heroes the Oracle once described to him. The party's success confirms that they are worth further investment, though not yet the ultimate test. He does not reveal this. He thanks them, pays them fairly, and tells them he will have further work.

**Milestone:** Level 2 is awarded at the conclusion of this scene.

### The Months Between

What follows is a period of approximately six months during which the party serves as part of Casomir's inner circle of operatives. This period is not played scene by scene — it is narrated, with the GM establishing its shape through summary, selected vignettes, and the accumulation of small details that build the relationship between the party and the king.

The GM should convey the following through narration and, where time permits, brief roleplayed moments:

**The work.** Casomir puts the party to use. After the foundry, there are other assignments — a series of escalating tasks that take the party across the city and into the surrounding territory. Demon probing of the walls. A magitek relay station that has gone dark. A dispute in the converted farmland districts that turns violent when someone discovers a gremlin nest beneath the granary. Rumors of a coordinated demon movement near one of the gate towns. None of these are trivial, and several are dangerous enough to test the party's growing capabilities. The work is real, and Casomir does not invent it to keep them busy — Nexus has no shortage of crises. But he is watching how they handle each one, and his assessment sharpens with every report.

**The relationship.** Over six months, the party's interactions with Casomir shift. The early meetings are formal — briefings in the working chamber, reports delivered and received, payment and dismissal. By the third month, the formality has softened. Casomir begins asking the party's opinions on tactical matters. He remembers details from their reports and references them weeks later. He shares meals with them when the work runs late — simple food, eaten standing over maps, the conversation drifting from the current assignment to the state of the city, the history of the walls, the engineering problems of the foundries. He does not talk about himself, and he deflects personal questions with the same practiced ease the party observed in their first meeting. But his trust in their competence is evident, and the trust is mutual. By the sixth month, the party has seen Casomir under pressure, seen him make hard decisions with imperfect information, seen him grieve quietly when an operation costs lives he could not afford to lose. They know the man. Not the secret — never the secret — but the man.

**The city.** Six months in Nexus deepend the party's roots to their home. They know the layout of the streets better than before, they've learned the names of the watch captains, the rhythms of setting up the full-moon barricades. Perhaps they have favorite taverns, those frequented more by the military than the civial population. They have earned enemies among the demons who have learned to recognize them. They have relationships with NPCs the GM introduces during this period — soldiers, foundry workers, merchants, rangers and scouts they may encounter when working outside of the city. Maranthe Soldati becomes a familiar presence: the half-orc seneschal who handles the logistics of their assignments, who always knows where Casomir is and what he needs before he asks, who treats the party with the gruff professional respect of someone who has watched them earn their keep. The city is not a backdrop. It is home. This is essential — when the party fights to defend Nexus in Chapter 12, they should are fighting for a place they know and people they care about, not only because of the experience of their earlier lives, but because they have shed blood and made real sacrifices for their city.

**The Beast.** During these six months, the party witnesses at least two full-moon barricade nights from a military perspective inside the city, not from behind baracaded doors and windows as in their former lives. They hear the Beast — the distant sounds of something enormous moving through the streets, the crash of masonry, the screams that stop suddenly. They see a fuller picutre of the aftermath: the damaged buildings, the blood, the grim efficiency of the cleanup crews. They may hear rumors — that the Beast has been seen more often lately, that the intervals between appearances are shortening, that the alchemists who supply Casomir's "hunting trips" have been working longer hours. The GM should plant these details without emphasis, trusting the players to connect them retroactively after Casomir's revelation in Chapter 3.

> **GM note on pacing:** This narrative interlude can be as brief or as detailed as the table requires. Some groups will want to play through selected assignments — the relay station, the granary, a particularly dangerous wall patrol — as full scenes with encounters and skill challenges. Others will prefer a montage: a few minutes of narrated summary, a handful of character moments, and a transition to Chapter 2. Either approach works. What matters is that the party reaches Chapter 2 feeling that they know Casomir, that they have earned his trust, and that the city of Nexus is a place worth defending. If the GM chooses to run selected assignments as full scenes, these should be designed to award the party additional gear and connections rather than additional levels — level 2 is the milestone for completing the foundry, and level 3 comes from the Oracle. The six months of service fills the narrative space between those milestones without accelerating the mechanical progression.

> **Optional: The Barricade Night.** If the GM wants to give the party a direct encounter with the Beast's aftermath (not the Beast itself), consider running one full-moon barricade night as a scene. The party is deployed with a watch unit to hold a section of the inner perimeter. They hear the Beast in an adjacent district. Something crashes through a wall three streets over. The watch sergeant orders them to hold position — under no circumstances do they pursue. The party spends the night listening to the city being savaged by something they cannot see and are not permitted to fight. In the morning, they help with the cleanup. This scene costs nothing mechanically but pays enormous dividends in Chapter 3, when the party learns that the thing they spent a night hiding from is the man they have been working for.

## Chapter 2: The Oracle's Prison *(Levels 2–3)*

### Chapter Summary

After six months of service, Casomir has seen enough. The party has proven themselves capable, reliable, and — most importantly — possessed of the judgment and resilience that distinguish effective operatives from merely competent ones. He entrusts them with a far more dangerous assignment. He believes that a demonic leader has taken the Nexian Oracle hostage in her sanctum east of the city — an ancient being who once served as the arbiter of worthiness to walk the Rainbow Road. He needs to know if she still lives, and he has a question only she can answer. The party must cross demon-infested territory to reach the sanctum, fight through the forces occupying it, and speak with the Oracle herself.

The reality is more complex than Casomir understands. The demons were sent by Tornsoul to block access to the Oracle, but they are not holding her hostage — they are terrified of her. The Oracle is the last functioning piece of the celestial system's arbiter mechanism, and her power is anathema to the asuras. They have surrounded her sanctum and sealed the approaches not to contain her, but to prevent anyone from reaching her. Their leader, a powerful asura, has established a command post in the ruins of a village on the approach road and maintains a perimeter of lesser asuras around the sanctum itself.

When the party reaches the Oracle, she delivers a cryptic message — layered, compressed, and only partially interpretable on first hearing. What Casomir hears in this message is the answer to the question he has carried for five hundred years: whether he and the Beast can ever be separated. The Oracle's answer is no. This is the trigger that sets Casomir's final plan in motion. Level 3 is awarded after the Oracle meeting.

### GM Guidance

This chapter is the campaign's first genuine test. The foundry was an introduction; the Oracle's Prison is a mission with real stakes, real danger, and a payoff that reshapes the adventure's trajectory. The territory between the city and the sanctum should feel hostile — not a dungeon, but a landscape that has been claimed by something that does not want the party there. The asuras are organized, watchful, and operating with a purpose the party does not initially understand.

The Oracle scene is the chapter's emotional center. Everything before it — the journey, the fights, the ruined village — is preamble. When the party stands before the Oracle and receives her message, they should feel the weight of the moment even if they cannot fully parse what she tells them. Write the Oracle's message before the session and seed it with at least three pieces of information: one relevant to Casomir's death in Chapter 3, one relevant to a specific Warlock turning later in the campaign, and one relevant to the endgame. The party should not be able to decode all of these on first hearing. They should have the experience of remembering her words at the exact moment one of them suddenly makes sense, worlds later.

*If the party is cautious about leaving the city:* Casomir provides a military escort to the city walls and arranges for the watch to cover their return. He does not minimize the danger — he tells them plainly that the territory between the walls and the sanctum is some of the most dangerous ground on the continent, and that previous attempts to reach the Oracle have failed. He is asking them because the soldiers he has sent before have not come back.

### Scene 1: The Briefing

Casomir summons the party to his working chamber. After six months, they know this room as well as any in the city — but the atmosphere today is different. He is more direct than usual, and the directness has an edge to it that the party has learned to associate with assignments he considers genuinely dangerous.

> *The working chamber is familiar by now — you have delivered reports across this table dozens of times, shared meals here when assignments ran late, argued over tactical maps while Casomir listened with the focused attention that makes you feel simultaneously valued and transparent. But the maps on the table have changed. The city logistics documents are gone, replaced by a single large chart of the territory east of the walls — the farmlands, the abandoned villages, the road that runs toward the distant gate town, home to the old gate terminus leading to the world of Diaspola, if the legends are true. Red marks cluster along the road and around a structure at its far end, two days' travel from the city. Casomir stands behind the table, and his expression is one you have learned to read over six months of service: he is about to ask you to do something he would not ask of anyone he trusted less.*
>
> *"East of the city, two days out on the old Diaspola road, there is a structure called the Oracle's Sanctum," he says. "An ancient being resides there — the Nexian Oracle. She has been there for as long as anyone remembers. I need to know if she is still alive, and I need to ask her a question." He taps one of the red marks. "The demons have been concentrating in the territory between the walls and the sanctum for years. They have killed every patrol I have sent. The last one was eight soldiers. Reports say they made it to within a day's march of the sanctum before they were overwhelmed."*
>
> *He looks at you. "I am asking you because eight soldiers were not enough, and I cannot spare eighty. What I can spare is a group of people who have already demonstrated that they can handle demons in tight spaces with limited support. The question I need to ask the Oracle is personal. I will provide it in a sealed letter. You do not need to read it — she will know what it says."*

*If the party asks what the question is: Casomir declines to answer. His expression suggests this is not a test of their loyalty but a matter he genuinely cannot bring himself to discuss. Players who press (Sense Motive DC 15) understand that the question is not strategic — it is personal and painful.*

*If the party asks why the Oracle matters: Casomir explains that in ages past, the Oracle served as the arbiter of worthiness for those who would walk the Rainbow Road. She confirmed heroes before they began their journey. He believes she may still serve that function, and he has reason to think the demons agree — their concentration around the sanctum suggests they are trying to prevent anyone from reaching her.*

*Casomir provides: the sealed letter, a rough map of the approach road with known demon positions marked (though he warns it is months out of date), and supplies sufficient for five days — two out, one at the sanctum, two back. He does not provide additional soldiers — he does not have them to spare.*

### Scene 2: The Demon Road

The territory between the city walls and the Oracle's Sanctum stretches nearly forty miles along the old Diaspola road — two days of hard travel through land that was once the agricultural heart of Nexus and is now something closer to a graveyard. Five centuries of demon occupation have not merely emptied the territory; they have transformed it. Farmsteads have collapsed into their own foundations. Orchards planted in the early years of Nexus have grown wild, their fruit blackened and bitter, their trunks split by parasitic vines. The magitek infrastructure that once sustained the outlying settlements — irrigation pylons, wardstones, signal relays, grain-drying arrays — stands in various stages of decay, some still flickering with residual energy, most dark and corroded beyond recognition. Where the wards have failed entirely, the land has soured: soil that produces only thornbrush, water that collects in stagnant pools sheened with an oily iridescence that is not natural. The asuras did not do this deliberately. Their presence is simply corrosive to the systems that maintained the land, and five hundred years of that corrosion has left a landscape that feels less abandoned than poisoned.

The party should feel, from the moment they pass through the eastern gate, that they have left the world of the living and entered territory that belongs to something else. The silence is wrong. The vegetation is wrong. The light falls at angles that seem slightly off, as though the land itself has been subtly warped by prolonged exposure to creatures that were never meant to exist in it. And above all, the party should feel watched — not intermittently, but constantly, from the moment they leave the walls until the moment they reach the sanctum.

> *Beyond the eastern gate, the road runs straight through what was once the city's breadbasket. The fields are long gone — waist-high thornbrush and scrub have swallowed the old plots, and the stone walls that once marked property lines are half-buried in black earth. A magitek irrigation pylon stands at the first crossroads, tilted fifteen degrees, its crystal array cracked and dark. Someone has scratched marks into its base — claw marks, dozens of them, layered over one another as though the thing that made them returned to the same spot again and again. The remains of a village appear on the left after an hour's travel: roofless houses, a well with a collapsed cover, a signpost too weathered to read. Nothing moves. The silence is not the silence of emptiness — it is the silence of something paying attention.*

The journey to the sanctum is divided into five stages across two days, each presenting a different aspect of the territory and the forces that occupy it.

**Stage 1: The Outer Perimeter (Day 1, Morning).** For the first several miles, the party encounters signs of demon activity without direct contact — tracks in the soft earth, claw marks on ruined walls, the remains of previous patrols (bones, scattered equipment, no bodies — the asuras consume what they kill). The road passes through two abandoned villages in the first half-day, both reduced to foundations and chimney stacks. Between them, the remnants of the old magitek network are visible: a line of wardstones that once formed a defensive perimeter around the farming district, now toppled or shattered, their crystal cores pried out and crushed; a signal relay tower whose upper platform has been torn apart, its transmitting array scattered across the road in pieces. Perception checks (DC 14) reveal that the party is being watched from the tall grass by small shapes that retreat when approached. These are tripurasura scouts — gremlins — and they do not engage. They are reporting the party's approach to the asura leader.

The GM should describe the landscape deteriorating as the party moves further from the city. Closer to the walls, the ruins are recognizable as human habitation — crumbled but comprehensible. Further out, the vegetation grows denser and stranger, the ruins harder to distinguish from natural rock formations, and the magitek remnants more thoroughly destroyed. The demons have been here longer, and it shows.

**Stage 2: The Scavengers (Day 1, Afternoon).** In the early afternoon, as the road passes through a stretch of particularly dense overgrowth between two collapsed farmsteads, the party encounters what appears to be a small caravan of battlefield scavengers — three figures with a handcart, picking through the ruins of a magitek relay station. They are dressed in rough traveling clothes and carry salvage tools. One of them waves when the party approaches and calls out a greeting, claiming they are out of the city collecting magitek components for resale.

This is a lie. The three figures are **tripurasuras** using their Change Shape ability (see stat block) to assume humanoid forms. They are posing as scavengers to get close to the party and attack when the opportunity presents itself. The deception is good but not perfect — the details of their cover story do not hold up under scrutiny.

*Detecting the deception:* A Sense Motive check (DC 16) reveals inconsistencies — their story about operating out of the city does not match the reality that no sane salvage operation would venture this deep into demon territory, their "tools" are props that show no signs of actual use, and their demeanor is subtly wrong in ways that are difficult to articulate but unmistakable to someone paying attention. A Knowledge (planes) check (DC 15) allows a character to recognize behavioral tells specific to shapechanged asuras — a faint shimmer at the edges of their forms in direct sunlight, an inability to maintain eye contact for more than a few seconds, or a tendency to mirror the body language of whoever they are speaking to with mechanical precision.

*If the deception is detected:* The tripurasuras attack immediately when confronted, reverting to their true forms. The party acts in initiative order as normal.

*If the deception is not detected:* The tripurasuras maintain their cover until the party turns to leave or lets their guard down, then attack from behind. The party must succeed on a Perception check (DC 18, opposed by the tripurasuras' Stealth +19) or be surprised for the first round.

**Encounter: The False Scavengers**

- **Enemies:** 3 **tripurasuras** (Pathfinder RPG Bestiary 3, CR 2 each). They fight in their true Tiny forms, using their speed and size to stay mobile. They favor flanking, use their stingers for the poison effect (1/day each — Elixir of Truth, Fort DC 13), and attempt to separate party members by darting through difficult terrain.
- **Terrain:** The ruined relay station offers scattered cover — fallen stone walls, the corroded frame of the relay array, thick undergrowth on three sides. A functional but unstable magitek conduit runs beneath the site; if damaged (hardness 5, 10 hp), it discharges a burst of residual energy (2d6 electricity damage, Reflex DC 14 half, 10-ft. radius) that affects everyone in the area, including the tripurasuras.
- **Tactics:** The tripurasuras are ambush predators, not soldiers. If two of the three are killed, the survivor attempts to flee into the undergrowth. If it escapes, it reports to the asura leader at the village garrison, who reinforces the garrison encounter in Stage 4 with an additional 2 tripurasura scouts.

*GM note: This encounter teaches the party that the demons in this territory are not merely savage — they are cunning, deceptive, and willing to use patience and disguise to achieve their objectives. The tripurasuras' shapechanging ability is a defining feature of their kind, and establishing it here pays dividends throughout the campaign as the party encounters asuras on other worlds who use the same tactics at greater scale.*

**Stage 3: The Ranger Camp (Day 1, Night).** The party makes camp as darkness falls — the road is not safe to travel at night, and the gremlin scouts that have been shadowing them since Stage 1 are more active after dark. Sometime during the second watch (or whichever watch the GM prefers), figures approach the camp.

> *The sound reaches you before the sight — a low whistle, repeated twice, the kind of signal that says "I am here and I am not trying to surprise you." Then a voice from the darkness, calm and deliberate: "We're coming in. Four of us, plus animals. We are not demons. We'd appreciate it if you didn't shoot."*

The newcomers are a patrol of four **Nexian rangers** — scouts attached to the city watch who operate in the territory beyond the walls on long-range reconnaissance missions. They are lean, weathered, and visibly surprised to encounter civilians this far from the city. Their leader, a woman named **Sergeant Halda Voss**, explains that they are tracking a large group of demons that has been moving south through the territory over the past week — larger than any single raiding party they have seen before, and moving with apparent purpose rather than the usual random predation.

Each ranger carries a **magitek longbow** — a composite longbow fitted with a crystal-enhanced aiming array that provides a +1 enhancement bonus to attack rolls. These are rare and expensive weapons, issued only to the ranger patrols and Casomir's personal guard, and their presence signals that these scouts are not ordinary soldiers. Each ranger also has an animal companion: Voss has a hawk, two of the rangers have hunting dogs, and the fourth — a quiet, scarred man named **Torben** — has a companion that draws immediate attention. It is a wolf-shaped **magitek construct** — a creature of metal and crystal with the form and behavior of a living animal but the faint hum of active enchantment running through its frame. Torben's original companion was killed on a previous patrol, and the construct was provided as a replacement by the city's magitek foundries. It functions identically to a wolf animal companion but is a construct rather than an animal (immune to mind-affecting effects, does not need to eat or sleep).

**Information the rangers can share:**

The rangers have been operating in this territory for three weeks and can provide the party with current intelligence that supplements Casomir's months-old map.

- The demon concentration around the Oracle's Sanctum has increased since the map was drawn. The sanctum itself is now surrounded by a ring of empty ground — the demons will not approach the structure, but they have established a tighter perimeter around it.
- A ruined village roughly half a day's march further along the road has been converted into a demon command post. The rangers have observed it from a distance but have not attempted to engage — their mission is reconnaissance, not combat. They estimate a garrison of at least a dozen gremlins and at least two of a type they call **nightclaws** — small, black-skinned demons that move fast, disappear in the middle of a fight, and seem to command the gremlins. Voss warns the party not to underestimate them based on size: "We lost a two-man patrol to nightclaws last season. Found the bodies three days later. Clean kills — throat and kidney."
- The magitek infrastructure along the road is in worse condition than the city authorities realize. Several wardstones that were still functional a year ago have gone dark, and the signal relay network is completely dead past the second village. The rangers communicate with the city by hawk, not by relay.
- The large demon group they are tracking appears to be moving toward the gate town — the cluster of settlements that once surrounded the terminus of the Rainbow Road leading to Diaspola. If true, this suggests the demons are reinforcing their control over access to the gate itself, though the gate has been dormant for centuries.

*If the party asks about the Oracle:* Voss knows the Oracle exists — it is common knowledge among the rangers — but has never attempted to reach her. She confirms that previous patrols sent specifically to the sanctum have not returned. She advises the party to approach the village garrison carefully and offers to mark their map with the patrol routes the rangers use to avoid the worst demon concentrations, though she notes that no route to the sanctum avoids the village entirely.

*If the party asks the rangers to accompany them:* Voss declines. Her orders are to track the southern demon movement, not to assault the sanctum perimeter. She wishes them well and means it — she has lost friends to this territory and respects anyone willing to walk further into it.

The rangers depart before dawn, heading south. Their visit provides the party with tactical intelligence, a sense that they are not entirely alone in the wilderness, and a reminder that Nexus's defenders are stretched thin across a vast hostile territory.

**Stage 4: The Ruined Village (Day 2, Midday).** On the second day, the road passes through the village the rangers described — a larger settlement that the asuras have converted into a command post. The landscape on the approach has deteriorated further: the thornbrush is thicker, the magitek remnants more thoroughly destroyed, and the gremlin scouts that have been shadowing the party since Stage 1 are now visible on the road ahead, watching openly rather than hiding. They know the party is coming, and they are no longer concerned about concealment.

The village itself sits in a shallow depression where two roads once crossed. It is larger than the ruins the party passed on the first day — perhaps thirty buildings at its peak, now reduced to walls and rubble — and the asuras have made it their own. Gremlin lookouts perch on the remaining rooftops. The main road through the village has been kept clear of debris, creating a killing lane. Claw marks and scorch marks cover every surface. The air smells of char and something else — the metallic, ozone-sharp scent of asura presence concentrated in a small space.

The party can attempt to bypass the village through the fields (Stealth checks, DC 16 for the group, against the gremlins' Perception +10) or fight through it. The fields offer concealment but are choked with thornbrush (difficult terrain, 1 point of piercing damage per round of movement through dense patches) and dotted with the stumps of destroyed wardstones. An alternative route through a dry streambed to the south (Survival DC 14 to locate) avoids the thornbrush but adds two hours to the journey.

**Encounter: The Village Garrison**

- **Enemies:** 2 **hatyararata** (CR 3 each; see Stat Block Appendix, "Nightclaws"). Small outsiders (asura, evil, extraplanar, lawful). AC 17, 30 HP each, fast healing 2, DR 5/cold iron or good. These are the garrison's commanders — wingless, imp-like assassins with soot-black skin and razor claws. Their Shadow Double ability lets them create illusory duplicates of themselves as a swift action, allowing each hatyararata to flank an opponent on its own. They have sneak attack +2d6, poisoned claws (Dex damage, Fort DC 14), and access to *vanish* and *invisibility* for repositioning. Additionally, 4 **tripurasura** scouts (CR 2 each) on the rooftops provide harassment — they do not engage directly but use their stingers on targets of opportunity and attempt to flank. If a tripurasura escaped in Stage 2, add 2 additional tripurasura scouts to this encounter.

*GM note: The hatyararata are the first asura type the party encounters that fight with genuine tactical intelligence and signature supernatural abilities beyond the tripurasuras' shapeshifting. Their Shadow Double creates a flanking partner out of nothing — the party must learn to dispel the duplicate (any hit destroys it) or reposition to deny the flank. This encounter should be the hardest fight the party has faced so far and should communicate that the asuras near the sanctum are organized and purposeful in a way the earlier encounters were not. This is a garrison, not an infestation.*

- **Terrain:** The village offers abundant cover — ruined walls, collapsed roofs, overgrown gardens. The main road is a 15-foot-wide cleared lane between the buildings. The rooftops are 10 feet high and accessible via rubble piles at several points. A destroyed magitek grain-drying array at the village center provides elevated ground (15 feet) but is unstable — any creature weighing more than 100 pounds that climbs it must succeed on a Reflex save (DC 12) or cause a section to collapse, falling prone and taking 1d6 damage.
- **Tactics:** The hatyararata begin the encounter invisible (*invisibility*, 1/day, already cast when the party approaches). They wait for the party to engage with the tripurasura scouts on the rooftops, then strike from ambush — appearing behind the most lightly armored party member, generating a Shadow Double to establish a flank, and delivering sneak attack damage on the opening strike. After the initial ambush, they use *vanish* (at will) to break line of sight and reposition between attacks, fighting from the cover of ruined walls and doorways. They are disciplined enough to focus fire — both hatyararata target the same PC when possible. If one hatyararata is killed, the other uses *vanish* and attempts to disengage, retreating into the fields. The rooftop tripurasuras harass with hit-and-run stinger attacks throughout, dropping down, striking, and using their small size to retreat through holes in the walls. If both hatyararata are defeated, the gremlins scatter into the fields and do not pursue.

**Stage 5: The Sanctum Approach (Day 2, Afternoon).** Beyond the village, the road deteriorates into a footpath through increasingly dense undergrowth. What little remains of the magitek network here is the oldest and most damaged — foundation stones of structures that predate the current city, their purpose no longer identifiable, half-swallowed by root systems that have been growing through them for centuries. The air grows heavier — not physically, but in the way that proximity to something powerful and old presses against the senses. The gremlin scouts fall back. The larger asuras are absent entirely. The party is approaching the boundary of the Oracle's influence, and the demons will not follow.

The asura presence thins as the party nears the sanctum. The demons have pulled back from the structure itself, maintaining their perimeter at a distance of several hundred yards. The party passes through a ring of empty ground — trampled, scored with claw marks, littered with the detritus of a long encampment — and then the sanctum appears.

> *The structure is older than anything you have seen in Nexus. It does not match the city's architecture — it is rougher, stranger, built from stone that does not look like it was quarried so much as grown into the shape it holds. Moss and vine cover most of the exterior, and the entrance is a low archway that opens into darkness. The demons have not entered. Their marks surround the building on every side — scratches, scorch marks, the gouges of creatures that have thrown themselves against the walls and been repelled — but the archway is untouched. Whatever is inside, they cannot or will not follow.*

*GM note: The asuras' reluctance to enter the sanctum is genuine. The Oracle's presence is anathema to them — her power as an arbiter of the celestial system is antithetical to their nature as corruptions of the divine. They can surround her, prevent others from reaching her, and wait for her to weaken or die. They cannot enter. The party, upon crossing the threshold, is safe from pursuit.*

### Scene 3: The Oracle

The interior of the sanctum is a single chamber — larger than the exterior suggests, lit by a sourceless warm light, and empty except for the Oracle herself. The GM determines the Oracle's form — she may be a luminous figure of uncertain shape, an ancient humanoid of a race the party does not recognize, or something stranger. What matters is the impression: age beyond reckoning, authority that does not require explanation, and attention that makes the person being observed feel as though they are being read at a depth they did not know existed.

> *The chamber opens before you like the inside of a cathedral that has been folded into a space the size of a modest room. The walls are smooth and featureless, and the light that fills the space has no source — it simply is, warm and steady, casting no shadows. At the center of the chamber, [the Oracle] waits. She does not speak first. She does not need to. You understand, without being told, that you are being assessed — not judged, but read, in the way that a master artisan reads the grain of wood before deciding where to cut.*

The Oracle does not engage in ordinary conversation. She acknowledges the party's presence and their purpose — she knows why they are here before they speak. If they present Casomir's letter, she reads it without opening it (or reads it instantly, or has already read it — the mechanism is deliberately opaque). She then delivers her message.

**The Oracle's Message**

The message should be written by the GM before the session, tailored to the specific campaign. It should contain at minimum:

1. **An answer for Casomir.** This must be clearly interpretable as a denial — the Beast and Casomir cannot be separated. The phrasing should be cryptic but unmistakable once Casomir hears it. Example: *"The two who are one have always been one. The root cannot be cut from the tree without killing both."*

2. **A seed for a Warlock turning.** A phrase or image relevant to one of the eight outer-world Warlocks — ideally one whose lever is not obvious. This should be uninterpretable on first hearing and should only click when the party encounters the relevant situation. Example: *"The dancer in the box remembers the song her father played."* (Sona's Ivory Music Box on Diaspola.)

3. **A seed for the endgame.** A reference to Tornsoul, the corruption, or the nature of the final choice. This should be the most opaque element and should not become clear until the party is deep into Phase 2 or entering Phase 3. Example: *"The warden has become the prisoner. The prison become a weapon. What was given freely will be taken back in fire."*

The Oracle may also offer oblique acknowledgment of the party's potential — she recognizes what they are, even if they do not yet understand it themselves. She does not explain the Rainbow Road, the mythic system, or the party's role. She confirms nothing directly. She speaks, and then she is done.

*GM note: After delivering her message, the Oracle falls silent. She does not answer follow-up questions — or if she does, her answers are so compressed as to be useless without context the party does not yet have. The party should leave the sanctum carrying words they do not fully understand. This is intentional. The Oracle's message is a seed bank. Each seed sprouts at a different point in the campaign, and the moment of recognition — "that's what she meant" — is one of the most satisfying payoffs the adventure offers. Do not explain. Do not clarify. Trust the players to remember.*

**The Oracle's Token**

When the Oracle has finished speaking, and just as the party prepares to leave, something appears. There is no gesture, no incantation, no visible act of creation — the object is simply not there, and then it is, resting in the Oracle's outstretched hand as though it has always existed and the party has only now been permitted to see it.

> *The Oracle extends her hand. In her palm lies a small wooden token — no larger than a coin, carved from a wood you do not recognize. It is simple in design but finely worked, every line precise, the surface polished to a deep luster that catches the sourceless light of the chamber. It depicts a sunburst mounted on outstretched wings. The wood is warm to the touch — not the warmth of something that has been held, but a warmth that seems to originate from within the token itself, faint and steady, like a heartbeat.*

The Oracle offers no explanation for the token. She does not say what it is, what it does, or why she is giving it to them. She holds it out, and the weight of the gesture makes clear that it is meant to be taken. If the party hesitates, she does not withdraw her hand. She waits.

A Knowledge (religion) check (DC 18) identifies the sunburst-and-wings device as an ancient symbol of the Host of Heaven — the collective term for the celestial beings who built the Nine Worlds. The symbol has not been used in Nexus for centuries; it predates the current religious practices of the city entirely and belongs to an era when the celestials were still present and active. No living person in Nexus would recognize it without scholarly training. The token itself radiates faint transmutation magic if examined with *detect magic*, but its specific properties are not immediately identifiable.

*GM note: The token has no mechanical function at this stage. Its purpose is narrative — it is the Oracle's mark of recognition, her confirmation that she has examined the party and found them worthy of the path ahead. Casomir will recognize the token in Scene 4, and its significance will deepen at the Ancillary in Chapter 3 when the celestials acknowledge the party as successors to the original champions. Keep the token in the party's inventory. It may serve as a key or proof of authority at later points in the campaign, particularly at places of power where the celestial system's arbiter mechanisms are still partially functional.*

### Scene 4: The Return and Casomir's Response

The journey back to Nexus is less dangerous than the approach — the asuras have been disrupted by the party's passage through the village garrison, and their scouts are in disarray. The return trip should be narrated rather than played encounter by encounter unless the GM wants to add tension. The party arrives at the city with the Oracle's message.

Casomir receives them immediately. He asks for the Oracle's words — specifically the words, not a summary. He listens with the focused attention the party has come to expect from him. And then something changes.

> *You have seen Casomir Zukra composed under pressure, direct in conversation, steady in the way of a man who has been holding things together for a very long time. You have not seen him like this. The words reach him and something behind his eyes shifts — not breaks, not crumbles, but settles, as though a question he has been carrying for longer than you can imagine has finally received its answer, and the answer is the one he feared. He is silent for a long time. When he speaks, his voice is the same, but the man behind it is different.*
>
> *"Thank you," he says. "You have done more than you know."*

If the party shows Casomir the Oracle's token — or even if they simply mention that she gave them something — his reaction is immediate and unmistakable. The composure cracks. Not far, and not for long, but enough.

> *Casomir looks at the token in your hand. For the first time since you have known him, the king of Nexus is not in control of his expression. He stares at the wooden disc — the sunburst, the wings, the warm polished grain — and then, without a word, he reaches into his collar and draws out a chain. Hanging from it is a token identical to yours. The same wood. The same device. The same faint warmth. His is older — the polish worn smooth by five centuries of contact with skin — but the craft is the same, and the meaning is the same.*
>
> *"She gave me one of these," he says quietly. "A long time ago. When my companions and I stood before her and she told us we were worthy of the Road." He looks from his token to yours and back again. Something in his face shifts — grief, certainly, but also something that might be relief. "I have been waiting for someone else to carry one of these for five hundred years."*

*GM note: This is the moment Casomir's plan crystallizes from contingency into certainty. He has spent centuries searching for successors — heroes capable of walking the Rainbow Road, completing the journey his companions failed. The Oracle's token is her seal of worthiness, the same confirmation she gave to Casomir's original group before they set out. Its appearance in the hands of this party tells him two things: the Oracle has judged them ready, and his own role is finished. Everything that follows in Chapter 3 — the arena, the Beast, the mercy killing — flows from this moment. He is not despairing. He is handing off the mission to the people the Oracle has chosen to carry it.*

> *He tucks the chain back beneath his collar. "Rest. Eat. Prepare yourselves for something harder than anything I have asked of you so far." He pauses. "I am sorry for what I am about to ask."*

*If the party does not show or mention the token:* Casomir asks whether the Oracle gave them anything besides the message. If they produce it then, play the scene above. If they withhold it (unlikely, but possible), Casomir proceeds without the confirmation — his plan is already in motion based on the Oracle's answer alone — and the token's significance is revealed later at the Ancillary in Chapter 3 when the celestials recognize it.

He does not explain further. He dismisses them with courtesy and genuine gratitude, and the party does not see him again until Chapter 3.

*GM note: Casomir has just learned that there is no separating himself from the Beast. The alchemical containment that has kept him functional for five centuries was always a delay, not a cure. The Oracle has confirmed what he suspected: the only way to stop the Beast is to kill the man. He has been preparing for this eventuality. The sealed arena beneath his citadel, the standing instructions to his seneschal, the search for heroes worthy of the Rainbow Road — all of it was contingency planning for this answer. Now the contingency becomes the plan. What follows in Chapter 3 is not despair. It is the last act of a competent man who has run out of options and is determined to make his death count.*

**Milestone:** Level 3 is awarded at the conclusion of this scene.

## Chapter 3: The Death of Casomir *(Levels 3–4, Mythic Tier 1)*

### Chapter Summary

Armed with the Oracle's answer, Casomir Zukra sets his final plan in motion. He summons the party to the citadel and offers a partial truth — that he was the leader of the ten heroes who walked the Rainbow Road, that their journey corrupted them, and that the Oracle has confirmed something he feared. He tells them there is more, but that he needs to show them. He leads them down through the citadel's lower levels into a sealed arena beneath the foundations — a chamber he has spent centuries preparing. Only there, with the iron doors closed behind them, does he reveal the full truth: he is the Beast Blavin. He has always been the Beast. And he has brought them here to kill him.

The party's response determines what happens next. If they agree — or if they hesitate long enough for Casomir to see that they understand — he answers their questions and makes his peace while time allows. If they protest, resist, or attempt to leave, Casomir triggers the transformation early, forcing the confrontation before the party is ready. Either way, the Beast emerges. The mercy killing becomes a combat as the transformation begins, and the party must fight a partially manifested Beast while Casomir struggles to hold it back long enough for them to strike. His death is neither clean nor simple. It is the end of the longest fight in the history of the Nine Worlds — a man who held himself together for five hundred years finally letting go because the right people have arrived to carry what he cannot.

Casomir's seneschal, **Maranthe Soldati**, does not know what her king has planned. She has long suspected a connection between Casomir and the Beast — the coincidences were too consistent, the absences too convenient — but loyalty kept her from pressing the question. Casomir has left her a letter explaining everything, timed to be discovered after the arena doors close. She finds it, understands, and rushes to stop him, but arrives too late. The party meets her in the aftermath, and she becomes their bridge to the next phase of the campaign.

After Casomir falls, Maranthe opens the passage to the Great Ancillary using the key and instructions Casomir left her. The party enters the ancient temple at the heart of Nexus, steps into the rune circle beneath the magitek orrery, and is taken outside space and time. There they meet representatives of the three celestial races, who confirm the party's mission, grant mythic tier 1, and bestow the first fragment of the Song of Heaven. The journey begins.

### GM Guidance

This chapter has two halves that serve entirely different narrative functions. The first half — the arena — is an action sequence built on emotional stakes. The second half — the Ancillary — is a mythic initiation built on wonder. The transition between them should feel like the campaign itself is changing register, because it is. Everything before this chapter was prologue. Everything after it is the adventure.

**The Deception.** Casomir's decision to reveal the truth in stages — a partial confession in his study, the full revelation in the arena — is not cowardice. It is strategy. He knows the party well enough by now to understand that if he tells them everything in a room with an unlocked door, they will try to find another way. They will argue. They will delay. They will petition the Oracle, search the archives, consult scholars. He cannot afford that delay — the Beast is growing stronger, the full moon is approaching, and the alchemical containment is failing. By getting the party into the arena before revealing the complete truth, he removes their ability to seek alternatives. The iron doors close. The conversation happens. And when the transformation begins, there is no option but to fight. This is manipulative, and Casomir knows it. It is also the act of a man who has run out of time and trusts the party to forgive him once they understand why.

**The Arena.** Casomir's death scene should be harrowing but not hopeless. The party is level 3 and fighting a creature that is, in lore terms, far beyond them — but the encounter is designed so that Casomir's preparations and his own resistance give the party a genuine chance to win. The Beast is not at full power. It is emerging, not emerged. The fight should feel desperate and barely survivable, not mathematically impossible. If the party fights well and uses the environment, they should win with significant resource expenditure and possibly a downed party member. If they struggle, Casomir's killswitch ensures the Beast cannot simply TPK them — though using the killswitch costs Casomir his last moments of lucidity, and the party will feel that cost.

The emotional core is Casomir himself. He has been the most competent, composed, and authoritative figure the party has known — the man who held Nexus together by force of will for five centuries. Watching him lose control, fight the thing inside him, and ultimately die asking the party to finish what he started should land with weight. Do not rush the pre-combat conversation. Let Casomir be honest. Let him be afraid. Let the party understand exactly what they are being asked to do before the Beast forces the issue.

**Maranthe.** The seneschal's arrival after the fight is a deliberate emotional counterpoint. The party has just killed a king in a sealed basement. They are bloodied, shaken, and possibly grieving. Then Maranthe arrives — furious, devastated, and too late — and the party must explain what happened to the one person in Nexus who loved Casomir enough to run toward the Beast rather than away from it. Her reaction grounds the scene in human cost. She is not a narrative convenience; she is the person who has to live with what Casomir chose. Play her grief and her anger honestly, and then let her composure return, because she is also the person who carries the key to the Ancillary and the instructions that move the campaign forward. She grieves, and then she does her job. That is who Casomir chose to trust.

**The Ancillary.** After the grief and violence of the arena, the Ancillary should feel like stepping into a cathedral. The tone shifts from urgent to solemn, from combat to ceremony. The celestials are not quest-givers — they are the architects of a system that has been waiting for this moment for longer than the party can comprehend. Their confirmation of the party's mission should feel earned, not expository. The mythic tier grant and the first Song fragment are the rewards for everything the party has survived in Chapters 1 through 3, and the chapter should end with the party standing at the threshold of something vast.

*If a player asks why they can't just contain the Beast as Casomir has been doing:* Casomir explains that the containment was never stable. It required constant alchemical maintenance, personal discipline, and the suppression of nearly all his magical and mythic abilities — and even then, the Beast broke free regularly. No one else can replicate what he has done, because the containment is keyed to his specific physiology and his knowledge of the corruption's behavior. The Beast dies when Casomir dies, there is no other way.

*If the party attempts to find an alternative to killing Casomir:* They will not succeed. The Oracle has already confirmed that separation is impossible, and no magic available in Nexus can accomplish what five centuries of alchemical research could not. Casomir knows this and has taken the possibility of delay out of the equation. The party can insist on delay all they want, but this will actually lead to a worse outcome. 

### Scene 1: The Summons

Three days after the party delivers the Oracle's message, Casomir sends for them. The summons comes not from a servant but from the seneschal personally — a tall, broad-shouldered half-orc woman named **Maranthe Soldati** who has served Casomir for over a century and whose expression, when she finds the party, carries a gravity that the previous summons did not. Maranthe is not a courtier. She is an administrator — blunt, efficient, and possessed of a memory for logistical detail that borders on the supernatural. She has kept the machinery of Nexus running for decades while Casomir focused on the larger crisis, and the respect between them is mutual and unspoken. She escorts the party to a part of the citadel they have not seen before — not the working chamber with its maps and reports, but a private study deep in the residential wing, smaller and quieter, with no maps on the walls and no documents on the desk. A room for thinking, not for governing.

> *Casomir is sitting when you enter — the first time you have seen him not standing. The Oracle's token hangs outside his collar now, visible, as though he has stopped concealing it. He looks as though he has not slept. He gestures to the chairs across from him and waits until the door closes behind Maranthe before he speaks.*
>
> *"I owe you a truth I have not shared with anyone in this city," he says. "Not all of it — not yet — but enough to explain what comes next." He pauses. "Five hundred years ago, I led ten heroes across the Rainbow Road. We completed the journey. We defeated the threat we were sent to face. And then the journey broke us. Every one of the ten was changed — corrupted — by the mythic power we carried and the horrors we witnessed. Some fell to madness. Some to cruelty. Some to despair."*

Casomir explains the corruption of the ten heroes in broad terms. He tells the party that each of his companions scattered to one of the eight outer worlds and that their corruption has manifested differently in each case — though he does not yet name them as Warlocks or describe their individual fates in detail. He tells the party that his own corruption took a form he has spent five centuries containing, and that the Oracle's answer confirmed what he feared: the containment cannot hold. He does not yet say that he is the Beast.

> *"There is more," he says. "More than I can explain sitting in a room. I need to show you something beneath the citadel — a place I have been building for a very long time. What I have to tell you will make more sense when you can see it." He stands. "Come with me. Please."*

*GM note: Casomir's partial revelation is calculated. He gives the party enough truth to explain the gravity of the situation — the corrupted heroes, his own failing containment, the Oracle's confirmation — without revealing the specific truth that would cause them to refuse the descent. He is not lying; everything he says is accurate. He is withholding the part that matters most until escape is no longer an option. Players who are suspicious (Sense Motive DC 20) detect that Casomir is holding something back — not lying, but choosing his words with a precision that goes beyond his usual directness. He is afraid, and the fear is not of the party but of what he is about to do.*

### Scene 2: The Arena

Casomir leads the party down through the citadel's lower levels — past the dungeons, past the foundations, into corridors cut from the bedrock itself. The descent takes twenty minutes. The air grows cooler and carries a faint chemical smell — the alchemical suppression systems that line the arena's walls. The corridors are old but meticulously maintained; Casomir has been preparing this space for a very long time.

> *The arena opens at the bottom of the last staircase — a circular chamber roughly eighty feet in diameter, with a domed ceiling thirty feet high. The walls are lined with crystalline arrays that pulse with a faint amber light, and the air tastes of metal and something sharper, something that makes your eyes water if you breathe too deeply. Iron doors seal the only entrance behind you — Casomir triggered the locking mechanism as you descended, and the heavy bolts engaged with a sound that echoed up the stairwell. The floor is smooth stone, scored with circular grooves — channels for alchemical fluid if you're not mistaken. At the center of the chamber, a raised platform holds a heavy iron lever.*

Casomir walks to the center of the room, turns to face the party, and tells them the rest.

> *"Beast Blavin is me." He says it without preamble, without softening, without the careful measured delivery you have come to expect. He says it like a man setting down something he has been carrying for too long. "The corruption I described — the one I tried to contain through alchemy — the containment failed. Or rather, it half-succeeded. The corruption manifests as the Beast. It shares my body. It shares my mind. Every time it has emerged and killed, I have been inside it, watching."*
>
> *He gestures to the crystalline arrays on the walls. "These are alchemical suppression systems. I built them over two centuries. They slow the transformation — they do not prevent it. The lever at the center floods the chamber with concentrated suppressant. If the Beast manifests fully, pull it. The gas will weaken the creature enough to fight, though it is toxic to you as well." He looks at the iron doors behind you. "The doors are sealed. I sealed them on the way down. They will not open until the mechanism resets, which takes approximately one hour."*
>
> *He removes his armor — the magitek plate that the party has never seen him without — and sets it carefully against the wall. Beneath it he is thinner than expected, his body marked with old scars and the faint tracery of alchemical burns along his arms and torso. He draws his sword and reverses it, driving it point-first into the stone floor so it stands upright between him and the party.*
>
> *"I have stopped taking my suppressants. The transformation will come — within minutes, if I allow it, or at the next full moon regardless. I brought you here because I need you to kill the Beast, and the Beast dies only when I die. The Oracle confirmed it. There is no cure. There is no separation. There is only this."*
>
> *He meets your eyes. "I am sorry for the deception. If I had told you upstairs, you would have tried to find another way. There is no other way. I have spent five hundred years looking."*

**If the party agrees:** Casomir's relief is visible and immediate — not joy, but the release of a tension he has been holding since before the party entered the room. He answers their questions with the honesty of a man who no longer has reason to conceal anything.

- *If asked about the Beast's capabilities:* "The shoulders are the weak point. Cut the tendons behind the shoulder blades and the arms lose their reach. The spine goes first in the transformation, then the jaw — when the jaw changes, I cannot speak. You will have a window while I am still partially in control. Use it."
- *If asked about what comes after:* "I have left instructions for Maranthe — my seneschal. She holds the key to the Great Ancillary, a temple at the center of Nexus that has been sealed since my companions and I returned. After I fall, she will open the way. Go inside. What you find there will explain more than I ever could."
- *If asked about Maranthe:* "She does not know what I am doing. She suspects more than she has ever said — she is not a fool, and the pattern of my absences and the Beast's appearances is not as well hidden as I have pretended. But she has never asked, and I have never told her. I left her a letter. She will find it soon." A pause. "She will try to stop me. That is why the doors are sealed."
- *If asked why he did not tell them sooner:* "Because you would have tried to save me. I have watched you. You are not the kind of people who kill a man when there might be another option. I could not risk the delay. The full moon is in four days."
- *If asked about the forty-three:* The number catches in his throat. Forty-three people killed by the Beast — that he knows of. Forty-three people who would be alive if he had done this sooner.

Casomir answers questions for as long as the party wishes to ask them. When the conversation reaches a natural end — or when the first tremor runs through his hands — he straightens and nods.

> *"It's starting," he says. "Thank you. For the Oracle. For this. For what you are about to do."*

**If the party protests, refuses, or attempts to leave:** Casomir has anticipated this. He watches the party argue, plead, or try the sealed doors, and his expression carries the particular grief of a man who predicted exactly this reaction and wishes he had been wrong. He does not argue back. He waits until the party's initial response has spent itself, and then he speaks.

> *"I understand," he says. "I would do the same, in your place. But you cannot leave this room, and I cannot give you more time." He holds up his hands. The tremor is already there — faint, but visible. "I have not taken my suppressants in three days. The transformation will come whether you agree to this or not. The only question is whether it comes while I still have enough control to help you fight it, or whether it comes after I have lost everything and the Beast is at full strength." His voice hardens — not with anger, but with the absolute clarity of a man who has made his decision and will not be moved from it. "I am giving you every advantage I can. Do not waste it."*

If the party continues to resist, Casomir triggers the transformation deliberately — ceasing his mental suppression entirely and allowing the corruption to surge forward. This removes the grace period the party would have had if they had agreed; the transformation begins immediately, and the party enters combat at a disadvantage (see the encounter below). If the party comes around before the transformation begins, play the scene as though they agreed, with whatever time remains for questions.

*GM note: The sealed doors are the key to this scene's dramatic structure. Casomir has removed the party's ability to walk away — not out of cruelty, but because he knows that the merciful option (delay, research, alternatives) is a trap. There is no alternative. The Oracle confirmed it. Every day of delay is a day closer to the full moon and a day in which the Beast might break free in the city. By sealing the arena, Casomir forces the party to confront the situation as it is rather than as they wish it were. Some parties will resent this. Let them. The resentment is honest and earned, and it will make the eventual grief more complex and more real.*

### Scene 3: The Beast

The transformation begins. Whether triggered by Casomir's failing control or forced by his deliberate release, the process is the same — only the timing changes.

**The Transformation**

The transformation is not instantaneous. It begins with small changes — Casomir's hands twisting, his posture shifting, bone grinding against bone beneath the skin — and accelerates over the course of two to three rounds. During this period, Casomir is still partially present. He speaks through gritted teeth, directing the party, calling out weak points, warning them when a surge of the Beast's power is about to hit. He is fighting the transformation with everything he has, buying the party time.

> *The change starts in his hands. The fingers lengthen, the knuckles swelling and splitting the skin, and Casomir looks down at them with an expression that is not surprise — he has seen this hundreds of times — but something closer to resignation. "It's starting," he says, and his voice is still his own. "The spine goes next. When the jaw changes, I won't be able to talk. Listen to me now — the shoulders are the weak point. Cut the tendons behind the shoulder blades and the arms lose their reach. Don't let it—" His back arches. Something cracks. He catches himself on his knees, and when he looks up, one eye is still Casomir's. The other is not.*

*If the party refused and Casomir triggered the transformation early:* The transformation is faster and more violent. Casomir's control is compromised by the deliberate release — he has less resistance to spend, and the Beast surges forward with a ferocity that suggests it has been waiting for this moment. Skip the read-aloud text above and use: *"The change is sudden and brutal. One moment Casomir is standing, his hands raised in something that might be surrender or might be apology. The next, his spine snaps backward at an angle no human body can sustain, and the sound that comes from his throat is not a voice but a tearing, as though something enormous is clawing its way out through too small an opening."* In this case, Phase 1 is shortened to 2 rounds instead of 3, and Casomir's Aid grants only a +1 circumstance bonus instead of +2.

**Encounter: The Emergence of Beast Blavin**

This encounter is designed as a phased fight with three stages, representing the Beast's progressive manifestation. The party is fighting a creature that is growing more powerful as the fight continues — urgency is the defining mechanic.

**Phase 1: The Half-Emerged Beast (Rounds 1–3)**

The Beast is partially manifested — larger than a man but not yet fully formed, its movements jerky and uncoordinated as Casomir resists from within. In this phase, the creature is vulnerable.

- **Stats:** Use a **Large creature** with approximately CR 4 statistics (AC 16, HP 55, two claw attacks at +7 for 1d8+4, bite at +7 for 1d6+4). The Beast has DR 5/— from its partially formed hide but suffers a –2 penalty to attack rolls and Reflex saves as Casomir's resistance impedes its movements.
- **Casomir's Aid:** Once per round, as a free action, Casomir reasserts control momentarily. This manifests as the Beast hesitating, lowering its guard, or exposing a weak point. The GM should describe this as Casomir's voice breaking through — a shouted instruction, a strangled "now!" — and grant the next PC to attack a +2 circumstance bonus to their attack roll.
- **Weak Points:** The shoulders, as Casomir described. A called shot to the shoulder tendons (attack at –4, or a successful Knowledge [nature] or Heal check DC 15 to identify the target without penalty) deals an additional 1d6 damage and reduces the Beast's claw attack bonus by 1 (cumulative, to a minimum of +3).

**Phase 2: The Full Emergence (Rounds 4–6)**

At the start of round 4, the transformation completes. The Beast is fully manifested — a massive, distorted humanoid form, its features a grotesque exaggeration of Casomir's own, radiating heat and the sharp ozone-scent of corrupted mythic power. Casomir's voice goes silent.

- **Stats:** The Beast's statistics improve. HP increases by 20 (to a maximum of 75 minus any damage already dealt), attack bonuses increase by +2, and the DR improves to DR 5/good. It gains a slam attack (+9, 2d6+6) as its body mass increases. Movement speed increases to 40 ft.
- **Casomir's Resistance Fades:** The +2 circumstance bonus from Casomir's aid is no longer available. The Beast acts with full autonomy.
- **Suppression Crystals:** The amber crystals on the walls pulse more intensely, and the chemical smell in the air grows stronger. The suppression system is still functioning, imposing a –1 penalty to the Beast's attack rolls and saving throws. If the Beast takes a full-round action to smash a crystal array (hardness 8, 15 hp), it can destroy one section of the suppression system per round. There are four sections; if all four are destroyed, the penalty is removed and the Beast gains fast healing 2 as its regenerative abilities are no longer suppressed.

**Phase 3: The Killswitch (Conditional)**

If the Beast reaches 30 or fewer HP in Phase 2, it attempts to destroy the remaining suppression crystals and break down the iron doors to escape. If it reaches the doors (hardness 10, 60 hp), the party has approximately 4 rounds before it breaks through.

If the party pulls the killswitch lever (a standard action by a character adjacent to the platform at the center of the room), the alchemical channels in the floor erupt with a thick amber gas that fills the chamber. All creatures in the arena take 2d6 points of nonlethal damage per round from the toxic suppressant (Fortitude DC 15 halves). The Beast's statistics are reduced: –4 to all attack rolls, –2 to AC, movement speed halved, and fast healing (if active) is suppressed. The gas disperses after 5 rounds, or immediately if a character retreats to the staircase and opens the ventilation grate there (a full-round action, Strength DC 12).

*GM note: The killswitch is a safety valve, not a win button. It makes the Beast manageable but poisons the party at the same time. The ideal outcome — from a narrative standpoint — is that the party kills the Beast in Phase 1 or early Phase 2, while Casomir is still partially present. This allows for a mercy killing that carries emotional weight. If the fight drags into late Phase 2 or Phase 3, the Beast is fully in control and the kill is a combat victory, not a mercy — still satisfying, but different in tone. The killswitch exists to prevent a TPK in a chapter where a TPK would end the campaign. Use it if the party is in genuine danger of being wiped out, but not before.*

*The specific stat blocks for this encounter will be provided in the Stat Block Appendix. The numbers above are guidelines — adjust HP, AC, and attack bonuses as needed to produce a fight that is hard, frightening, and survivable for a level 3 party of four. The Beast should drop at least one PC to negative hit points during the fight. It should not drop more than two unless the party makes serious tactical errors.*

**Casomir's Death**

When the Beast is killed, the transformation reverses. The monstrous form contracts, the distorted features smooth, and what is left is Casomir — broken, bleeding, dying, but himself again. If the killing blow was struck during Phase 1, Casomir has a moment of lucidity. He can speak a few words — enough to thank the party, to tell them to find the Ancillary, to say something the GM determines is appropriate to the specific party and the relationships they have built over three chapters.

> *The Beast collapses. The massive frame shudders, contracts, and then Casomir is lying on the stone floor, smaller than you remember, his body torn by the transformation and the wounds the party inflicted on the creature that wore his shape. His breathing is shallow and irregular. One hand reaches for the sword he drove into the floor — not to grasp it, but to touch the hilt, as though confirming that something still belongs to him.*

If Casomir has a moment of lucidity (Phase 1 kill), add:

> *His eyes find you. They are clear — entirely, unmistakably his. "The Ancillary," he says. "Maranthe has the key. She'll know what to do." A breath. Another. "Tell them I held on as long as I could." His hand falls from the sword. The amber light of the suppression crystals dims as the system, no longer needed, powers down. The chemical smell fades. What is left is silence, and a man who kept his word.*

If the Beast was killed in Phase 2 or 3, Casomir does not speak. The party finds him in the wreckage of the creature, already gone. The silence is its own kind of grief.

*GM note: Allow the party time with this moment. Do not rush to the next scene. Casomir's death is the emotional hinge of Phase 1, and the weight of it should be felt before the wonder of the Ancillary replaces it. If a player wants to take Casomir's sword, his armor, or his Oracle's token — let them. These are not magic items (the sword and armor are masterwork magitek equipment, and the token is as described in Chapter 2), but they carry narrative significance that may become important later.*

### Scene 4: Maranthe

The iron doors reset approximately one hour after sealing. When they open, **Maranthe Soldati** is on the other side.

She is not composed. She is not the efficient, steady administrator the party met at the start of this chapter. She is a woman who found a letter on her desk ten minutes after the king of Nexus walked into a sealed basement with the only people in five centuries he trusted enough to kill him, and who has spent the last hour descending through the citadel at a dead run, pulling rank on guards, breaking through locked doors, and arriving too late.

> *The iron doors grind open. The woman standing in the stairwell is breathing hard, her uniform disordered, a crumpled letter clutched in one fist. Her eyes go past you to the arena floor — to the body — and something in her face breaks in a way that makes the violence you just survived feel simple by comparison.*
>
> *"You—" she starts, and stops. She looks at you. She looks at Casomir. She looks at the blood on your weapons and the amber residue on the floor and the suppression crystals dimming on the walls. Her jaw works. When she speaks again, her voice is controlled, but the control is a visible effort, like watching someone hold a door shut against a storm.*
>
> *"He wrote me a letter," she says. "He left it on my desk where I would find it after you went down. He explained everything. The Beast. The corruption. What he was asking you to do." She unfolds the letter, then folds it again, as though the act of reading it is something she cannot do in front of other people. "I tried to reach you. The doors were sealed."*

Maranthe's reaction depends on the party's demeanor and what they tell her. She is not hostile — she understands, intellectually, what Casomir did and why — but she is grieving and angry, and the anger is not entirely rational. She is angry at Casomir for not telling her. She is angry at herself for not pressing the question when the suspicions were there. She is angry at the party for being the ones Casomir trusted with his death when she was the one who had stood beside him for a hundred years. None of this is the party's fault, and she knows it, but grief does not answer to logic.

*If the party explains what happened:* Maranthe listens. She asks sharp questions — how long the fight lasted, whether he spoke at the end, whether he was in pain. She does not ask whether it was necessary. She read the letter. She knows it was.

*If the party shows sympathy or acknowledges her loss:* Maranthe accepts it stiffly. She is not a woman who grieves publicly, and the party's kindness may crack her composure further than she is willing to allow. A brief nod, a tight "thank you," and then she straightens and becomes the seneschal again.

*If the party is callous or dismissive:* Maranthe's anger crystallizes. She does not threaten or obstruct — she is a professional, and the letter makes clear that the party's mission is more important than her feelings — but the relationship is damaged. She will be efficient and helpful for the remainder of the chapter, and she will not be warm.

After the initial confrontation, Maranthe's composure reasserts itself — not fully, and not without visible effort, but enough to function. She has read the letter. She knows what comes next.

> *She refolds the letter and tucks it into her belt. When she looks at you again, her expression has changed — not softened, but settled, as though she has put the grief somewhere she can reach it later and brought the rest of herself back to the work at hand. "He left me instructions," she says. "The passage to the Great Ancillary — I have the key. He gave it to me years ago and told me I would know when to use it." She looks once more at the body on the arena floor, and whatever she sees there is between her and the man she served. Then she turns. "Follow me."*

*GM note: Maranthe's ignorance of Casomir's plan is essential to this scene's emotional honesty. If she had known and agreed, her arrival would be procedural — a scheduled handoff. Instead, her arrival is a reckoning. The party has just killed the king of Nexus in his own basement, and the first person they face afterward is someone who loved him and whom he deliberately excluded from the decision. This is not comfortable. It should not be comfortable. Casomir's final act was selfless and necessary and also a betrayal of the person closest to him, and Maranthe's reaction is the cost of that choice. Play it honestly.*

*Maranthe's long suspicion about the connection between Casomir and the Beast is a detail worth seeding in earlier chapters if the GM wishes. A stray comment from a guard about how the seneschal always seems to know when the barricades are needed. Maranthe's particular attentiveness during conversations about the Beast. A moment in Chapter 1 or 2 where the party notices her watching Casomir with an expression that is not quite worry and not quite understanding. These small details make her arrival in this scene feel earned rather than convenient.*

### Scene 5: The Great Ancillary

Maranthe leads the party to a section of the citadel's lower levels that they passed on their descent to the arena but did not enter — a sealed door of the same ancient stone as the Oracle's sanctum, conspicuously different from the surrounding architecture. She produces a heavy iron key — old, ornate, unmistakably magitek, with crystalline inlays that pulse with a faint inner light.

> *"The passage to the Great Ancillary has been sealed since before I was born," she says. "He gave me this key years ago and told me I would know when to use it." She turns it in the lock. The mechanism engages with a sound that is less mechanical than harmonic — a deep resonance that travels through the stone floor and settles somewhere behind the sternum. The door opens. "He said you would know what to do when you arrived." A pause. "He also said to tell you that he was proud of you. He asked me to write it down so I would say it in those words."*

The door opens onto a corridor of polished stone that descends gently for several hundred yards before opening into the Ancillary itself.

> *The Great Ancillary is not a room. It is a space — vast, circular, and open to something above that is not the sky. The ceiling, if it can be called that, is a dome of darkness studded with points of light that move, slowly, in patterns that are not random. Beneath this dome, suspended by forces that have no visible source, hangs the orrery.*
>
> *Eight spheres of stone and crystal orbit a central sphere at the dome's heart, each one different — one sheathed in ice, one wrapped in cloud, one burning with a steady amber flame, one dark as a moonless night. The central sphere glows with a warm golden light. Above them all, a tenth sphere hangs motionless, radiant, impossibly bright, casting light downward onto the mechanism below. The orrery turns with a faint harmonic resonance — not quite music, not quite silence, but something in between that settles into the bones.*
>
> *Beneath the orrery, inscribed into the floor of the chamber, is a great diagram of interlocking runes. The lines glow with a light that matches the central sphere above — warm, steady, alive. The diagram is large enough for a dozen people to stand within it. The runes are not in any language you can read, but you understand, with a certainty that has no rational basis, that you are meant to step inside.*

When the party enters the rune circle, they are taken outside space and time. The chamber does not vanish — it simply ceases to be relevant. The party stands in a space that has no walls, no floor, no sky, only light and presence. The celestial representatives appear.

**The Celestial Meeting**

The celestials are three — one Archon, one Angel, one Agathion — representing the three races that created the Nine Worlds. They are not physical beings in this space. They are presences, vast and luminous, sorrowful and ancient. They do not introduce themselves with names. They may offer titles or epithets if pressed, but what matters is the impression: these are the architects of everything the party has seen, and they have been waiting for this moment since the system was first conceived.

> *Three presences resolve from the light. They are not humanoid — or they are, and more, and less, shifting between forms that your mind interprets differently each time you try to focus. One carries the weight of law in every line of its being, as though justice itself had taken form and found the burden heavier than expected. One moves like mercy — fluid, grieving, unwilling or unable to look away from the suffering of the world. One is wild but patient, with the stillness of deep forests and the attention span of something that has been watching the world grow since before it had a name for itself.*
>
> *They regard you. The regard is not judgement — you have already been judged, by the Oracle, by Casomir, by the road you walked to reach this place. What the celestials offer is recognition. They see what you are. They see what you may become. And in their vast, sorrowful faces — if faces is the right word — you see something that might be hope.*

The celestials convey the following, through speech or through direct understanding (the GM should use whichever feels appropriate for the tone of their game):

1. **Confirmation.** The party has been chosen. The Oracle's token confirms their worthiness. The death of Casomir — the last of the original champions — marks the beginning of a new cycle. The Nine Worlds were built to produce heroes. The party is what the worlds produced.

2. **The Mission.** The eight outer worlds are each ruled by one of Casomir's former companions, each corrupted by the same force that created the Beast. The Rainbow Road — the network of pathways connecting the worlds — has been shut down for five centuries. To restore it, the party must travel to each world, confront its Warlock, and activate the mythic place of power that serves as the Road's anchor on that world. Each place of power will grant the party a portion of mythic energy and a fragment of the Song of Heaven — the melodic key that opens the next segment of the Road.

3. **The Choice.** The Warlocks are not monsters. They are people — corrupted, damaged, dangerous, but people. Some can be saved. Some cannot. The celestials do not tell the party which is which. They say only that the manner in which the party confronts each Warlock will shape the outcome of the journey and the fate of the worlds themselves. Every death carries weight. Every redemption carries cost.

4. **The Grant.** The celestials bestow mythic tier 1. The GM should describe this as a physical and spiritual transformation — the party feels the power of the system enter them, ancient and purposeful, designed for mortal vessels but originating from something far beyond mortal understanding. Each PC chooses their mythic path at this time (Archmage, Champion, Guardian, Hierophant, Marshal, or Trickster). The celestials present the paths as facets of the same power — the party is not choosing a specialization so much as discovering which aspect of the mythic force resonates with their nature.

5. **The First Fragment.** The celestials teach the party the first fragment of the Song of Heaven — a short melodic phrase, simple but haunting, that settles into their memory with the permanence of something that has always been known and only now remembered. This fragment, when carried to the gate town that once served the world of Diaspola, will reopen that segment of the Rainbow Road.

If the party shows the celestials the Oracle's token, the celestials acknowledge it with something that approaches reverence. They confirm that it is the Oracle's mark of worthiness — the same seal she placed on Casomir's group five hundred years ago. If the party also carries Casomir's token (taken from his body or given to them before his death), the celestials' response deepens. Two tokens in the hands of the same group has not happened before. The significance is not explained, but the celestials' reaction makes clear that it matters.

*GM note: The celestial meeting is not a dialogue — it is a ceremony. The celestials answer questions, but their answers are compressed and allusive, more like the Oracle's speech than ordinary conversation. They do not explain the corruption mechanic, the ally-tracking system, or the specifics of Warlock turning. They speak of choices and consequences in terms that are true but not yet useful. The party will understand them retroactively, as they understood the Oracle's words. The purpose of this scene is to give the party their mission, their power, and their first step — and to make all three feel earned.*

*If a player asks which mythic path is "best":* All paths are viable. The celestials do not recommend. If the party lacks healing, a Hierophant is useful; if the party is melee-heavy, a Champion or Guardian provides mythic combat options; if the party wants mythic spellcasting, Archmage or Hierophant. Marshal is excellent for parties that want a tactical leader. There are no wrong choices — the system adapts to what the champions become.*

### Scene 6: The First Step

The party returns to the physical world in the Great Ancillary. The orrery still turns above them, but something has changed — one of the eight orbiting spheres, the one sheathed in ice, now glows faintly brighter than the others, as though responding to the Song fragment the party carries. The rune circle beneath their feet has dimmed. The ceremony is complete.

Maranthe is waiting outside the sealed door. She does not ask what happened inside — she can see it in them. Something has changed, and whatever it is, she recognizes the shape of it because she saw it described once, in Casomir's private journals, when he wrote about what he and his companions looked like the day they walked out of this same door five centuries ago.

> *"It's done, then," she says. Not a question. She studies you for a moment, and whatever she sees confirms something she has been weighing since the arena. "He left provisions. Maps. Everything you would need." She produces a sealed chest. "He also left you a letter. And he left me a job to do while you are gone — someone has to hold this city together, and he seems to have decided that someone is me." The ghost of something that is not quite a smile crosses her face. "He was always better at delegating than he admitted."*

Maranthe produces a sealed chest containing Casomir's personal provisions for the journey: a set of maps covering the Rainbow Road terminus points for each gate town (drawn from the original expedition's records and updated where possible), a supply of alchemical goods from the citadel's stores (GM determines specifics — healing supplies, alchemical weapons appropriate for level 4 characters, and one or two utility items), and a letter.

The letter is brief. It is addressed to "the ones who come after" and written in Casomir's precise hand:

> *You carry what I could not. The Song will guide you — follow it, and the Road opens. Each world has a place of power. Each place of power has a guardian who was once my friend. Some of them can still be reached. Some cannot. You will know the difference when you stand before them.*
>
> *The path is fixed: Diaspola first, then Tanis, then Auzra, and so through all the worlds. The Song determines the order. Do not attempt to deviate — the Road will not permit it.*
>
> *Trust the Oracle's judgement. She chose you for a reason, even if she did not explain it. She never explains.*
>
> *I held on as long as I could.*
>
> *— Casomir Zukra, King of Nexus, Last of the Ten*

*GM note: The letter serves double duty — it is an emotional capstone to Casomir's arc and a practical reference document that confirms the world sequence for the party. If the party has questions about the order of worlds or the nature of their mission, the letter provides answers without requiring the GM to break the celestials' allusive tone. Casomir, characteristically, prepared for the possibility that the divine briefing would leave his successors confused.*

*Maranthe's role going forward: She becomes the regent of Nexus in Casomir's absence — a position no one appointed her to and everyone accepts because she is the only person capable of holding the city together. The party will encounter her again at the start of Chapter 12 (The Battle of Nexus), where the strength of her leadership and the state of the city's defenses will depend in part on the ally strength the party has accumulated across the eight worlds. She is a recurring NPC — competent, loyal, still grieving, and determined to make Casomir's death mean what he intended it to mean.*

**Milestone:** Level 4 and Mythic Tier 1 are awarded at the conclusion of this chapter. The party should have time to level up, choose mythic paths, and prepare before departing for the Diaspola gate town.
