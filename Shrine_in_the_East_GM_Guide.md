# THE SHRINE IN THE EAST
## Game Master's Guide

*A Pathfinder First Edition adventure for characters of levels 1–12*

---

# INTRODUCTION

## About This Adventure

*The Shrine in the East* is a full-length campaign adventure set in the kingdom of Haven, a young nation carved from the western coast of a vast and largely unmapped continent by exiles from a dying world. It takes characters from their first tentative steps as ordinary subjects of the Crown to the threshold of legend — and asks, at the end of that journey, whether the legend their kingdom has built for itself is worth preserving.

The adventure spans four acts and the full breadth of a continent. In the first act, the player characters serve the Crown as agents, handling the dangerous and politically entangled problems that the king cannot address through conventional means. In the second, they depart Haven on a sacred quest to recover the kingdom's founding artifact from the far side of the world. In the third, they find that the far side of the world remembers things Haven has chosen to forget. In the fourth, they return home armed with an object of immense power and the knowledge of what their kingdom actually is — and must choose what to do with both.

The dark truth at the heart of this adventure is not a villain in a dungeon. It is a history: the history of how a people in desperate circumstances made choices they could not take back, built a story around those choices that grew more comfortable with each retelling, and passed that story to their children as fact. The founders of Haven were not evil. They were frightened, exhausted, and operating with incomplete knowledge under conditions that left little room for deliberation. The harm they caused was real regardless. Three centuries of accumulated consequence have not gone away simply because the official account does not mention them.

The player characters will not know any of this when the campaign begins. They will learn it — piece by piece, revelation by revelation — across the course of a journey that takes them from everything familiar to everything foreign and back again. What they do with that knowledge is the adventure's engine.

## How to Use This Guide

This book is organized as follows:

- **The Realm** covers everything needed to understand and portray Haven, its geography, its people, its institutions, its history, and its secrets. Read this section thoroughly before running the adventure.
- **Running the Adventure** covers the mechanical and narrative systems underpinning the campaign: milestone leveling, the quest pool, the magic economy, the historical revelation tracker, the winter downtime framework, the ordeal system, the Act IV crisis framework, and the multiple-endings structure.
- **Chapters 1–9** present the adventure itself, act by act. Each chapter opens with a summary and GM notes, followed by scene-by-scene descriptions.
- **Appendices A–D** contain all stat blocks, magic items, and reference tables.

**Read-aloud text** is presented in blockquote format:

> *This text is meant to be read aloud or paraphrased to your players. It describes what the characters see, hear, and experience.*

**GM notes** appear in italics throughout the chapter descriptions and offer guidance on pacing, player choices, and likely outcomes.

## Adventure Synopsis

**Act I: Agents of the Crown** *(Chapter 1, Levels 1–5)*
The player characters are elevated to royal service by King Harold II, the young and embattled ruler of Haven. The kingdom is under pressure from multiple directions simultaneously — a noblewoman's conspiracy, a church official suppressing a dangerous heterodox movement, a dragon raiding the frontier, a grief-driven elven sorceress, and a cursed knight no one can kill. The characters are dispatched to deal with these problems in whatever order they choose, learning that the order matters and that every solution leaves something behind. Act I ends when Harold II, satisfied that the party has proven itself, commissions them for the Sword Quest and formally names their order.

**Act II: The Sword Quest** *(Chapters 2–4, Levels 6–8)*
The characters journey east across the continent in search of the Sword of the Redeemer, Haven's founding artifact, borne east three centuries ago by King Cuthbert and left at a shrine at the far end of the world. The journey takes them through the Wild — the great forested wilderness at Haven's eastern border and deep into the unmapped interior. At the midpoint, early-closing weather forces them to winter on the western side of a great mountain range. On the far side of the mountains, they enter Aldenmere: a land Haven's people have never seen and whose people remember things Haven would rather forget.

**Act III: The Far East** *(Chapters 5–7, Levels 9–11)*
The characters arrive in Aldenmere and discover that the people there know Cuthbert not as a perfect hero but as a man who left his kingdom because he was ashamed of what it was becoming. They meet Signi, the current Redeemer, who believes the time has come to return the Sword to the west. They are sent to Reinja — Cuthbert's elven wife, still living, still guarding the approach to the five ordeals she designed. If they prove worthy, they undergo those ordeals across five sites scattered through Aldenmere's eastern reaches. At the end, the Sword passes to them, and Cuthbert's bound spirit speaks the truth of his departure — the truth that Haven has never known.

**Act IV: The Return** *(Chapters 8–9, Level 12)*
The characters return to Haven. The crises of Act I have escalated in their absence. Factions press from every side. The Sword's return is not a secret, and everyone with power in the kingdom has a position on what should be done with it. The characters must decide: give the Sword to the king, give it to a noble house, use it to rally the common people, or find a fourth path that the adventure does not anticipate but cannot foreclose.

## Themes and Tone

*The Shrine in the East* is a romantic fantasy in the Arthurian tradition: it concerns itself with questing knights, fey magic, feudal honor, tests of worthiness, and the weight of heroic destiny. The world is one in which valor matters, sacred artifacts have genuine power, and the gods take a personal interest in the affairs of the people who worship them. The adventure asks players to inhabit this register sincerely — it is not a deconstruction. The romance is real.

What the adventure also does, within that register, is examine where the romance came from and what sustains it. The story Haven tells about itself is, broadly, the Arthurian story: a great king, a divine mission, a sacred weapon, a people made worthy of their land through the king's heroism. The adventure reveals, gradually and without melodrama, that this story has omissions. The land had people in it before the exiles arrived. The divine mission was complicated from the beginning by disagreement among the gods. The great king had doubts he did not share with anyone and took those doubts east with him rather than reckon with them in public.

The tone this calls for is one of genuine weight. The revelations the characters encounter should feel consequential, not cheap. The fey who have raided Haven's border settlements for three centuries are not going to stop because the party learns that their grievance was legitimate — but knowing the grievance is legitimate should change how the party thinks about what they are defending and what they are willing to defend it as. The GM's task is to hold both things at once: a world that is genuinely beautiful and genuinely flawed, in which heroism is real and its costs are also real.

**Tone guidance:** Portray Haven as a place worth caring about before asking players to question it. Let the king be a man the party can respect. Let the common people be people the party can feel for. Let the fey be strange and the ordeals be hard and the spirit of a dead king be genuinely moving. None of this is diminished by the truth. If anything, it matters more because of it.

**On magic:** Magic is a wonder in this world, and it should be treated as one. It does not appear in shops. It does not appear casually in the hands of minor antagonists. When it manifests — in a fey gift, in the power of a sacred artifact, in the ordeals — it should feel like an encounter with something larger than the everyday world. The GM should resist the temptation to normalize it through frequency.

## Adjusting for Your Party

*The Shrine in the East* is designed for a party of four to five characters. Pathfinder's action economy and feat system make party composition more consequential than in some systems; the following adjustments account for smaller and larger groups.

- **Three characters:** Reduce enemy hit point totals by 25% throughout. Lower skill DCs by 2 in social and exploration scenes. The honorable duel with the Dark Knight should be available to any party member rather than requiring a single combatant — three characters' worth of resources will have been more heavily spent than four's by the time the quest reaches him. In Act III, consider reducing the number of required ordeals from five to four; the Sacrifice ordeal is the most narratively resonant and should not be cut.
- **Six characters:** Add hit points to all named antagonist encounters. The Sorceress's charm effects and the dragon's breath weapon should have larger areas or affect additional targets. The five ordeals should each challenge the full party rather than a single member.

The adventure's social and investigative content is not materially affected by party size. Its combat encounters are designed with four characters in mind and should be scaled accordingly.

## Dealing with the Fey

Haven's people have accumulated a body of lore about fey interaction across three centuries of border contact. Much of it is accurate. Some of it is not. The GM should treat this section as a reference for fey interaction throughout Act II and Act III. When players ask what their characters know about dealing with the fey, allow a Knowledge (Nature) or Knowledge (Local) check and share information selectively — noting that some of what their characters "know" may be wrong.

The entries below present each common belief, followed by a GM note indicating whether it is true, partially true, or false.

---

**"A gift from the fey carries an obligation."** *(True)*

A gift given by a fey creature carries an obligation unless the giver explicitly releases the recipient using the precise ritual form: *"I offer this without obligation, let, or lien, and without expectation it will return to me."* Any gift given without this declaration creates a debt — the nature and scope of which the fey will define at a time of their choosing. The obligation does not expire, cannot be dissolved unilaterally, and is recognized by all fey as a legitimate claim regardless of whether the specific giver is present. Parties who receive fey gifts without the ritual release should note what was given and who gave it.

---

**"Do not eat or drink what the fey offer you."** *(Partially true)*

Fey food, if freely and explicitly offered, may be consumed safely. The offer must be genuine — there is a meaningful difference between food placed before a guest and food taken without invitation. However, those who eat fey food often find mortal food disappointing afterward: the flavors are incomparable, and the loss accumulates with each meal eaten in the ordinary world. This effect is not magical in the mechanical sense; it simply reflects the quality of what the fey produce. It does not harm. It does change.

Fey wine is a different matter entirely and should never be consumed under any circumstances. Its effect is not intoxication but a gradual displacement of the self — a loosening of one's sense of where and when one is — that the affected party rarely notices until it has progressed to a point that is difficult to reverse.

---

**"Do not follow fey music into a fairy ring or circle of standing stones."** *(False)*

Fairy rings and standing stones are not inherently dangerous, and fey music played in their vicinity carries no special additional risk. This belief conflates the genuine danger of fey music — which exists and is addressed below — with specific locations that have no particular significance in fey tradition. The error obscures both truths.

---

**"When traveling in the fey realm, never stray from the path."** *(True)*

This is precise and should be followed literally. A traveler who leaves a defined path in the fey realm — whether a road, a track, or even a trail of footsteps clearly understood to be the way forward — will become lost immediately, in ways that cannot be resolved through ordinary navigation. The fey realm does not have geography in the sense that the material world does; paths are conventions the realm honors for those who use them, and departing from a path removes that courtesy entirely. Being lost in the fey realm is not metaphorical.

---

**"Do not speak first to a fey creature unless absolutely necessary."** *(False)*

Speaking to a fey is harmless. Speaking carelessly to a fey is not. This belief is a misreading of a more specific principle: that one should not make promises, give one's name, or enter into agreements without deliberate thought. The prohibition has been generalized into a superstition about speech itself, which the fey do not recognize and which causes unnecessary awkwardness in fey interactions.

---

**"Never walk widdershins around a fairy mound or standing stone."** *(False)*

No consistent tradition supports this belief and the fey do not recognize it as a rule. It appears to derive from confusion with the rule of three, which is a real and significant principle of fey interaction, or from isolated incidents that have been overgeneralized.

---

**"Always be polite. But never say 'thank you,' 'I'm sorry,' or 'you're welcome.'"** *(True)*

Politeness is genuinely required in fey interaction. The fey take offense at rudeness with a specificity and a memory that mortal cultures rarely match, and an offense given cannot always be easily repaired. However, three common expressions of politeness are themselves problematic:

*"Thank you"* places the speaker under obligation to the recipient — an obligation the fey may subsequently claim.

*"I'm sorry"* is read as a formal admission of fault, which the fey may record and use against the speaker in future negotiations.

*"You're welcome"* implies the speaker has performed a service, which in fey convention is understood as an implicit request for reciprocation.

The correct approach is to express gratitude, acknowledgment, and hospitality through other forms — "I am glad of it," "this is well done," "your house honors us" — which carry the appropriate warmth without the problematic implications.

---

**"Never give your name to a fey creature unless they offer theirs first."** *(True)*

Names hold genuine power in fey tradition. Giving one's name to a fey who has not first offered their own grants them a specific and exercisable authority over you — sufficient, in the case of a powerful fey, to curse you directly, without contact or proximity. The convention is mutual: a fey who offers their name first is acknowledging a reciprocal vulnerability, which is a significant act of trust and should be recognized as such. The party should never give their names first and should note the significance when a fey does.

---

**"Never dance with a fey creature, and be wary when listening to their music."** *(True)*

Both dancing with a fey and sustained listening to fey music can produce enchantment, the primary effect of which is lost time: minutes experienced as moments, hours experienced as a pleasant evening. The fey do not always use this ability intentionally — for some it is simply the effect of their presence — but they are aware of it and capable of deploying it deliberately. The precaution is to maintain active awareness of one's surroundings when music is playing and to avoid dancing unless one has specific grounds to trust the fey in question.

---

**"Never give a fey creature an article of your clothing."** *(False)*

The fey love receiving clothing as gifts. They value craftsmanship highly, and an article of clothing offered with appropriate form — and of genuinely good quality — is one of the more reliable gestures of goodwill toward an unfamiliar fey. The danger runs in the other direction: clothing of poor quality, offered as if it were adequate, is a deep insult, equivalent to suggesting that the fey's judgment cannot distinguish good work from bad. Offer clothing only if it is something you would be proud to present.

---

**"The fey always follow the rule of three."** *(True)*

This principle governs fey social interaction broadly. A gift will be offered three times and is expected to be declined twice before acceptance is appropriate — accepting on the first or second offer is considered presumptuous. Promises made by fey are not binding unless stated three times; a promise made only once or twice is a statement of intent, not a commitment. A request made to a fey only once or twice has not yet been fully made. Parties who understand this rule will find many fey interactions more navigable than they would otherwise appear; parties who do not will misread a great deal.

---

**"Mentioning a newborn child in front of a fey will cause them to attempt to steal it."** *(False)*

No general truth underlies this belief. Specific fey have interests in specific children for reasons specific to those situations; there is no general principle by which mentioning a child triggers fey behavior. This superstition appears to derive from isolated incidents that have been generalized beyond their actual scope.

---

**"If you borrow from the fey, never repay more than you borrowed."** *(True)*

Repaying a fey debt with more than the original amount is understood as a comment on the value of the original gift — specifically, as implying that the borrower felt the fey could not afford to make the gesture adequately in the first place. The fey find this extraordinarily insulting. The correct repayment is exact, offered in the same spirit in which the original was made.

---

**"Always keep your word when given to the fey."** *(True)*

A word given to the fey is binding. Breaking it allows the fey to claim a service of equivalent or greater value from the one who broke it, without the breaking party's consent and without recourse. This rule is symmetrical: a fey who gives their word and breaks it is similarly bound and may be held to the same claim. This mutuality is one of the few points of genuine symmetry in fey interaction and one of the party's most reliable tools when negotiating with fey whose honesty they cannot otherwise rely upon.

# THE REALM

## Haven

Haven is a kingdom three centuries old, founded by exiles from a dying world and built on the bones of something older. It occupies a sub-continental peninsula on the western coast of a vast continent — bounded to the north, west, and southwest by the sea, to the south by the Badlands, and to the east by the Wild. Within those borders, Haven's people have built something that in some lights resembles civilization and in others resembles a long argument about what civilization is for.

The kingdom's cultural inheritance is Anglo-Saxon in register: hierarchical, honor-conscious, deeply religious, and built on a model of obligation that flows from the king downward through his nobility to the common people who work the land. The Crown governs through the nine Earls who hold the kingdom's settled regions, each of whom owes the throne military service and taxation in exchange for the right to govern their earldom as they see fit. Between the Earls and the king there is a permanent and structural tension — the kind that attrition can pause but cannot resolve — and it has flared into open rebellion within living memory.

The people of Haven are predominantly human. Dwarves, concentrated primarily in the northeastern hills and in the skilled trades throughout the kingdom, form the second-largest group. Halflings, found in particular density on the central prairie earldoms, make up a smaller but economically significant portion of the population. Elves and gnomes exist at the kingdom's margins — some having integrated into border communities, most regarded with a mixture of wariness and fascination, all understood to be outsiders regardless of how long they have been present.

The kingdom's relationship with magic is one of official condemnation and practical tolerance. The Pentad, Haven's unified church, opposes magical practice as a usurpation of divine power — and because the church reaches into virtually every community in the realm, this opposition has real social force. Practicing magic openly invites suspicion and sometimes worse, particularly in the more religiously conservative earldoms. At the same time, the gods do not grant clerical power to every petitioner; true clerics are rare, and the practical needs that clerical magic would otherwise serve do not disappear because clerics are unavailable. Druids, alchemists, and folk practitioners of various kinds operate throughout the kingdom in a grey zone of social tolerance — valued for what they can do, distrusted for how they do it.

### Sanction — The Capital

Sanction occupies the shores of Aldenmere Lake — a broad, cold body of freshwater at the center of the royal lands — and has for three centuries served as the seat of a kingdom that is not always certain what it wants the word "seat" to mean. The city is not Haven's largest; Barstow, the commercial center in the south, carries more trade and more people. But Sanction is where decisions are made, where the court gathers, where the church's highest officials come to argue with the Crown's administrators, and where the processional road that runs south through Falkrith arrives after its long passage from Landing. Every significant thing that happens in Haven eventually passes through Sanction.

Tintavel, the king's castle, stands on a rise at the lake's western bank: a structure of dark stone that has been added to, rebuilt, and reinforced across three centuries until it has ceased to resemble any single architectural moment and simply resembles itself. The great hall at its center remains from the original construction — broad, low-ceilinged, and lit by oil lamps that give it the permanent character of late afternoon. The towers are newer, built in response to the last rebellion and clearly intended to make a statement about permanence. The statement is unconvincing to anyone who looks closely at the mortar.

The city extends south and east from the castle's base, running along the lake's edge in both directions before turning inland. The lake itself is a resource the city uses constantly: fishing boats work its surface in the early mornings, merchant barges cross it throughout the day, and in the evenings the castle's reflection lies on the water in a way that is, by most accounts, genuinely beautiful. Sanction has a habit of being more beautiful than it strictly needs to be, which may be why its people are loyal to it in ways that do not always have obvious rational explanations.

### The Nine Earldoms

Haven's noble titles follow convention: a male holder of an earldom is an Earl; a female holder is a Countess. The title of Countess also belongs to the wife of an Earl, which means it is sometimes unclear from the title alone whether the Countess in question rules in her own right or through her husband's. This ambiguity is a feature of Haven's political culture, not a bug, and has been exploited accordingly by those who benefit from it.

**Kharas** occupies the northeastern hills, country that would be forbidding if its inhabitants were not well-suited to it. The dwarves who have called this region home since the exile fleet's landing work the hills for iron, coal, and the occasional vein of silver, and they have made Tormund — the earldom's chief settlement — into a city of workshops and forges whose output is relied upon by the entire kingdom. Kharas's eastern border faces the Wild directly, and the string of strong forts that line it represent several generations of accumulated military opinion about the correct relationship between a dwarven settlement and a fey forest. The Earl of Kharas is currently on speaking terms with the Crown, which is not always the case.

**Waldsea** stretches east-northeast of Sanction into wide, flat grasslands that transition without ceremony from farmable to merely habitable to actively strange. The region has always been prone to magical anomalies — lights in the sky, places where sound behaves incorrectly, isolated pools that reflect landscapes no one recognizes — and its proximity to the Wild makes fey incursions a routine feature of life in its eastern settlements. Harbow, the trading town that serves as the earldom's commercial center, has been raided enough times that its residents have developed a resigned competence about it. The current Earl of Waldsea was among the four who rebelled against Harold II; the bad blood has not fully resolved.

**Cant** occupies the eastern interior, north of Goodlund and south of Waldsea, on land productive enough to have supported a significant population across all three centuries of Haven's existence. The people of Cant are devout — more devout, on average, than Haven's population generally — and their piety has a particular character that expresses itself in part through a heightened suspicion of things that cannot be accounted for by the Pentad's official theology. Witch hunts have occurred in Cant more frequently than anywhere else in the kingdom. The Pentad's institutional presence is strong here, and the relationship between Cant's clergy and Cant's population is one of genuine mutual investment rather than mere social convention. Aerbra, the earldom's chief settlement, is a city of shrines and small temples that draws pilgrims from as far as Falkrith.

**Umbra** is lightly populated by Haven's standards, its terrain — rocky in the south, rolling grassland in the north — having selected for self-sufficiency over density. Its southern border is defined by a great river that serves as a natural barrier between the settled earldom and the goblinoid-inhabited mountains beyond. The river has held for three centuries, not without difficulty. Dalkeith, the earldom's primary settlement, is a small port city that does most of its meaningful business with the sea rather than the interior. The current Countess of Umbra was not among the four who rebelled, a fact she is occasionally required to mention.

**Swansea** is, in the estimation of most people who do not live there, a place of marshes and difficult weather and incomprehensible local customs. In the estimation of people who do live there, it is a place of marshes and difficult weather and customs that work perfectly well once understood, and the incomprehension is the other party's problem. The north-central earldom's population concentrates in Carrack, a delta town on the coast, and in the scattered fishing communities that dot the marsh's edges. Swansea has the smallest military contribution of any earldom and the most reliable supply of certain fish that the rest of the kingdom eats without thinking about where they come from.

**Goodlund** occupies the central-east prairie east of the royal lands, bounded by Swansea to the north, Cant to the northwest, Waldsea to the northeast, Caerwold to the south, and Umbra to the southeast. It is the halfling heartland of Haven: prosperous, agricultural, and possessed of a brewing tradition that the rest of the kingdom has strong opinions about. Dale, the earldom's large market town, handles much of the agricultural surplus that flows from Goodlund's farms and orchards westward toward Sanction and southward toward Barstow. The Earl of Goodlund has a reputation for pragmatism that makes him useful to whoever currently holds the Crown.

**Caerwold** is Haven's commercial heart: large, productive, connected to the sea by multiple port towns, and home to the institutional headquarters of the Pentad in Barstow. The church's presence makes Caerwold politically complicated. The Earl answers to the Crown; the Archbishop of the Pentad in Barstow answers to theology; these two lines of authority overlap in ways that both parties manage carefully and neither fully acknowledges. Barstow itself is a substantial city by Haven's standards — larger than Sanction, more cosmopolitan, and less comfortable, in the way that places with a great deal of traffic and a great deal of money tend to be less comfortable than places with less of both. The Earl of Caerwold was among the four who rebelled.

**Tana** occupies the northwestern coast: wooded country, isolated by geography from the rest of the kingdom, home to a population that has developed in its isolation a set of characteristics that the rest of Haven finds either charming or maddening depending on context. Logging and fishing are Tana's economic foundations. Heathclyde, the earldom's chief settlement, is a small port town that exports timber and imports most things it needs. The current Earl of Tana was not among the four who rebelled and has subsequently been cautiously friendly with Harold II without committing to anything specific.

**Falkrith** runs west-central along the coast and inland across well-settled grasslands, home to the ancient processional road that connects Landing — where the exile fleet first made landfall three centuries ago — to the capital at Sanction. The road is Haven's oldest infrastructure and carries both commercial traffic and a steady stream of pilgrims who regard the journey from Landing to Sanction as a form of re-enacting the founding. Landing itself is a port city of considerable historical weight and moderate current significance. The Earl of Falkrith controls the processional road, which is an asset that has historically been used with more or less subtlety depending on the political situation. He was among the four who rebelled.

### The Wild and Its Peoples

The Wild is not a place on Haven's maps so much as an acknowledgment that Haven's maps have edges. East of the kingdom's settled territory, the great forested wilderness covers the vast interior of the continent in an unbroken canopy that the exile fleet, arriving from the sea, never had occasion to enter. Haven's people encounter the Wild primarily at its margins — in the fey incursions that probe the eastern earldoms, in the occasional delegation that approaches a border settlement with purposes not always immediately clear, and in the stories told about what lies beyond the tree line, stories that are rarely reliable.

The peoples who inhabit the Wild are grouped under the term "fey" in Haven's common usage — a word that covers an extraordinary range of communities, traditions, and relationships to the world and to each other. Elves are the most commonly encountered, partly because they are most likely to venture into Haven's border regions and partly because Haven's people find them most intelegible. Gnomes appear somewhat less frequently and are regarded with a mixture of curiosity and wariness that is not quite the same as the wariness directed at elves. The traditional fey races — the small and the strange, the creatures who seem made of the forest itself — are encountered rarely enough that each encounter functions as an event rather than a routine.

The Wild also contains, now, the goblinoid peoples whose ancestors were displaced from the western coast when the exile fleet arrived three centuries ago. This is not a fact that Haven's official history dwells on. The goblins, hobgoblins, and their kin were pushed east into the Wild by the pressure of the exiles' settlement; they warred, in that displacement, with the fey peoples already living there; and the wars of that original dispossession have structured the relationship between Haven and the Wild ever since. The goblinoids have made a wary peace with the fey over the course of three centuries and maintained an enduring and specific hatred for Haven. Their raids against the kingdom's border settlements are not random violence. They know exactly what they are raiding and why.

From within Haven, the Wild is perceived as a source of threat, strangeness, and occasional beauty. The truth is that from within the Wild, Haven is perceived as a wound that has not yet finished bleeding.

### The Pentad

Haven's official religion is the Pentad: five deities understood as a unified pantheon, worshipped together as the divine guarantors of the exile fleet's survival and Haven's subsequent existence. The Pentad is not Haven's state religion in any formal legal sense, but it functions as one — the church is present in virtually every community in the kingdom, its influence reaches into political decisions at every level, and the vast majority of Haven's population is sincerely devout in the way that people often become when a religion has been the organizing framework of their culture for three hundred years.

The five gods of the Pentad are of intermediate divine strength. They were all once mortals — an unusual feature of the pantheon that is both widely known and not widely understood. The Pentad's official account frames their apotheosis as a reward for exceptional virtue. The fuller account, known to dedicated theologians and to the small number of individuals the gods have spoken to directly, is that Wotan chose them precisely because they had been mortal: because the experience of being human, of having needed things and feared things and failed things, would make them better gods for a people in desperate circumstances than any being who had known only divinity. The gods take an active hand in the world — appearing in visions, speaking to their chosen clerics, occasionally manifesting in more direct forms — in part because they are aware that their nature is shaped by the beliefs of their followers, and they manage that process actively to remain themselves rather than becoming whatever their worshippers need them to be.

True clerics — individuals granted divine spellcasting directly by the gods themselves — are rare. The gods do not grant this power casually or in response to petition; they choose their clerics according to criteria they do not explain. Most communities in Haven do not have a cleric. Those communities rely instead on the folk practitioners who fill the gap: druids who work with the natural world, alchemists who study material transformation, and various hedge practitioners who have acquired fragments of useful knowledge through means the church officially disapproves of. The Pentad's opposition to magical practice extends in principle to these practitioners as well, but the church generally calibrates its enforcement to what the local community can sustain.

**The Five Gods:**

*Wotan, the Trickster,* is the god of diplomacy, travelers, rulers, and magic. He chose Cuthbert as his champion and was the primary architect of the exodus from the dying world. Among the Pentad, he led during the founding years — and has been progressively sidelined by the other four in the three centuries since. His name has been largely removed from Pentad ceremony; he is referred to in official contexts only by his title, which was assigned by his colleagues and which he regards with a specific dry amusement. In the prayers of Haven's common people, the Trickster appears as an afterthought or not at all. His domains are Luck, Magic, Rune, Travel, and Trickery. A heterodox movement in Haven is attempting to restore his proper theological position. It is not going well for the movement.

*Rana, the Mother,* is the goddess of community and nature. Wotan recruited her to help hold the exile fleet together across its internal divisions — a task she was particularly suited for by both temperament and power. She remains the most universally beloved of the five, the goddess most likely to appear in personal prayers and domestic shrines, the divine figure most easily associated with the ordinary experience of daily life. Her domains are Animal, Community, Earth, Plant, and Protection.

*Tor, the Warrior,* is the god of weather, warfare, and physical strength — a deity of great force and mercurial temperament whose relationship with Wotan goes back to their shared mortal lives as adventurers. He is invoked in battle, in storms, and in feats of endurance. His worship has a physical, immediate character that appeals to certain kinds of devotion and troubles certain kinds of theologians. His domains are Chaos, Fire, Strength, War, and Weather.

*Tyr, the Father,* is the god of rulers and justice. He is called the far-seeing, said to observe the entire world from his mountain fortress at the edge of the divine realm. The primary patron of knights and noble houses, Tyr's worship is most concentrated among the military and governing classes and among the devout populations of earldoms like Cant that take seriously the idea of divine order expressed through social hierarchy. His domains are Air, Glory, Law, Nobility, and Sun.

*Mara, the Scholar,* is the goddess of knowledge and civilization, with a warrior aspect that concerns itself with strategy and planning rather than force. She is the most intellectually oriented of the five, the patron of archivists, administrators, and tacticians, and the deity most likely to inspire the kind of slow, careful devotion that does not announce itself in public. Her domains are Artifice, Healing, Knowledge, Liberation, and Water.

### Key Factions

**The Crown.** Harold II is not the king Haven would have designed for itself in a period of stability. He inherited the throne at an age that left him immediately vulnerable, spent the first years of his reign managing a rebellion that attrition rather than victory ended, and has governed since in the shadow of that inconclusive war. He is not, however, naive. He understands the structural nature of the tension between the Crown and the nobility, he knows that the bad blood from the rebellion has not resolved, and he is attempting — in the commissioning of the party and the Sword Quest — to build something that previous kings did not think to build: a source of royal prestige and popular legitimacy that does not depend on the nobility's cooperation. The Crown's interests and the party's will align throughout most of the campaign. Whether they align at the end depends on what the party learns and what they choose to do with it.

**The Nobility.** The nine Earls are not a unified faction. They have individual interests, individual histories with the Crown, and individual relationships to the rebellion that four of them participated in and five did not. What they share is a structural position — they hold the kingdom's productive land, they command its local military forces, and they are aware that their collective power exceeds the Crown's so long as they can be persuaded to act collectively. The Countess, as the primary antagonist of Act I, represents this faction at its most dangerous: aristocratic ambition operating through conspiracy rather than open rebellion. The noble who might receive the Sword in Ending 2 represents it at its most capable: a figure who can use legitimate power to impose a legitimate settlement.

**The Pentad.** The institutional church is Haven's most pervasive power. Its presence in virtually every community gives it a reach that neither the Crown nor the nobility can match, and the sincerity of the common people's devotion means that the church's endorsement or condemnation of a political position carries genuine weight. The church is not monolithic: the conservative faction, concentrated in Cant and represented at the national level by figures like the Priest in Act I, prioritizes theological orthodoxy and social order. The reforming impulse represented by Father Beede is heterodox but rooted in genuine scholarship. By the end of the campaign, the party will have encountered enough of the Pentad's history to understand how it diverged from its origins — and what restoring Wotan's proper place would actually require.

**The Common People.** Haven's serfs, smallholders, craftspeople, and fishermen have no formal political power and considerable accumulated patience with that condition. Father Beede's movement is the most visible expression of their interests but not the only one. The conditions that make the third ending possible — a popular rising sufficient to challenge both the Crown and the nobility — are present in Haven. They require someone with sufficient legitimacy and sufficient nerve to activate them. The Sword provides the legitimacy, the nerve is the party's.

**The Wild's Peoples.** Not a unified faction. A collection of communities and traditions with three centuries of accumulated grievance against a kingdom that has alternately ignored them, persecuted them, and failed to understand that the two amount to the same thing. Some fey communities near Haven's border have reached a functional accommodation with its settlements; others have not. The Sorceress in Act I represents one expression of fey grievance: personal, specific, and comprehensible once its origin is known. The broader pattern of fey-Haven relations is the kind of problem that cannot be resolved through a single quest, only gestured toward through individual acts of comprehension.

**The Goblinoids.** Pushed into the Wild by the exile fleet's arrival, now an established presence in the continental interior. At uneasy peace with the fey peoples whose territory they share; in a state of ongoing low-level war with Haven. Their raids against the kingdom's border settlements are the persistent expression of a wrong that has never been acknowledged. They are not available as allies in this adventure, but understanding them is part of understanding the true history that the party will learn in Aldenmere.

### Key NPCs

**King Harold II (The Young King).** Harold II is twenty-eight years old, five years younger than the rebellion wished he were and five years older than the experience of the last decade has made him feel. He is not a large man — average height, lean, with the particular stillness of someone who learned early that nervous movement is a tell. His hair is the dark auburn of the royal line, worn short in the military fashion. He has been a good king in the ways that do not always show — administratively capable, attentive to the reports that cross his desk, skilled at identifying competent subordinates — and an inadequate king in the way that most matters: he has not yet found a lever that moves the fundamental problem of Haven's internal division.

Before the throne came to him, he was a questing knight of some reputation — a youth spent not in court but on the road, addressing problems that required someone willing to go and do something about them rather than send others. The common people remember this about him, and many believe it is why his grandfather chose him over candidates closer in age to the throne. It is also why they like him in a way that runs somewhat ahead of his actual accomplishments as a monarch.

Harold II is, at his core, a pragmatist who is also deeply pious — a combination that creates a consistent internal tension and makes him a more complicated figure than either quality alone would produce. His faith is genuine and shapes everything: how he speaks to subordinates, how he makes decisions under pressure, what he considers acceptable in pursuit of a political goal, and who he trusts. He prays. He is not self-conscious about it. He considers the welfare of his subjects a religious obligation and not merely a political one, which occasionally leads him to positions that confuse the people around him. He is also capable, in service of a pragmatic goal, of decisions that are tactically sensible and spiritually uncomfortable, and he carries the weight of these decisions more visibly than he intends.

His relationship with the church is complicated by the fact that he does not consider the Pentad's institutional hierarchy to be equivalent to the will of the gods — a position that the church finds inconvenient and that Father Eadric, his confessor, has spent years helping him articulate in terms that do not produce a political crisis. His appointment of a royal magician — the Magister, the first such appointment in the kingdom's history — was viewed by the church as a deliberate provocation. Harold II considers it a practical necessity. Both characterizations are partially correct.

He is aware that the Sword Quest is, among other things, a gamble — that if it succeeds it gives him something no noble house can credibly claim, and if it fails it has at least demonstrated that the Crown is capable of attempting something ambitious. He will be honest about this with the party if asked. He will not volunteer it unsolicited.

*Harold II's primary motivation is the stability of his kingdom, which he believes is better served by the Crown than by a noble oligarchy. He is not wrong about this. He is also not entirely right, and the adventure will make this clear. His piety is not armor; it is a genuine engagement with difficult questions, and the party will encounter it most directly when the Sword and its history require him to reckon with what his kingdom actually is.*

**King Cuthbert the Redeemer.** Cuthbert is three centuries dead, and Haven has been constructing his legacy ever since. The construction is not malicious — most of it happened gradually, through the ordinary process by which a founding figure becomes a symbol and the symbol's requirements come to reshape the memory of the man. The man himself was genuinely exceptional: capable, compassionate, and clear-eyed about his own limits in a way that people who acquire the kind of power he received rarely manage to sustain.

He was also, by the end of his time in Haven, profoundly unhappy. He had watched the kingdom he built making choices he could not prevent and found himself unwilling to remain as a passive witness to what those choices were producing. His departure was not abandonment; it was a judgment — on what Haven was and what it was becoming, and on his own inability to change either while remaining at its center.

The party will not meet Cuthbert in person. They will meet his ghost, briefly, after the ordeals. The ghost is not the legend. It is a tired man who spent two centuries thinking about what he got wrong, and who will speak plainly to anyone willing to listen. He does not editorialize. He does not ask for anything. He states what was true and leaves the party to determine what to do with it.

**Reinja.** Cuthbert's second wife is alive in the way that elves are alive: continuously, attentively, and without the urgency that shorter-lived races bring to their hours. She was in Aldenmere before Cuthbert arrived, was aware of who he was before he told her, and chose to involve herself in his remaining years and his project because she found the project worth the investment. She has maintained this project and his legacy for two centuries since his death.

She is not warm in the conventional sense. She is precise — in her speech, in her attention, in the conditions she sets for the party's access to the ordeals. Her test of the party's worthiness is not a performance of severity; it is a genuine assessment, and she adjusts her response to the party based on what she actually observes. Parties that try to charm her will find her uncharmed. Parties that treat the question of their worthiness as a real question rather than a formality to be dispatched will find her unexpectedly willing to help.

*In interaction:* Lead with her attention to detail and her brevity. She says what she means and stops. She does not fill silence with elaboration. She has heard every argument for why an exception should be made and declined all of them; do not imply she will be different in this case. She is interested in the party in the way that a careful craftsperson is interested in the quality of material they have been handed to work with.

**Signi.** The current Redeemer is the second of Cuthbert's physical descendants to have passed the ordeals — the first being Cuthbert's own child by Reinja, who took the title generations ago and held it until her death. Signi is aware of the full history of Haven: the displacement of the goblinoids, the war with the fey, the sidelining of Wotan, the progressive simplification of Cuthbert's legacy into a story that serves the kingdom's ruling class better than the truth would. She has spent time deciding what to do with this knowledge and has concluded that it must go back. Not to expose Haven, or to condemn it — she has no interest in punishment — but because the Sword was never meant to remain in Aldenmere. It was meant to return when there were people in the west capable of using it properly.

She is cautiously hopeful about the party. She is too experienced to be uncautiously hopeful about anything.

*In interaction:* Signi is direct and warm without being familiar. She has received visitors from Haven before — not many, but some — and her reception of the party reflects genuine interest rather than practiced hospitality. She will tell them everything she knows about the true history without being asked; it is not information she withholds, only information that takes a conversation to work through. She will speak of Cuthbert with genuine affection and without sentimentality.

**Father Beede.** The leader of the Trickster movement is sixty-three years old, broad through the shoulders in a way that does not quite match the image of a scholar-monk, and possessed of a quality of attention that makes conversation with him feel, consistently, like the most important conversation happening in whatever room he is in. He spent decades in a monastery copying texts and being, by his own account, wrong about almost everything. The founding-era documents he discovered changed what he was wrong about.

He is not a revolutionary. He does not want to overthrow the church, the Crown, or the social order. He wants the Trickster's proper place in the Pentad restored, because the Trickster's proper place is the one that was true and the present account is a lie, and he finds that he cannot continue to participate in the lie now that he knows it. The social consequences of this position — the threat it poses to the church's authority structure, to the nobility's theological legitimacy, to the comfortable arrangement between the powerful and the divine — are not his preferred focus, but he is not naive about them.

If the party kills or captures him, they will return to a kingdom whose common people remember it.

*In interaction:* Father Beede speaks with the precision of someone who spent decades thinking about how words work. He is not confrontational; he is patient. He is also not going to pretend to believe things he does not believe, and he will say so if asked. He regards the party with genuine curiosity — they are, to him, an interesting development, and he will try to understand what they represent before deciding how to relate to them.

**The Magister.** The newly appointed royal magician is known in public only by his title. Harold II has not disclosed the man's name, and the Magister appears in any setting beyond Tintavel's inner chambers wearing a deep cowl that makes identification difficult. Speculation about his identity is a persistent feature of court gossip and has produced a number of theories, none of them correct. The common image of a wizard — slight, bookish, diminished by long hours of study — does not prepare most people for the reality: he is tall, physically strong, with a firm jaw, steely grey eyes, and a short-cropped grey beard that suggests a man past middle age who has worn it well. He carries himself like someone accustomed to being the most capable person in a room and not particularly interested in demonstrating it.

He is unfailingly correct in his dealings with the court, precise in his counsel to the king, and almost entirely absent from social occasions that do not require his attendance. Those who have attempted to learn more about him through ordinary investigative channels have found the trail unusually cold. Whether this is by design, by circumstance, or by some combination of the two is one of several questions about the Magister that remain unanswered.

*The Magister is a resource for the party in matters of magical knowledge and, if the king is their patron, occasional practical assistance. His history and identity are deliberately left open for the GM to develop. His relationship with Harold II is one of mutual professional respect with an undercurrent of something more complex on both sides.*

**Father Eadric.** The cleric of Tyr who serves as Harold II's confessor is not a man who inspires solemnity on first impression. He is large in the way that men become when they have spent years enjoying themselves and have had no compelling reason to stop: broad through the middle, with a florid complexion and an expression of permanent good humor that has occasionally been mistaken for simple-mindedness and has never been corrected by its owner, who finds the mistake useful. His reputation for lechery and feasting is well established and freely acknowledged by the man himself, who regards these as matters between himself and his god and not the church's institutional business.

His relationship with the church's institutional hierarchy is correspondingly strained. He operates at the formal edge of orthodoxy — close enough to avoid censure, far enough to say things that more cautious clerics will not. He is protected by his proximity to the king and by a personal notoriety that has made him, in certain circles, the kind of figure whose removal would raise more questions than his continued presence. He is, despite everything, a genuine cleric, genuinely chosen by Tyr, and the quality of his counsel to Harold II is better than anyone who has not observed it closely would expect.

*In interaction:* Father Eadric does not speak in the register of the pious. He speaks plainly, laughs readily, and reserves his theological weight for moments when it will actually land. He is fond of the party in the way that a man with a healthy appetite for interesting people tends to be fond of interesting people, and he will be more useful to them than he appears, more perceptive than his manner suggests, and more protective of the king than either his reputation or his comfort would indicate.

**Lady Teta.** The daughter of the Earl of Cant is twenty-three years old, dark-haired, and possessed of a quality of stillness that could be mistaken for composure and is, in fact, something more calculating than that. She is one of Harold II's two paramours in the tradition of courtly love — an object of public devotion rather than a private liaison, though the distinction between the two has never been entirely clear at Haven's court and Lady Teta does nothing to clarify it. She is widely regarded as the more suitable match: good family, good politics, the right earldom for a Crown that needs Cant's loyalty.

She is politically astute in a way that the court respects and slightly fears. The whispers about her — that she maintains a secret lover within the court, that her influence over certain decisions exceeds what her formal position explains, that her apparent deference to the king is a performance rather than a disposition — have never produced anything actionable, which is either evidence that the whispers are wrong or evidence that she is very good at what she does. Both possibilities have their advocates.

*In interaction:* Lady Teta is impeccably pleasant without being warm. Every conversation with her has the quality of an assessment: she is deciding what you are and whether you are useful, and she is doing it behind a manner that makes the process invisible to most people. The party will benefit from treating her as an ally only if they can offer her something real; she has no use for people whose usefulness is purely theoretical.*

**Lady Sorella.** The other of the king's paramours comes from a family of middling rank in Tana, and her presence at court is understood to be a function of the king's personal interest rather than any political calculation. Her grandmother was half-elven, and the inheritance shows: she has what the court calls otherworldly beauty, a description she has never been seen to react to in any direction. She is taller than most women at court, with the particular stillness of someone who has made peace with being looked at and decided that engaging with it is not worth the effort.

She is a voracious reader and an expert on courtly poetry, which she can occasionally be persuaded to recite, to the delight of everyone present except herself. Her singing voice is considered angelic by those who have heard it and coveted by every musical institution in the kingdom. She does not consider her position at court a privilege. She considers it something close to captivity, and the circumspection with which she manages this opinion is the product of intelligence rather than contentment.

*In interaction:* Lady Sorella is aloof by design. She is not unfriendly — she simply does not extend herself toward people who have not demonstrated that they are worth reaching toward. Characters who approach her as an interesting person rather than a court ornament will find her considerably more engaged. She knows things about Tana, about her fey heritage, and about the internal dynamics of the court that she does not volunteer but will share with someone she considers trustworthy.*

## Running the Adventure

### Milestone Leveling

This adventure uses milestone leveling. Characters advance not by accumulating experience points but by completing the narrative achievements that mark meaningful progress through each act.

**Act I (Chapter 1):**
- **1st level:** Campaign start.
- **2nd level:** Completion of the first quest in the Act I pool.
- **3rd level:** Completion of the third quest.
- **4th level:** Completion of the fifth quest.
- **5th level:** After the midsummer court assembly.

**Act II (Chapters 2–4):**
- **6th level:** After escaping or defeating the goblinoid raiding party.
- **7th level:** After escaping Queen Elandra's court.
- **8th level:** After their encounter with the Wild Hunt.

**Act III (Chapters 5–7):**
- **9th level:** After the encounter with Lord Wustan and Lady Tamera.
- **10th level:** After completing the second ordeal.
- **11th level:** After completing the fifth ordeal.

**Act IV (Chapters 8–9):**
- **12th level:** Upon their return to Haven.

*Note on Act II: Advancement is tied to the three major setpiece encounters rather than to geography or travel time. The goblinoid encounter opens Act II; Queen Elandra's court sits in the journey's middle; the Wild Hunt falls on the autumnal equinox. The party reaches these encounters at different points depending on their pace and choices, but each represents a genuine threshold regardless of when it arrives.*

---

### The Quest Pool System

Act I presents the party with five simultaneous problems in Haven's earldoms. These are not sequential — the party may choose which quest to pursue first, and their choices have consequences for the quests they defer.

**How the Quest Pool Works**

When the party accepts Harold II's commission and departs Sanction, all five quests are active. Each quest has an escalation track: the situation worsens each time the party completes another quest and sets out for the next. The act ends when either all five quests have been resolved or the court assembly occurs at midsummer, whichever comes first. A capable party will complete all five quests before the assembly; the assembly is the structural deadline that prevents indefinite deferral.

**Escalation Tracks**

The quest pool advances by one stage whenever the party completes a quest and sets out for the next. The escalation stages are indexed to quest intervals — the number of quests the party has already completed.

| Quest | Stage 1 (0–1 completed) | Stage 2 (2–3 completed) | Stage 3 (4+ completed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Countess | Network active; Alefrith preparing at Harbow | En route to Sanction with witch hunters | Arrived at court; strike imminent (counts as failure if assembly reached) |
| The Priest | Movement noticed; not yet considered a threat | Operating openly; heresy charges filed against members | Trial underway; civil unrest in Barstow; Beede killed if party has not intervened |
| The Dragon | Burning isolated farmsteads | One village destroyed; settlement evacuated | Second village destroyed; third under threat |
| The Sorceress | Spectacular appearance in Belham; accusations made | Withdrawn to glade; grief intensifying | Children kidnapped to force confrontation; if Sir Odda fails to respond, curse laid and children released |
| The Dark Knight | Wandering; one confirmed kill | Two to four confirmed kills | Six or more confirmed kills; knights refuse to patrol Tana–Falkrith road |

*Note on the Sorceress track: Meldreth's escalation is non-linear. She acts dramatically at Stage 1, withdraws through Stage 2 (a period of intensifying grief that makes her accessible and willing to speak), and takes direct action at Stage 3. Stage 3 has two phases: she first kidnaps local children to force a public confrontation with Sir Odda, releasing them once he responds or once she concludes he will not. If he fails to respond, she lays the curse on him and releases the children regardless. The party has a window at Stage 2 where negotiation is most available.*

**Interconnections**

Some quests provide information that opens additional options in others. These are available only if the party acts before evidence is concealed:

- Investigating Lady Alefrith *before she departs Harbow* reveals correspondence connecting her witch hunters to Father Marne's inquisition. This grants leverage in the Priest quest: presenting this evidence to Marne opens resolution options unavailable otherwise.
- Speaking with Father Beede (before or after addressing the Priest quest) grants access to the founding documents. The party learns the first Historical Revelation. These documents are directly relevant to the confrontation with Cuthbert's ghost in Act III.
- Resolving the Sorceress quest fully — obtaining justice for Sarena and Meldreth's acknowledgment that the debt has been paid — does not break Sir Aemond's curse, but it provides the full account of how and why the curse was laid. Parties who have spoken with Meldreth understand what they are actually dealing with when they meet the Dark Knight.

**Resolution and Failure**

Each quest has a range of possible outcomes from best to worst. Best outcomes contribute to the party's standing at the court assembly. Worst outcomes feed Act IV crisis conditions. Quests that reach their final escalation stage before the party addresses them begin from the worst possible starting position; in the case of the Countess, this represents a complete failure that carries forward as an Act IV crisis regardless of how the party eventually intervenes.

---

### The Magic Economy

There are no magic shops in Haven. A merchant who publicly sold enchanted items in Sanction would face social pressure at minimum and church censure or worse at worst. Items of obvious magical provenance are not traded through ordinary commercial channels.

Magic items enter the party's possession through five sources:

**Crafting.** Characters with appropriate feats and spells may craft items during downtime. Act I offers limited downtime between quests. Act II's winter downtime (Chapter 3) is the primary crafting window; available facilities depend on the party's shelter choice.

**Quest rewards.** Harold II's treasury contains a modest collection of useful items he will offer as appropriate rewards for significant service. Individual nobles and grateful subjects may give items of personal significance.

**Fey gifts.** Meldreth, the Act II fey settlement, and Reinja may offer items as gifts or as formal acknowledgment of an exchange. These items often carry properties tied to their origin and may attract attention in Haven.

**Found items.** Blacktongue has a hoard. Sir Aemond carries equipment connected to his pre-curse identity. The Shrine contains items of historical significance. Each found item has a history and a reason for being where it is.

**Pentad favor.** In exceptional circumstances, a Pentad cleric who trusts the party may provide access to clerical magic or items the church controls. Father Eadric is the most accessible channel for this, with all the unpredictability that implies.

The cost principle governs all of the above: magic in this adventure always has weight. Crafted items require feats, time, and materials. Fey gifts carry obligation. Pentad favor requires relationship. The party should feel that each significant magical item represents a real exchange.

---

### The Historical Revelation Tracker

The true history of Haven is revealed gradually across the campaign. By the time the party reaches the Shrine in Act III, they should have assembled most of this picture through their own investigation. Cuthbert's ghost does not lecture; it confirms and clarifies what they have already learned. The GM should track which revelations the party has received, since the ghost responds to what they know rather than to what they do not.

**Revelation 1: Wotan's Role**
*Available from:* Father Beede's documents (Quest 2, Act I).
*Content:* The original Prelate's account names Wotan as the architect of the exodus — the one who identified Cuthbert, negotiated with the other four gods, and organized the departure. Cuthbert's prayer book contains prayers addressed to Wotan by name, including a passage describing him as "first among our defenders and first among those we have forgotten." Any literate character who reads carefully can draw the conclusion that the church's current theology is a revision.

**Revelation 2: The Displacement**
*Available from:* Fey encounters in Act II; confirmed in Aldenmere (Act III).
*Content:* The goblinoids were living on the western coast when the exile fleet arrived. They were not attacking Haven's ancestors; they were displaced by them. The fey peoples remember this clearly and will state it plainly to any party member who demonstrates willingness to hear it.

**Revelation 3: Cuthbert's Departure**
*Available from:* Signi (Act III).
*Content:* Cuthbert did not leave Haven to continue the quest or to carry the Sword east as part of a plan. He left because he could no longer watch what Haven was becoming. Signi says this as fact, without bitterness.

**Revelation 4: The Sword's Intent**
*Available from:* Reinja (Act III).
*Content:* The Sword was never given to Haven's kings. It went east because Cuthbert carried it, and because he believed that its return to the west should be conditional on the people receiving it being ready to use it properly. The ordeals are his design, transmitted through Reinja, for what "properly" means.

**Revelation 5: What Cuthbert Got Wrong**
*Available from:* Cuthbert's ghost (Act III, after the ordeals).
*Content:* Cuthbert will tell the party, if asked, what he regrets. He did not tell Aleswith's children the full truth before he left. He chose his own grief over his obligations to the people who needed him. He does not ask for absolution. He states what was true and stops.

### The Winter Downtime Framework

During Chapter 3, the mountain pass closes for winter and the party must shelter until spring. The three shelter options produce materially different outcomes.

**Option 1: Dwarven Settlement**

A dwarven mining and garrison community occupies the lower slopes of the eastern range. It is warm, well-supplied, and equipped with smithing facilities that exceed anything in Haven's interior. The dwarves are fair and competent and will accept the party if the party does not make itself a problem.

- Crafting with access to a master-quality forge (+2 to Craft checks; crafting time reduced by one-quarter)
- Equipment repair and upgrade (mundane items)
- Training in dwarven combat techniques (specific martial benefits detailed in Chapter 3)
- Access to dwarven knowledge of the eastern range: maps, hazard information, route options

*Relationships available:* The garrison commander and master smith are both potential ongoing contacts. Dwarven relationships are slow to build and durable once established.

**Option 2: Fey Settlement**

A small fey community occupies a sheltered valley a half-day from the main route. They are aware of the party before the party is aware of them, and their invitation, when it comes, is deliberate. This is the party's first extended contact with the Wild's peoples on the peoples' own terms.

- Magical research and tuition (access to spells and knowledge unavailable in Haven; specifics detailed in Chapter 3)
- Early access to Revelation 2 — the fey will tell the party the history of the displacement if asked sincerely
- Receiving a fey gift if the party's conduct during the winter merits it
- Partial fluency in a fey language (Linguistics check)

*Relationships available:* The settlement's elder and a young elf guide who will accompany the party through the eastern range if trust is established.

**Option 3: Own Camp**

The party shelters independently. No outside assistance, no outside complications, no outside relationships.

- Any character may take a Skill Focus feat or equivalent personal development (GM discretion)
- The GM runs one short solo scene per character during the winter
- The party gains a practical wilderness bonus for the eastern range (+2 to Survival checks east of the mountains)
- Random encounter frequency is doubled

### The Five Ordeals System

The five ordeals in Aldenmere are the structural core of Act III. Each tests a specific quality the Sword's bearer is required to possess. The qualities correspond, not by accident, to the challenges the party faced in Act I.

| Act I Quest | Ordeal | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| The Dragon | The Ordeal of Courage | To act despite fear |
| The Sorceress | The Ordeal of Wisdom | To see truly |
| The Countess | The Ordeal of Strategy | To act rightly under complexity |
| The Priest | The Ordeal of Piety | To hold to what is real |
| The Dark Knight | The Ordeal of Sacrifice | To give what cannot be reclaimed |

The ordeals are not a test of power. A party that approached every Act I quest through force alone will find the ordeals unexpectedly difficult. A party that learned from their Act I encounters — who understood what Meldreth was really asking, who read Father Beede's documents rather than dismissing them, who found some way to honor the challenge of the Dark Knight — will find the ordeals recognize what they bring.

Specific ordeal mechanics are detailed in Chapter 7.

**Access Conditions**

Reinja controls access to the ordeal sites. The party cannot proceed without her sanction. Her assessment takes the form of an extended conversation. She is determining whether the party's engagement with their work in Acts I and II indicates the quality of attention the ordeals require. Parties who have received Historical Revelations and can speak to what they learned will find her more forthcoming. Parties who have not will find her less so — not as punishment, but because she genuinely does not know if they are ready.

### The Act IV Crisis Framework

The state of Haven when the party returns is determined by Act I quest outcomes. The GM should track outcomes using the states below and apply the resulting conditions to Act IV.

*The Countess:*
- **Resolved (early):** Alefrith was exposed at Harbow. The conspiracy is publicly known; the Earl of Waldsea faces a political crisis. If the party discovered Lady Teta's connection to Alefrith, that disclosure creates its own complication at court.
- **Resolved (late):** Alefrith reached Sanction and was exposed there. The assembly was disrupted; Harold's position is slightly weakened but defensible.
- **Failed:** Alefrith struck at the assembly. The forged confessions were presented. Harold is on the defensive against charges of impiety. Some nobles have formally called for a council.

*The Priest:*
- **Beede protected:** The inquisition was blocked. Beede and his movement operate openly. The founding documents are in circulation. Politically complicated but stable.
- **Beede killed by the party:** The movement is leaderless but the documents survive. Significant popular grief; the party's responsibility is remembered.
- **Beede killed by the inquisition:** The movement's martyrdom has made it larger than it was. The inquisition has overplayed its hand. Harold is being pressured from both sides.

*The Dragon:*
- **Resolved:** The Swansea-Kharas border is recovering. The party's deed is known and credited.
- **Unresolved:** Blacktongue is still active. Border settlement has collapsed; refugees have moved inland. The Earl of Kharas is furious.

*The Sorceress:*
- **Justice achieved:** Sir Odda is publicly exposed and his title is forfeit. Meldreth has returned to her glade. The party has a fey contact who may provide access to fey networks in Act II.
- **Meldreth defeated:** Meldreth was killed or her magic broken. Sir Odda is untouched. Fey-Haven relations on the Umbra border have worsened; this complicates Act II travel.
- **Unresolved:** Sir Odda is cursed, cannot remove his sword, and is becoming erratic. The Countess of Umbra is managing a situation that has no clean resolution.

*The Dark Knight:*
- **Resolved (honorably):** Sir Aemond recovers his identity and memory. He is now the king's cousin, disgraced but alive, a complex political figure in Act IV. Harold II is privately shaken.
- **Resolved (dishonorably):** The curse regenerates. Aemond returns. He appears in Act IV, still cursed, at a politically terrible moment. The party knows this is possible if they spoke with Meldreth.
- **Unresolved:** The Dark Knight is still active. Knights have stopped patrolling the Tana–Falkrith road. Both Earls have formally complained to the Crown.

---

### Managing the Endings

The adventure has four possible endings, determined by the party's disposition of the Sword of the Redeemer. The GM should not steer toward any particular ending; the design assumes parties may arrive at any of the four depending on what they learned, what they chose, and what story they want to be part of.

**Ending 1: The Sword Goes to Harold II**

The party returns the Sword to the Crown. Harold accepts it with the gravity the moment deserves. He uses it to consolidate his position with the nobility and addresses the Act IV crises from a position of unprecedented strength.

Harold II is a good king by the standards available in Haven. He will pursue fey peace, address the foundational injustice of the goblinoid displacement imperfectly and slowly but sincerely, and stabilize the kingdom. The party founds a royal chivalric order — the first in Haven's history. Harold names it, and he will ask the party what they would like to call it.

This ending is satisfying and does not fully resolve Haven's underlying problems. Harold is one man. The institutions he governs are unchanged. Cuthbert understood this.

**Ending 2: The Sword Goes to a Noble**

The party gives the Sword to one of Haven's Earls. This requires a specific noble — one who has demonstrated both the competence to lead and the integrity to use legitimate power legitimately. The GM should develop this figure before Act IV, guided by the party's relationships with individual Earls across Acts I and II.

The result is a substantial constraint on royal authority without its elimination. Whether this produces a better or worse outcome than Ending 1 depends on the specific Earl and the specific circumstances. The party founds a neutral knightly order, independent of both Crown and nobility.

**Ending 3: The Party Keeps the Sword**

The party acts as agents of the common people rather than of the Crown or nobility. This ending is available only if Father Beede's movement (or its legacy) has sufficient momentum, the party has built meaningful relationships with common people across Acts I and II, and the party is willing to lead rather than advise.

The confrontation this ending requires is not guaranteed to succeed. The Sword provides legitimacy; the party provides nerve; the common people provide numbers. Whether it is enough depends on preparation and the GM's assessment of the Act IV crisis state. This ending is the hardest and the most open.

**Ending 4: Open**

The party finds a resolution not covered by the three endings above. The GM should treat this as a collaborative opportunity: what does the party actually want to happen, and how does the Sword help or complicate it? The five gods are watching. Cuthbert's ghost spoke. What the party does with that information is theirs to determine.

## Chapter 1: The Crown's Service

*Act I | Levels 1–5*

---

### Overview

Chapter 1 encompasses all of Act I: the party's introduction to Haven, their commissioning by Harold II, and the five-quest pool that constitutes their service to the Crown before the Sword Quest begins. The chapter ends at the midsummer court assembly in Sanction, where Harold formally names the party's order and commissions them east.

The chapter's structure is unusual: a fixed opening, a fixed close, and a flexible middle made up of five quests in whatever order the party chooses. The interconnections between quests reward parties who investigate carefully. The escalation tracks punish parties who delay without purpose.

**The Quest Pool's Themes**

The five Act I quests each foreshadow one of the five ordeals in Act III. This correspondence is not something the party will discover for some time, but the GM should hold it in mind: a party that approaches the Countess quest as a test of strategic thinking, the Priest quest as a test of genuine piety, and the Dark Knight quest as a test of what one person is willing to give up alone has been learning the Sword's requirements without knowing it.

---

### Scene 1: The Summons

Harold II does not issue an official call for heroes. He sends personal messages — carried by discreet intermediaries, specific in content. Each message references something the recipient has actually done: not a rumor, not a reputation, but a specific act that someone in Harold's network witnessed or reliably reported. The message does not explain what is wanted. It extends an invitation to Sanction, at the recipient's earliest convenience, to speak privately with someone in a position to make their efforts worthwhile.

Party members arrive in Sanction independently or in small groups. The GM should establish how they know each other, if at all, before this scene; the audience with Harold works best if the party has at minimum a prior acquaintance. An intermediary — a royal household staff member distinguished only by efficiency — brings them to a small meeting chamber off Tintavel's main gallery, not the throne room.

> *The room is less than you expected. A table, six chairs, a fire that has been burning long enough to warm the stone. Maps on one wall — the kingdom, the eastern territories, the mountain range between them. A window looking out over the lake. Two men are already present: one large and unexpectedly cheerful, who introduces himself as Father Eadric, confessor to the king; and one who introduces himself simply as Harold.*

Harold II stands when they enter, which is not required of him. He shakes hands rather than receiving them formally. He asks their names and uses them.

**Harold II in This Scene**

Harold does not dissemble. He explains his position in plain terms: Haven is divided; the rebellion left wounds that have not healed; he needs something the Crown does not currently possess — a source of prestige and popular legitimacy independent of the nobility's goodwill and the church's endorsement. The Sword of the Redeemer, recovered by a company of knights the Crown can credibly claim to have sponsored, would be that thing.

Before he can send anyone east, he has five problems in the earldoms that his official apparatus cannot address without political complications. He needs people who can act on his authority without that authority being visible. He describes each problem briefly (see Quest Briefings below). He then makes his proposal: resolve these, and he will sponsor them as the founding members of a new royal order of knights, to be named at the midsummer court assembly when all of Haven's nobility will be present to witness it. The Sword Quest begins the morning after the assembly.

He does not pretend this is simple. He will answer honest questions honestly. If asked whether he is using them, he will say yes, and explain why he believes the use is justified.

**Father Eadric in This Scene**

Eadric is present throughout and speaks occasionally — to add context Harold has omitted, or to break tension with a well-timed observation that is usually funnier than it has any right to be. He is there as Harold's advisor and as his judge of character. If the party treats the conversation as a genuine exchange rather than a performance, Eadric will visibly warm to them. If they perform, he will not say so, but the room will feel slightly colder.

After the formal business is concluded, if the party remains to talk, Eadric will engage them individually. He knows things about all five quest situations that Harold's briefing did not include, and he will share them with people he thinks can use the information properly.

**The Naming Question**

Harold tells them the order's name will be announced at the assembly, and that he intends to consult them about it. If they ask now, he has ideas but wants to hear theirs first. He is genuinely open to suggestions. The name should reflect the order's purpose — service to Haven, the quest for the Sword, or the specific character of the party. This can remain an open question until the assembly scene at the chapter's close.

---

### Quest Briefings

Harold provides the following information on each situation.

**The Countess.** A noblewoman recently arrived in Waldsea — Lady Alefrith, by origin from a prominent Cant family with strong church connections, now wife to the Earl of Waldsea — has been associating with a company of zealous witch-hunters who operate outside the church's formal structure. This alone is unremarkable. What concerns Harold is that several of this company have passed through Sanction in the last season, and his household staff have observed them taking careful note of the court's composition. He does not know what she is planning. He needs someone to find out, and if necessary to prevent it.

**The Priest.** In Barstow, a former monk named Father Beede has come into possession of founding-era documents and has begun arguing that the Pentad's current theology misrepresents Wotan's proper place in the pantheon. The church's inquisition has noticed. Harold has read the documents — Father Eadric obtained copies — and finds them credible and politically alarming. He does not tell the party what to do about Beede. He says only that the situation will become unmanageable if it escalates, and that the party should understand all its dimensions before acting.

**The Dragon.** A black dragon has established itself in the border territory between Swansea and Kharas and has been burning farmsteads. The Crown's military apparatus is available but politically fraught: deploying royal forces into an earldom's territory without the Earl's formal invitation requires justifications that both the Earl of Swansea and the Earl of Kharas find inconvenient for different reasons. The party, operating as private agents, has no such constraint.

**The Sorceress.** An elven woman of considerable power has appeared in the town of Belham in Umbra and made public accusations against a local knight, Sir Odda, claiming he murdered a half-elf craftsperson named Sarena. Sir Odda has denied the accusations and suppressed local investigation. The Countess of Umbra, whose goodwill Harold values, has asked for assistance in resolving the situation before it becomes something she cannot manage.

**The Dark Knight.** A knight in black armor has been traveling the roads between Tana and Falkrith, issuing formal challenges to any knight he encounters, and killing those who accept. He will not speak his name and cannot remove his visor. Three knights are dead so far. The Earl of Tana has raised the matter formally. Harold does not know who the knight is. He says, with some care, that the proper response to a cursed knight wandering one's roads is not always obvious.

### Quest 1: The Countess

*The Test of Strategy — Level 1–2*

---

**Situation**

Lady Alefrith of Waldsea is twenty-six years old, recently married to the Earl of Waldsea following the death of his first wife, and in the process of executing the first stage of a conspiracy that, if successful, would destabilize Harold II's court at the worst possible moment. She is methodical, patient, and operating from a position of genuine ideological conviction: she believes the king is impious, that his appointment of the Magister is an affront to the Pentad, and that the nobility — specifically the nobility of the rebel earldoms — has both the right and the obligation to check him.

The conspiracy has three stages. The party will encounter the first: Alefrith's witch hunters are gathering intelligence on the court's composition and identifying minor court figures susceptible to coercion. The second stage — kidnapping those figures, forcing confessions, and returning to Sanction with fabricated evidence of Harold's impiety and adultery — is already in planning. The third stage, whatever it might be, is not yet developed in any form the party can discover.

**Lady Teta's Secret**

Lady Alefrith is the stepmother of Lady Teta — the two are close enough in age that the relationship is something that requires a moment to process when first explained, though it is not a secret. What is secret is this: they are lovers. Teta knows about the conspiracy. She has been providing Alefrith with information from inside the court — the same intelligence that Harold's staff noticed being gathered. She is a witting participant.

She is also entirely prepared to denounce Alefrith if the conspiracy becomes a liability. This is not something she would need to be provoked into; it is a contingency she has already considered and accepted as necessary. She does not regard this as a betrayal. She regards it as politics.

The GM should determine how much of this the party can discover. Evidence of Teta's involvement exists but requires active investigation of Alefrith's correspondence at Harbow; it will not surface from investigating Alefrith's witch hunters alone. If the party confronts Teta with this evidence, she will deny it with complete composure. If they have incontrovertible proof, she will arrange a private conversation in which she offers them something they want in exchange for their discretion — and deliver on that offer. She is not foolish enough to make enemies unnecessarily.

**Key NPCs**

*Lady Alefrith.* The Countess of Waldsea is organized, precise, and genuinely devout in the mode that produces the most political difficulty: she believes what she believes, has thought about it carefully, and is not acting out of ambition alone but out of a considered view that Harold II is dangerous to the kingdom's spiritual health. She is not a cartoon villain. She is wrong about the king and right about the church, and the party will find her frustratingly coherent if they bother to speak with her. She will not surrender or confess willingly; she will, if cornered, attempt to negotiate from principle.

*The Earl of Waldsea.* He is being used more than he is a participant. He knows his wife has strong views about the king's impiety and has heard about the witch hunters in general terms; he does not know the specifics of the conspiracy. He is a man whose first wife's death left him less capable of managing his household than he was before, and Alefrith married into that gap.

*The Witch Hunters.* A company of twelve, led by a capable former soldier named Aldwyn who believes in what he is doing. They are dangerous in a fight and more dangerous as investigators — they are skilled at finding leverage on people. They are not officially connected to the inquisition. Letters found in Alefrith's correspondence at Harbow show that Aldwyn and Father Marne have exchanged communications; the tone suggests professional acquaintance rather than direction.

**Key Location: Harbow**

Harbow is a trading town in Waldsea's flat grasslands — large enough to have a market, a garrison, and a reasonable inn, small enough that strangers are noticed. Alefrith has established her base here while the Earl is away on earldom business. The witch hunters are quartered separately, at the edge of town.

The party can find Alefrith at the Earl's Harbow residence (a secondary household, not a castle — a substantial manor with a small household staff). Her correspondence is kept in a locked study. Aldwyn and his company are accessible at the edge of town; they are not friendly, but they have not been given orders to interfere with travelers.

**Escalation and Timing**

At Stage 1 (intervals 0–1), Alefrith is at Harbow, her witch hunters are assembled, and the plan is in preparation. She has not yet departed. The party can confront her here, investigate her correspondence, and neutralize the threat before it moves.

At Stage 2 (intervals 2–3), Alefrith and the witch hunters are on the road to Sanction. They travel as a minor noble's retinue — plausible, unremarkable. The party can intercept them on the processional road or in a waystation town. Alefrith's correspondence is with her; harder to access but not impossible.

At Stage 3 (interval 4+), they have arrived in Sanction ahead of the assembly. The witch hunters are distributed through the city under cover. The plan is in motion. Neutralizing this without disrupting the assembly requires surgical precision; a public confrontation at this stage risks giving Alefrith exactly the audience she wants.

**Resolution Outcomes**

*Best outcome:* Alefrith's conspiracy is exposed at Harbow before she reaches Sanction. Evidence is recovered; the connection to the inquisition is documented. The Earl of Waldsea is informed and cooperates. Teta's connection either was not discovered or was handled discreetly. Harold II's court is protected; the earl faces political consequences but the party has not created a public crisis.

*Acceptable outcome:* Alefrith is stopped on the road or at Sanction before she strikes. Evidence is partially recovered. Some degree of public disruption occurs but is contained. Harold's position is not seriously weakened.

*Worst outcome (failure):* The assembly occurs with Alefrith's conspiracy intact or only partially exposed. Forged confessions are presented. The party arrives at the Act I close to a court already in crisis.

**Act IV Implications**

If the party discovered and can demonstrate Lady Teta's involvement, Harold II must grapple with the fact that one of his paramours — the politically advantageous one, the one whose family represents a rebel earldom he needs — was providing intelligence to a conspiracy against his court. What he does with this information, if it reaches him, is one of the most complex decisions he will face in Act IV. The party will need to decide whether to tell him.

### Quest 2: The Priest

*The Test of Piety — Levels 2–3*

---

**Situation**

Father Beede's Trickster movement is not yet a crisis. It is becoming one. The founding documents he has recovered and begun circulating are theologically credible — credible enough that even the church's own scholars, if they engage honestly with the text, cannot dismiss them — and the inquisition's response so far has been to treat them as a threat to institutional authority rather than a question to be answered on its merits. This is the correct institutional response to an incorrect institutional position, and the resulting dynamic is accelerating.

The two documents are:

*The Prelate's Account.* Written by the church's first Prelate during Haven's founding generation, this text describes the theology of the Pentad as Cuthbert and his advisors understood it. Wotan appears by name and in detail — as the god who initiated the exodus, who chose Cuthbert personally, and who was understood to be the first among equals in the pantheon's early years. The text also describes, in passing, how the other four gods viewed Wotan's relationship with Cuthbert as an imbalance that should be corrected in time. The implication that the correction happened, and that it was deliberate, is available to any careful reader.

*Cuthbert's Prayer Book.* A collection of prayers recorded by Cuthbert's court historian, described in a prefatory note as "prayers spoken by the king in private, set down for preservation." Several are addressed directly to Wotan by name; one includes the line "first among our defenders, first among those we have forgotten." The historian's note at the end observes, without editorial comment, that this prayer was recorded in the king's final year in Haven.

These documents do not say that the church is lying. They say that something was true and is no longer acknowledged as true. The distinction matters to Father Beede; it matters less to Father Marne.

**Key NPCs**

*Father Beede.* See his entry in Key NPCs (The Realm section). In this quest, Beede is operating from a monastery in Barstow's lower district — not his home monastery, which declined to shelter him after the documents became public, but a smaller community of Trickster-sympathetic brothers who have offered him space and are now experiencing the consequences of that decision.

He is not afraid of the inquisition in any way the party can detect. He has thought about the risk carefully and concluded that the documents are more important than his comfort, and possibly more important than his life. He will tell the party this if asked. He will not be dramatic about it.

*Father Marne.* The inquisitor assigned to the Beede case is forty-two years old, thin, meticulous, and operating under institutional pressure from the Archbishop of Barstow that has narrowed his options considerably. He is not personally cruel; he is professionally capable and politically constrained. He believes Beede's movement is genuinely dangerous — not because he thinks the documents are false, but because he understands what accepting them as true would require the church to acknowledge, and he has decided that the institution's stability matters more than the specific theological question.

The party can deal with Marne through confrontation, negotiation, or leverage. Evidence of his communication with Alefrith's witch hunters (available only if the party obtained it in Quest 1) gives them a tool that Marne will find highly inconvenient: he has allowed an informal relationship between inquisitorial personnel and a noble conspiracy, which is well outside his mandate. He will not thank the party for having this information, but he will respond to its existence.

**Key Location: Barstow**

Barstow is the largest city in Haven by most measures — larger than Sanction, more commercial, more cosmopolitan, and organized around the Pentad's institutional headquarters in the way that cities organized around such structures tend to be: the church is the largest landowner, the largest employer, and the most significant architectural presence. The Archbishop's palace is a building that says something deliberate about what the church thinks it is. Father Marne operates from within it.

Beede's de facto base is in the Weavers' District, a lower-slope neighborhood whose residents have no strong church affiliation and considerable sympathy for anyone who makes the Archbishop's life complicated. The movement has significant popular support in Barstow; this is not invisible.

**Escalation and Timing**

At Stage 1, Beede is circulating copies of the documents and speaking to gatherings of common people, tradespeople, and minor clergy. The inquisition has him under surveillance but has not acted. Marne is receiving instructions.

At Stage 2, the inquisition has filed formal heresy charges. Beede and several movement members have been summoned to appear before a church tribunal. Beede has not complied. Several members have been arrested for failure to appear; a larger number have gone to ground. The city is paying attention.

At Stage 3, an attempted arrest of Beede has produced a street confrontation — not a planned riot, but the kind of thing that happens when a crowd decides it has seen enough. Several members of the movement are in inquisitorial custody awaiting trial. Barstow's common people are in a state of active unrest. If the party does not intervene, Father Beede is killed in a subsequent attempt — either by inquisitorial violence or in the chaos of the escalating confrontation.

**Resolution Outcomes**

*Best outcome:* The heresy charges are dropped or made unenforceable. Beede is alive and the documents are in circulation. Marne's connection to Alefrith's network has been documented and used as leverage, potentially ending his effective ability to pursue the case. The founding documents reach a wider audience; this is the first substantial step toward public awareness of Wotan's proper role.

*Acceptable outcome:* The immediate arrests are reversed and the trial is delayed or abandoned. Beede is alive but the movement is operating under significant pressure. The documents are preserved.

*Worst outcome:* Beede is killed — whether by the party's decision or by the inquisition's action. The movement continues without him, larger and angrier. If killed by the party: the party returns in Act IV to common people who know what they did and have not forgotten it. If killed by the inquisition: the party failed to prevent an institutional murder and will be assessed accordingly by every common person who hears of it.

### Quest 3: The Dragon

*The Test of Courage — Levels 3–4*

---

**Situation**

The creature the border settlements call Blacktongue is a young adult black dragon that established its lair in the brackish wetlands between Swansea and Kharas approximately two years ago. It did not initially prey on settlements — its territory overlapped with farmland only at its margins, and the early losses were to livestock rather than people. It has since grown bolder as its territory has grown, and in the past season has begun burning the farmsteads it previously only raided. No one has attempted to communicate with it. No one has attempted to kill it. The local response has been evacuation.

The dragon's name, as far as anyone knows, is whatever it calls itself — which is unknown, because no one has asked. The locals call it Blacktongue for its habit of tasting the air as it circles before striking, which is a thing all black dragons do but which the survivors of its attacks find specifically unnerving.

**Key Location: The Fen**

Blacktongue's lair is a half-collapsed stone structure — the remnant of a border fortification from an earlier century, now half-submerged in the fen — approximately twelve miles east of the last intact farmstead. The fen is navigable but unpleasant: ankle-deep in most places, chest-deep in others, with soft ground that swallows wrong steps. The dragon knows its terrain. The party should be made to feel this.

The lair itself is accessible through a partially flooded ground-level entrance or through a collapsed section of roof. The dragon keeps its hoard in the highest dry chamber — a second-floor room whose floor has partially given way, with the hoard stacked against the intact portion.

**The Encounter**

Blacktongue is CR-appropriate for a party of four at 3rd–4th level. The GM should consult standard black dragon statistics for an individual of appropriate age and adjust the encounter difficulty to match the party's composition. The fen's terrain complicates the approach; the dragon's acid breath, darkness, and comfort in water are advantages it will use.

The dragon is not interested in negotiation. It is territorial, confident, and has not encountered anything that has given it reason to be otherwise. A party that approaches openly will be treated as prey; a party that approaches with sufficient subtlety may earn a brief exchange before the combat begins. The dragon is not stupid, but it is young enough to be arrogant, and a party that demonstrates genuine capability — forcing it below half hit points before it can disengage — will find it suddenly more interested in conversation than it was at the opening.

*If the party negotiates:* Blacktongue's primary interest is territory. It has been expanding because expansion works. A credible alternative — an arrangement that gives it a territory boundary it has reason to respect — is something it can understand. This is not a simple resolution, and it does not constitute full success, but it represents a meaningful outcome for a party that approached the situation thoughtfully.

**Escalation**

For each quest interval at Stage 2 or beyond, one additional settlement has been burned. The GM should track this concretely: named farms and villages, some with families the party has encountered. The dragon becomes slightly harder to negotiate with for each settlement burned, not because it is more powerful but because it has been reinforced in the view that what it is doing works.

**The Hoard**

Blacktongue's hoard is the product of two years of raiding and includes a collection of items taken from farmsteads, a modest quantity of coin looted from the garrison post it destroyed last spring, and one item of genuine significance: a small chest containing correspondence between the previous garrison commander and the Earl of Kharas's office, discussing the dragon's presence and the decision to conceal information about its escalation from Sanction. The Earl of Kharas was trying to manage the problem quietly and failed. This is a minor diplomatic complication with Act IV relevance if the party chooses to use it.

**Resolution Outcomes**

*Best outcome:* The dragon is killed. Border settlement can resume; reconstruction is possible before winter. The party's deed is known.

*Acceptable outcome:* A territorial arrangement is negotiated. The burning stops; settlement reconstruction begins cautiously. The dragon remains a long-term concern but is not an immediate crisis.

*Worst outcome:* The party fails to engage or the dragon is not stopped. Additional settlements are destroyed in the party's absence. The border is effectively abandoned.

### Quest 4: The Sorceress

*The Test of Wisdom — Level 4*

---

**Situation**

Meldreth is not what Haven's people mean when they say sorceress. She is not young, not driven by ambition, not pursuing power as an end. She is ancient in the way elves are ancient — present in these forests before the exile fleet landed, acquainted with the history of the displacement from the inside, and now, for the first time in centuries, in direct contact with Haven's human institutions because of something they did to someone she loved.

Sarena was a half-elf smith who lived in Belham, a mid-sized town in Umbra, known throughout the region for her work. She was commissioned by Sir Odda, a local knight of moderate holdings, to forge a custom sword. The work took longer than his patience. When she presented the finished blade and insisted on the agreed price, he accused her of cheating him on the time, killed her in the argument that followed, and disposed of her body in the river. When the body was found downstream some weeks later, he used his position to redirect the investigation toward fey involvement.

Meldreth came for Sarena's body. She carried it to a glade she has kept in the forest above Belham for longer than anyone in the town has been alive, and she wept over it for thirty days and thirty nights. On the thirty-first day, Sarena's spirit appeared to her and told her the truth of her death.

Meldreth wants Sir Odda publicly exposed. She does not want private justice or private satisfaction. She wants the people of Belham — and the Countess of Umbra's court, which allowed his cover story to stand — to know publicly what he did and to acknowledge it. She made her opening move in Belham because that is where it needs to happen: in the town where Sarena lived, in front of the people who accepted Sir Odda's story.

**The Curse on Sir Odda**

If the party reaches Stage 3 of Meldreth's escalation track without having confronted Sir Odda, she kidnaps several local children from Belham and sends word to him: come out and face her accusation publicly, or she will not return them. She is not threatening the children's lives — they are kept safely in her glade — but she is willing to hold them indefinitely. If Sir Odda responds and faces the public confrontation, she releases them immediately. If he refuses, she releases the children regardless and turns to a different instrument.

**The Curse on Sir Odda**

When Sir Odda fails to respond to the kidnapping, Meldreth lays a curse on him and releases the children. The curse has two components: he cannot win any contest of arms while carrying the sword Sarena made — the same sword he killed her over — and he cannot be parted from it willingly or unwillingly. This is not a curse of power; it is a curse of testimony. The sword that marked Sarena's death will not leave his hand, and in any contest of arms that hand holds it, he will not prevail.

The curse makes Sir Odda unmanageable. He cannot be tried in ordinary courts — he cannot surrender a weapon — and he cannot win any physical confrontation that would allow him to be restrained. He is becoming a problem the Countess of Umbra cannot solve by ordinary means.

**Key NPCs**

*Meldreth.* Ancient, controlled, in a state of grief that has had time to resolve into something quieter and more purposeful than rage. She speaks precisely and expects precision in return. She is not hostile to the party by default — they are humans from Haven, which is a category she has complicated feelings about, but she is capable of distinguishing between the category and the individuals. Parties that approach her as if she is a problem to be solved will get a response calibrated to that assumption. Parties that approach her as if she is a person with a legitimate grievance will get something different.

She knows about Sir Aemond. She will not volunteer this information, but she will answer a direct question: yes, she laid a curse on a knight who came to her seeking power against the Young King, because she found him unworthy of power and decided she could use his ambition for her own purposes. She describes this without apology. She will not tell the party how to break the curse; she will tell them what the curse requires: that it will not release until it is satisfied, which is a different kind of answer.

*Sir Odda.* A minor knight of forty, physically capable, politically connected enough to have made his cover story work for several months. He is not brave, exactly — he is the kind of man who commits violence impulsively and then manages its aftermath with considerable competence. He genuinely believes that his position entitles him to have been right about the price. He has not told himself a story in which he is a murderer; he has told himself a story in which he made a reasonable business decision and a fey agitator is making trouble about it.

*The Countess of Umbra.* She did not rebel; she values Harold's goodwill; she wants this resolved. She is willing to cooperate with the party if they approach her properly and does not require much persuasion to act against Sir Odda — she has no particular loyalty to him — but she needs a basis in evidence. She cannot act on Meldreth's accusations alone without acknowledging fey testimony as valid, which creates precedents she is not prepared to set. Give her evidence, and she will use it.

**Key Location: The Glade**

Meldreth's glade is approximately two hours on foot above Belham, up a slope of old forest that becomes noticeably quieter and more attentive as the party ascends. The glade itself is centered on a specific oak and a specific stone; Sarena's body is no longer there — her spirit has departed — but the site retains the quality of somewhere that mourning has been done with complete seriousness. Meldreth lives in the forest nearby, not in a structure. She will be aware of the party's approach before they arrive.

**Finding the Evidence**

The evidence of Sir Odda's guilt exists and is recoverable through investigation.

- *Witnesses:* Two people saw the argument: a stablehand at the commission site who heard raised voices and saw Sarena alive and Sir Odda alone shortly before her disappearance, and a fisherwoman downstream who observed a bundled shape thrown from a bridge at night and noted Sir Odda's banner on the horse. Neither has come forward; both can be found with effort.
- *The sword itself:* Sarena's work is identifiable to any skilled smith who examines it. The method of construction and her specific techniques are distinct. A craftsperson consulted in Belham or Dalkeith will recognize her work immediately and can testify to it.
- *Sir Odda's correspondence:* A letter in his possession from a church contact in Belham thanks him for his "generous contribution" to the local shrine and speaks of "the difficulties with the foreign woman" having been properly closed. The phrasing is careful but the implication is not.

**Resolution Outcomes**

*Best outcome:* Sir Odda is publicly exposed — in Belham, in the Countess of Umbra's court, or both. His title and lands are forfeit. Meldreth acknowledges that the debt has been paid. She returns to her glade; the kidnapped children (if Stage 3 was reached) are released. The party has a fey contact of genuine capability who may provide access to fey networks during Act II.

*Acceptable outcome:* Sir Odda is tried and convicted through a process that does not achieve the public exposure Meldreth wanted but produces legal consequences. She accepts this as partial satisfaction but does not consider the debt fully paid; her disposition toward Haven does not improve significantly.

*Worst outcome:* The party defeats Meldreth in combat. Sir Odda is untouched. Fey-Haven relations on the Umbra border worsen measurably. The party has demonstrated to the fey that Haven handles fey grievances by eliminating the one expressing them. This complicates Act II travel and closes the channel through which Meldreth could have provided intelligence about Sir Aemond.

### Quest 5: The Dark Knight

*The Test of Sacrifice — Level 4*

---

**Situation**

The knight in black armor has no name, as far as anyone in Tana or Falkrith can determine. He will not speak it. He cannot remove his helm. He rides a pale horse, issues formal challenges in the archaic language of the old knightly tradition, and has killed four knights by the time the party receives its commission.

He is Sir Aemond — the king's elder cousin, a man who was once considered a potential successor to Harold II's grandfather before Harold's grandfather made his selection, who took that exclusion poorly, and who rode to the border of the Wild in the first year of Harold's reign to find someone who could give him the power to take what he believed was his. He found Meldreth. She listened to his request, assessed his quality, found him lacking in every dimension that the thing he wanted required, and cursed him instead. She did not hate him specifically. She saw an instrument of disruption available at low cost and used it.

The curse has five components: he cannot reveal his identity; he cannot remove his armor; he cannot stop issuing challenges to knights he encounters on the road; he possesses supernatural strength that makes him exceptionally difficult to defeat in combat; and he neither sleeps nor eats nor drinks, sustained by the curse against his body's ordinary needs. He also cannot form new memories or recall who he is — each day begins for him in the same place it always begins, with no accumulated experience of what has come before. He is trapped in the performance of knightly challenge, without rest and without time, for as long as the curse holds, which is until he is defeated in honorable one-on-one combat.

**What "Honorable" Means**

The curse's condition is specific. The defeat must be achieved through direct, unassisted, single combat: one challenger against Sir Aemond, without magical enhancement from outside the duel, without other party members intervening. This is not a condition the party can finesse by having one member fight while others cast preparatory spells the evening before — the curse recognizes the spirit of the requirement, not only its letter.

If the party attempts to circumvent this — attacking en masse, using ongoing magical effects from outside the circle, having another party member intervene to prevent a killing blow — the curse does not break. Aemond's wounds heal. He mounts his horse and departs. He will appear again at the midsummer court assembly in Sanction, still cursed, at the most politically damaging possible moment: with every noble in Haven present to witness him challenging knights in the castle courtyard, and with the party unable to explain who he is without disclosing that Harold's cousin came to a fey sorceress seeking the power to depose him.

The party may know this in advance if they spoke honestly with Meldreth about the curse she laid on a knight who sought power against the king. She will confirm it on direct questioning. Whether they have this information when they first encounter Aemond is determined entirely by whether they addressed Quest 4 first and addressed it well.

**The Challenge**

The knight issues his challenge in archaic formal language from horseback, one hand on his sword, waiting. He will wait as long as he needs to. He will not attack unless the challenge is accepted. If the party refuses the challenge and attempts to leave, he will follow them until the challenge is met. He is not permitted by the curse's terms to attack without an accepted challenge; this is not mercy but mechanism.

The combat itself is genuinely dangerous at 4th level. Sir Aemond was a capable knight before the curse, and the curse has had months to reinforce the only thing he is still able to do. The party member who accepts the challenge should go in prepared; the GM should not pull the encounter's difficulty. The Test of Sacrifice is named for what it costs, not for what it spares.

**After the Duel**

When defeated legitimately, the curse breaks. Sir Aemond's visor opens. He is a man in late middle age — thin, exhausted, clearly having been inside that armor for some time — who looks at the person who defeated him and then looks at his hands as if he is not entirely certain whose they are.

He does not immediately know where he is in time. His last clear memory is Meldreth's forest. He was told he would be released when he was defeated by someone worthy. He understands what has happened but needs a moment to fully arrive in it.

He knows he is the king's cousin. He knows what he went to Meldreth to request. He will not deny either of these things if asked directly. Whether the party tells Harold II what they have learned, how they tell him, and what they do with Aemond in the interim are decisions the adventure leaves entirely to them.

**Escalation**

The Dark Knight's kill count advances with each quest interval. The road between Heathclyde and Landing — the main route between Tana and Falkrith — has become effectively impassable for any party that includes a knight. Local lords are considering abandoning knight errantry for the duration. By Stage 3, six knights are dead and the Earl of Tana has sent a formal letter demanding royal action.

**Resolution Outcomes**

*Best outcome:* Aemond is defeated honorably. The curse breaks. The party recovers the king's cousin, makes informed decisions about disclosure, and arrives at the court assembly without a cursed knight loose in the kingdom. Harold II learns the truth, or does not, on the party's terms.

*Acceptable outcome:* Aemond is defeated but the party chooses not to disclose his identity at the court assembly. He is returned to some form of custody or supervision. The situation is managed but not resolved.

*Worst outcome:* The curse is not broken, or the party bypassed it and the regeneration condition has triggered. Aemond appears at the court assembly still cursed. The party must explain what they know and how they know it in front of the assembled nobility of Haven.

### Scene 2: The Court Assembly

The midsummer court assembly is the first year since the end of the rebellion that all nine Earls have agreed to participate. Harold II has spent months creating the conditions for this attendance; it represents the most significant moment of political repair in his reign to date. The assembly runs for three days, and the three days are designed to do different work. The first creates an occasion no one can easily refuse. The second builds goodwill through spectacle and competition. The third conducts the kingdom's actual business from a position of established warmth. This is deliberate, and the Earls who have been to court before know it — but they come anyway, because Harold has set the terms well and refusing would be the more conspicuous choice.

---

**Day One: The Opening Feast**

The guests of the assembly arrive throughout the morning and are received by Harold's household staff with precise attention to rank. The Earls and their retinues are installed in the guest quarters of Tintavel and in the better houses along the lake's western edge. The common city — which has been anticipating this occasion for weeks — fills the streets with the organized chaos of a holiday: market stalls, street performers, the smell of roasting meat and early summer flowers.

The feast begins at sunset.

> *The great hall of Tintavel, which in ordinary hours has the character of late afternoon and the smell of old stone, has been transformed. Hundreds of candles burn along the walls and from chandeliers of wrought iron lowered close enough to illuminate faces. The tables are set end to end the hall's full length, covered in white linen, loaded with plate. At the far end, beneath the royal standard, Harold II sits with his household and his most trusted guests.*
>
> *Then the first apprentices enter — six young men and women in the grey robes of the Magister's service, taking positions along both walls. At a signal no one quite sees, they begin.*
>
> *The room fills with light that has no source. A great stag made of green and silver radiance steps between the tables and walks the hall's length with the unhurried dignity of a creature that has never been afraid of anything. Behind it come other shapes — a fleet of ships in miniature, sails spread, tacking through a wind only they can feel; a flock of summer birds that scatter upward into the vaulted ceiling and disappear; a tree that grows from nothing in the center of the hall, flowering, fruiting, shedding its leaves, and standing winter-bare, all in the span of a minute. The Earls watch. Some of them do not let their expressions change. Others cannot help themselves.*
>
> *When the courses begin arriving, it becomes clear that the kitchens have been given instructions to compete with the spectacle. A pie the size of a cartwheel is carried in by four servants and set before the high table. The head cook produces a knife, cuts, and a dozen white doves burst upward in a rush of wings and feathers, circling the hall three times before making their way out through the high windows. No one speaks for a moment. Then a pastry swan is carried in behind it — nearly three feet from beak to tail, its feathers glazed to a high shine, its eyes made of some dark preserved fruit — and the hall finds its voice again.*
>
> *And then Fenna sings.*
>
> *The royal court bard is not easy to look away from under ordinary circumstances: she is tall, copper-skinned, with the particular quality of stillness that performers cultivate and most never fully achieve. The small horns at her temples, the tail that moves with independent attention — these things matter less, by the time the first note has settled into the room, than the fact of the voice itself. It is not the largest voice the assembled guests have ever heard. It is the most precisely beautiful one. The hall quiets completely. The candles burn straight. The doves are still in the rafters above.*
>
> *For the length of the song, the assembly is not nine fractious earldoms and a king who won a contested throne by attrition. It is a kingdom.*

Fenna performs three times across the evening, each set lasting twenty to thirty minutes and separated by conversation, additional courses, and the managed chaos of servants replenishing wine. She does not speak to the assembly between performances; she performs and then withdraws to a position near the back of the hall where she watches the room with an attention that is clearly professional and possibly personal.

She is, in Haven, genuinely unusual: tieflings exist but are uncommon, and uncommon things at the royal court invite comment. Her presence in Harold's household reflects his general indifference to categorizing people by lineage rather than capacity — and, as Father Eadric has observed, "her voice alone is worth any three knights in terms of its effect on the mood of a difficult room."

*The party is seated at the assembly. Their placement reflects their status: guests of distinction, but not yet of rank. The table is good, the service attentive, and they are watched — by other guests who want to assess what the king's new agents actually are, and by those who already have opinions and are looking for confirmation. The Magister, visible at Harold's left hand for the length of the feast, does not look at them. Or does not appear to.*

---

**Day Two: Games and Celebration**

The second day belongs to competition and display, and it is structured to give every Earl's retinue something to distinguish itself in.

The martial games begin in the early afternoon on the grounds south of Tintavel, where temporary lists have been set up for jousting and a cleared field established for archery and wrestling. The jousting is the day's centerpiece: twenty-four knights in the lists, two elimination rounds across the afternoon, the final at sunset. The archery contest draws a wider field — any guest of good standing may enter — and the wrestling is a rougher affair conducted with more audience participation than the heralds would prefer. Prizes for all three are awarded by Harold in person: gold, and the public acknowledgment that is worth more than the gold.

The party may enter any or all of these contests. The specific contest mechanics are at the GM's discretion; the following guidelines apply:

- *Jousting:* Requires a mount (available to rent from the stables at modest cost for the occasion) and the appropriate Combat Maneuver checks. Success in the lists brings genuine public notice — knights from several earldoms will seek out a skilled jouster afterward.
- *Archery:* A series of Perception and ranged attack checks against increasing DCs as the range extends. The final round introduces a moving target; the target is a painted wooden boar pulled by a rope at walking pace.
- *Wrestling:* Grapple-based. The elimination format means the party member must win two matches to reach the final; three to win the contest. Opponents are scaled to be challenging but beatable at 4th level.

*Winning any contest produces meaningful social results: an Earl whose champion was beaten by a party member has complicated feelings about it, and complicated feelings are useful. A party member who performs well in the lists will find that soldiers, knights, and serving people treat them differently for the remainder of the assembly.*

The poetry and music tournaments begin at the first evening hour, in a pavilion set up along the lake's southern shore. The format is a series of performances before a panel of three judges — a senior Pentad cleric of some learning, the Earl of Caerwold's court poet, and Fenna, who participates in the judging with visible interest and no apparent interest in diplomatic scoring. Party members with relevant skills may enter; success here moves different people than success in the martial games. Lady Sorella attends the music tournament; it is one of the few occasions during the assembly when she is visibly present and visibly engaged.

The second feast is held outside, in a series of elaborately decorated pavilions extending from Tintavel's southern gate down to the lake's edge.

> *The pavilions glow from within — lanterns of colored glass, dozens of them strung overhead so the light falls soft and varied, green and amber and the deep red of summer wine. The lake is still, and the pavilions' reflections lie in it undistorted. Tables run the full length of each pavilion, and servants move between them with the quiet competence of people who have been preparing this for weeks.*
>
> *The food is less theatrical than last night's — no live birds, no pastry engineering — but more careful: long-braised meats, fresh fish from the lake dressed with herbs, bread still warm from the ovens, fruit from the orchards of Goodlund arranged on platters that seem too beautiful to disturb. The wine is from Caerwold's cellars, as it always is, and the Earl of Caerwold accepts the compliments with the expression of a man who knows he is being managed and has decided to enjoy it anyway.*
>
> *An hour after dark, the first of them goes up.*
>
> *The firework rises from a barge anchored in the lake's center, reaches the height of its arc, and bursts into a chrysanthemum of white light that reflects perfectly in the water below — so that for a moment, it looks less like a display above a lake and more like an opening in the world, with light beyond. Then another. Then a sequence of three in rapid succession, gold and red and green, and the crowd along the shore makes the sound that crowds make when something exceeds their expectations, and keeps making it.*
>
> *The assembly is not yet healed. The bad blood of the rebellion does not dissolve in fireworks. But for the length of the display, everyone watching is watching the same thing, and the lake holds all of it without judgment.*

---

**Day Three: The Business of Court**

The third day is the assembly's formal purpose. It opens with ceremony and closes with Harold's most significant announcements of the year. What happens in between is negotiation, grievance, and the managed expression of opinions that have been waiting for a venue.

**Opening Ritual**

The assembly convenes in the great hall at the second morning hour. The Pentad's highest-ranking cleric present — typically the Archbishop's representative from Barstow, though the Archbishop himself occasionally attends — leads an invocation to the five gods. The prayer is long and formal and includes the seasonal observances specific to midsummer: thanks for the harvest's progress, petition for the kingdom's health, the formal acknowledgment of the Pentad's role in Haven's founding. Wotan is not named in the prayer. This is not remarked upon.

Following the invocation, a herald announces the names and titles of the nine Earls in order of earldom precedence — Kharas first, as the eldest earldom, Falkrith last, as the most recent formally titled. Each Earl rises in acknowledgment. The significance of the order is something every Earl in the room knows: seniority, in Haven's noble culture, is both honorific and political, and the manner in which each Earl rises tells the room something about their mood.

Harold enters last. He enters without announcement — this is by custom, not by accident — and takes his seat with the same absence of ceremony he applied to meeting the party months ago. He is in formal dress, which on him looks like someone has placed formal dress on a questing knight and the knight is tolerating it.

Father Eadric, at the king's right, says the words that formally open the assembly. He says them correctly and with appropriate weight and then makes a small joke that makes two-thirds of the room laugh and reminds the remaining third why they find him difficult. Harold's expression does not change.

**The Day's Business**

The morning is given over to petitions — formal grievances brought by the Earls to the Crown's attention, which Harold hears and either addresses immediately, refers to his administrators, or defers to later consideration. The petitions are partly genuine governance and partly political theater: an Earl who brings a compelling petition demonstrates effectiveness; an Earl who is denied demonstrates something else. The party will recognize several of the issues raised from their own quests. Crisis conditions will be visible in the room's temperature: an Earl whose earldom was affected by an unresolved quest will be more forceful, less cooperative, harder to read as friendly.

The afternoon is formal negotiation in smaller sessions, conducted in the receiving rooms off the main hall. Harold moves between them. The Magister attends some but not all of these sessions; when he is present, he says very little and the quality of the room's attention changes.

**Harold's Announcement**

Late in the afternoon, the full assembly reconvenes. Harold makes the year's formal pronouncements from the throne — tax adjustments, border policy, appointments to royal office, the acknowledgment of a new charter for a Goodlund market town. These are received with the attention of people who have been waiting all day to hear whether any of them apply unfavorably.

Then he says: "Before we close, I have one matter of personal significance to announce."

He calls the party forward.

*The party walks to the front of the assembled court. Every significant political figure in Haven is watching. The Earls of rebel and loyal earldoms alike. The church's representatives. The court's lesser officials, who understand that what happens in front of the full assembly becomes the record. The party's five months of work, in all its specificity and consequence, has arrived at this moment.*

Harold names them as agents who served the Crown in five matters of extraordinary difficulty and names each quest by its nature — not in detail, but in enough specificity that the Earls from affected earldoms understand exactly what is being referenced. He speaks of the Sword Quest: what it is, what it requires, why the Crown believes it is time. He announces the founding of the order and presents the name — the name the party has chosen — as the Crown's formal designation. He asks the assembled court to witness it.

The responses from the individual Earls range from genuine warmth to careful neutrality to a silence that is the political equivalent of reservation. The Earl of Waldsea's response depends entirely on how Quest 1 was resolved; in the worst case, he has a great deal to say, and says none of it here because this is not the forum, and Harold has chosen the forum deliberately. The Pentad's representative has opinions about the Sword Quest that will surface in the morning.

**Naming the Order**

The name is spoken aloud in front of every significant political figure in Haven. It should feel like what it is: the party's first permanent mark on the world they are about to leave. The GM and players should take a moment here. The name was discussed; this is where it becomes real.

**The Magister at the Announcement**

The Magister stands at Harold's left through the announcement. He offers the party a formal nod when Harold introduces them — precise, brief, carrying no particular warmth but no particular coldness either. He is present in the way that significant things are present: not drawing attention, not avoidable. If any party member attempts to speak with him after the announcement, he will respond politely and with complete unhelpfulness on any question of personal significance. He will say something accurate and useful about the Sword Quest. He will then have somewhere else to be.

**Lady Teta at the Assembly**

Lady Teta is present throughout all three days. Her conduct is impeccable; she does not put a word or a step wrong in the entire assembly. If the party discovered her connection to Alefrith and chose not to disclose it, she will find a private moment — in a side corridor, at the edge of the evening pavilions, during the confusion of the fireworks display — to acknowledge, without saying anything that could be repeated, that she understands the position they are in and that she intends to remain useful to people who have been useful to her. She will deliver on that offer. If the party disclosed her connection, she has already taken steps. What those steps are depends on the timing of the disclosure and the GM's judgment; she does not improvise, and she does not panic, and whatever she has done will have been done carefully.

**The Commission**

On the morning after the assembly's close, Harold meets the party privately in the small chamber where they first spoke. There is no ceremony. He gives them letters of royal authority, a modest fund for travel expenses, and one item from the royal treasury appropriate to the party's level and composition — chosen, as everything Harold chooses, with attention to what is actually needed rather than what looks impressive.

He does not make a speech. He tells them what he told them at the start: this is a gamble, he believes it is worth making, and he is grateful they are the ones making it. If they ask him how he will manage while they are gone, he will say, with complete honesty, that he will manage the way he always manages, which is by addressing each problem as it appears and trusting that the problems will not all appear at once.

Father Eadric is in the corridor when they leave. He will have something useful for the party — a contact's name, a piece of information, a small item he has been carrying since he decided they might need it — that Harold did not arrange and that Eadric will not mention to Harold afterward. He will wish them well in a way that manages to be both genuine and funny, which is his particular gift.

The party departs Sanction on the morning of the following day. Act II begins.
