# THE SHRINE IN THE EAST
## Player's Guide to Haven

*A Pathfinder First Edition adventure for characters of levels 1–12*

---

> This document reflects what a knowledgeable citizen of Haven knows or could discover. Haven's history is three centuries old and has been told many times. Not every telling is complete.

# Section I: Welcome to Haven

## The Kingdom of Haven

Three centuries ago, a people crossed the void between worlds. Where they had lived before, the land was dying — a slow catastrophe of failing seasons and encroaching dark that no governance could arrest and no faith could reverse. Of the thousands who took to the sea of stars in the great exile fleet, not all arrived. Those who did came to rest on the western peninsula they now call Haven, on a seacoast that would become the site of the city now called Landing. They had survived the crossing. They had nowhere else to go.

What made survival into founding was Cuthbert. He was not a man of noble birth, nor was he the fleet's commander — the command structure of the crossing had already fractured by the time the shore was sighted. He was a soldier of middle rank with a quality that distinguished him: he could be trusted with power and did not want it. The gods of the Pentad, who had guided the fleet through the void and would later shape Haven's covenant, chose him for exactly that reason. He received the Sword of the Redeemer from their hands — a divine gift, the sign of their commission — and with it, he led the survivors to build something that could last.

He reigned for nearly thirty years. In that time, he built the structures that still define Haven: the nine Earldoms, the covenant with the Pentad, the knightly code, the processional road between Landing and Sanction, the castle Tintavel on the lake's western bank. He was not a perfect king in the sense of never making mistakes; he was a perfect king in the sense that his errors were the kinds made by a man who cares more about what he is building than about his place in the building of it. When he left, two centuries ago, taking the Sword east to a shrine at the far end of the world, Haven did not collapse. This is perhaps the truest evidence of what he accomplished: the kingdom he built could continue without him.

Haven has continued. Three hundred years of farming and building and arguing have produced a kingdom with deep roots, a church that reaches into every settlement, a nobility that remembers its obligations in the breach as often as in the observance, and a common people with the particular stubbornness of those who have been told they live in a divinely favored land and have spent the intervening centuries deciding whether or not to believe it. The Pentad still governs spiritual life. The Earls still govern the settled regions. The king governs both, to the extent any king does.

The king is Harold II — thirty years old, twelve years on the throne, with a civil war comprising the first ten of them. He was a questing knight before he was a king, and the common people remember it. He settled the rebellion by four of his nine Earls through attrition more than resolution; two of the four have reconciled, two have not. He has appointed the kingdom's first royal magician, a decision the church opposes with a precision and vigor it reserves for matters it considers dangerous. He is attempting, with the patience of a man who intends to be here in twenty years and wants those twenty years to have mattered, to build the Crown into something more than the name given to the largest armed faction in Haven.

The Sword of the Redeemer is still east, said to be resting in the shrine where Cuthbert left it. Haven's knights have long undertaken the quest to recover it — the sacred weapon, the covenant's symbol, the one thing the founding story describes as unfinished. None of those who rode east for that purpose have returned. It is, in the register of chivalric tradition, Haven's great unfulfilled quest: acknowledged in every telling of the founding, attempted by every generation, and not yet accomplished.

## Life in Haven

### Sanction, the Capital 

Sanction is not the largest city in Haven — that distinction belongs to Barstow, three days' ride to the south — but it is the city that matters. The court sits here. The great decisions come here to be made or avoided. The Pilgrim Road, which runs two hundred miles west to Landing and the site of the original exile landing, terminates here, and on the feast days of the founding it floods with pilgrims completing the journey Cuthbert made three centuries ago.

The city sits on Aldenmere Lake's western shore, and the lake is its defining fact: the water visible at the end of every street that runs east, the fishing boats coming and going at all hours, the merchant barges that arrive from the three earldom rivers with grain and timber and wool. Tintavel, the king's castle, stands at the northern edge of the city where the original promontory gives it height over both lake and land. It is a dark stone structure of accumulated additions, built in every style that has been fashionable over three hundred years and therefore resembling nothing in particular, but it generates a peculiar attachment in the people who live in its shadow. They call it the Old House.

Daily life in Sanction moves to two rhythms: the court calendar, which controls the flow of nobles and petitioners and church officials through the city on a predictable seasonal pattern, and the lake, which controls everything else. A court session brings a sudden population of armsmen and servants and merchants who supply both; a quiet season brings the fishing boats and the canal traffic and the craftsmen who produce the goods the court will consume when it returns. The city runs on the premise that something important is always about to happen here, and it is usually correct.

### The Earldoms

Most of Haven is not Sanction. Most of Haven is a market town a day's walk from the nearest settlement of any size, surrounded by farms that have been worked by the same families across several generations and that will be worked by their children if the harvest holds and the tithe is not raised and the Ear's steward does not find a reason to complicate things this season.

Obligation is the architecture of rural life. The farmers owe labor and tithe to the Earl; the Earl owes military service and taxation to the Crown; the Crown owes, in the theory that the church teaches and the practice that varies, protection and justice to everyone below it. What this means in practice is that the farming year runs according to what the land requires, the rent calendar requires, and the religious calendar requires, and the art of rural life is managing the overlap. The church festivals that mark the seasons — six major observances, two dozen minor ones — are genuinely beloved, both because the faith is genuine and because feast days are feast days and there are not so many of them that people tire of them.

A traveler arriving at a rural earldom community will find a local inn with a single common room, a church presided over by a priest who likely serves three villages on a rotating circuit, a market cross where the weekly trade happens, and people who are neither unsophisticated nor welcoming of condescension. They have been visited before by people with grander ambitions than their own. They have, on the whole, outlasted those ambitions.

### Towns and Cities

Haven has settlements of every size, from single farmsteads at the edge of cleared land to the three communities that can properly be called cities. Most of the kingdom's settlements are small. A village of two or three hundred people, with a church and a market day and a mill if it is fortunate, is the characteristic unit of Haven's populated landscape. Market towns — communities of a thousand to several thousand, with resident merchants, a guild hall, weekly trade, and a periodic court — serve the surrounding countryside and anchor the earldom's economic life. But cities, in the sense of places large enough to contain multitudes and sustain genuine anonymity, Haven has only three.

Sanction, the capital, is where the court sits and the great decisions are made or avoided, on the western shore of Aldenmere Lake. Barstow, in the earldom of Caerwold, is Haven's largest and most commercially vigorous settlement — the Pentad maintains its institutional headquarters there, and its streets hold more wealth and more daily transactions than anywhere else in the kingdom. Landing, on Haven's western coast, is the smallest of the three but carries the most historical weight: it stands where the exile fleet first made shore three centuries ago, and the Great Processional Road connects it to Sanction in an unbroken two-hundred-mile line that pilgrims still walk on the founding feast days. A visitor to Landing will find a city half devoted to commerce and half devoted to memory, uncertain which function it values more and unwilling to choose.

### The Borderlands

The border earldoms — the communities in Kharas and Waldsea and Cant and Umbra where Haven's settled land meets the Wild's first trees — are different in kind from the interior. The difference is not hardness, exactly, though the work is harder. It is a quality of attention. People here know that the forest is watching, and they have built that knowledge into the way they inhabit their lives.

The Watch is Haven's border force: not a formal military structure but a tradition of trained scouts and standing patrols maintained by the border earldoms in exchange for Crown recognition and modest support. A Watch company covers twenty to forty miles of border on a circuit that varies by season, marked by watchtowers spaced close enough that a signal fire can relay an alert to Sanction within a day. The Watch does not stop every fey incursion — their function is to know what is crossing, assess what it wants, and summon appropriate response. Stopping individual incursions is a matter for the local village, which has its own customs.

Those customs are older than the Watch and more reliable in practice. Iron nails in the doorframe, above the threshold, and beneath the windowsill. Salt at the corners of a byre where livestock sleep. Never call out in the forest after dark — sound travels in the Wild in ways that favor neither the caller nor the intended recipient. The border communities have three centuries of accumulated knowledge about fey interaction, some of which is accurate and some of which is not, all of which is transmitted with equal conviction. What they know about gift obligation, the rule of three, the dangerous phrases, and the nature of fey music is generally correct. The rest is more variable.

# Section II: Geography

## The Nine Earldoms

The Crown's royal lands sit at Haven's center — the broad stretch of territory surrounding Sanction and Aldenmere Lake that the king holds directly, without an Earl intermediating between the throne and the people who farm it. The nine Earldoms surround these lands on nearly every side, each governed by a noble family holding the title by royal grant in exchange for military service, taxation, and the formal loyalty that the oath of fealty requires — and that the civil war's aftermath has made, in several houses, a more complicated matter than the ceremony suggests.

**Kharas** occupies the northeastern hills, country that would be forbidding to most and is home to the dwarves who have worked it since the exile fleet's landing. The hills hold iron, coal, and occasional silver, and Tormund — the earldom's chief city — has become a place of workshops and forges whose output the rest of the kingdom relies on without always acknowledging. Kharas faces the Wild directly along its eastern edge, and the string of strong border forts that line the approach to Tormund represent generations of accumulated military opinion about what that proximity requires.

**Waldsea** stretches east-northeast of Sanction across flat grasslands that transition without ceremony from farmable to strange. The region has always been prone to magical anomalies — lights that cannot be explained, places where sound behaves incorrectly, isolated pools reflecting landscapes no one recognizes — and its proximity to the Wild makes fey incursions a recurring feature of life in its eastern settlements. Harbow, the earldom's trading center, has been raided often enough that its residents have developed a resigned competence about it. The Earl of Waldsea was among the four who rebelled against Harold II. He has since sworn and returned to court; the oath holds, and the old conflict is not spoken of openly. Whether the grievance behind the rebellion has been resolved along with its formal expression is a question the court does not encourage.

**Cant** occupies the eastern border, a productive agricultural earldom whose people are, by general reputation, the most devout in Haven. This devotion has a particular character: it expresses itself partly through a heightened suspicion of what cannot be accounted for by the Pentad's official theology, and witch hunts have occurred here more frequently than anywhere else in the kingdom. The Pentad's institutional presence is strong in Cant, and the relationship between the clergy and the population is one of genuine mutual investment. Aerbra, the earldom's chief settlement, is a city of shrines that draws pilgrims from across the kingdom. The Earl of Cant was also among the four who rebelled. He has since sworn, and the formal relationship with the Crown is restored, though the combination of religious conservatism and recent memory makes Cant's court a place where Harold II's more controversial decisions — the Magister in particular — are received with a deliberateness that falls just short of open criticism.

**Umbra** is lightly populated by Haven's standards, its mixed terrain — rocky in the south, rolling grassland in the north — selecting for self-sufficiency over density. The earldom's southern border follows a great river that serves as a natural barrier between the settled lands and the goblinoid-inhabited mountains of the Badlands beyond. The river has held for three centuries, not without difficulty. Dalkeith, the earldom's port settlement, does most of its meaningful trade with the sea. The Countess of Umbra was not among the four who rebelled, though her relationship with the King is fragile. 

**Swansea** is marsh country, with difficult weather and customs that its residents maintain work perfectly well once understood. The earldom's population concentrates in Carrack, a delta town on the northern coast, and in the scattered fishing communities along the marsh edges. Swansea makes the smallest military contribution of any earldom and the most reliable supply of certain fish that the rest of the kingdom consumes without much thought about where they come from. Its Earl was not among the four who rebelled.

**Goodlund** occupies the central-east prairie east of the royal lands, the halfling heartland of Haven: prosperous, agricultural, and possessed of a brewing tradition about which the rest of the kingdom has strong and divided opinions. Dale, the earldom's market town, handles much of the agricultural surplus that flows westward toward Sanction and southward toward Barstow. The Earl of Goodlund has a long-standing reputation for pragmatism that has made him useful to whoever currently holds the Crown. He was not among the four who rebelled and enjoys a respectable relationship with the crown. 

**Caerwold** is Haven's commercial heart: large, productive, connected to the sea by multiple port towns, and home to the institutional headquarters of the Pentad in Barstow. The church's presence makes Caerwold politically complicated — the Earl answers to the Crown; the Archbishop in Barstow answers to the Pentad, and these two lines of authority overlap in ways both parties manage carefully and neither fully acknowledges. Barstow itself is Haven's largest city: more people, more trade, and less comfort than Sanction. The Earl of Caerwold was among the four who rebelled and has since formally sworn. The reconciliation is real in the legal sense and something considerably more complicated in every other sense. Caerwold is too large, too wealthy, and too important to the church's institutional power for the Crown to treat as a merely difficult house; the Earl is too aware of this to pretend his oath was made from conviction rather than calculation. Both parties maintain the form of the relationship with professional care.

**Tana** occupies the northwestern coast, wooded country isolated by geography from the rest of the kingdom, home to a population that has developed in that isolation characteristics the rest of Haven finds either charming or maddening depending on context. Logging and fishing are Tana's economic foundations. Heathclyde, the earldom's chief settlement, exports timber and imports most of what it needs. The Earl of Tana was not among the four who rebelled and has been cautiously friendly with Harold II since — a relationship that has never been formalized into anything specific, which both parties seem to prefer.

**Falkrith** runs west-central along the coast and across settled grasslands, home to the ancient processional road connecting Landing to Sanction. The road is Haven's oldest infrastructure, carrying both commercial traffic and a steady stream of pilgrims re-enacting the founding journey, and the Countess of Falkrith controls it — an asset that has been used with more or less subtlety across successive generations depending on the political situation. The current Countess's father was among the four who rebelled; she inherited both the title and the sworn oath his reconciliation produced, and her relationship with Harold II is the easier for it — the bad blood is his generation's, not hers, and she has some interest in keeping it that way. She is, of the four houses that rebelled, the one the court finds easiest to work with, which the other three have noticed.

## Sanction

The capital city, for all its claim to importance, is smaller than most visitors expect. Barstow has more people. Landing has more history in its architecture. What Sanction has is the court, the lake, and the particular quality of a place where everyone present has business that matters.

The city grows along Aldenmere Lake's western shore, running north toward the castle and south toward the lower docks in an irregular spread that follows the waterline rather than any plan. The lake is the organizing principle of the city's geography: visible at the end of every street that runs east, present in the smell of the air throughout the day, worked by fishing boats in the early mornings and merchant barges through the afternoon. On clear evenings, Tintavel's reflection lies on the water at an angle that most people, even those who have lived here for years, still stop to look at.

**Tintavel.** The king's castle stands on a promontory at the city's northern edge, a structure of dark stone that has been added to across three centuries until it resembles nothing in particular except itself. The great hall at its center is the original construction, broad and low-ceilinged and lit by oil lamps that give it the permanent quality of late afternoon. The towers are newer, built after the rebellion. The common people call it the Old House, which it does not particularly resemble.

**The Cathedral of the Five.** Sanction's primary church occupies the city's highest ground south of the castle, its five spires corresponding to the five gods of the Pentad. It is not the Pentad's grandest building — that distinction belongs to the Cathedral Complex in Barstow — but it is the one the king attends, which he does with both zeal and regularity. It maintains a significance that architectural scale cannot entirely supply.

**The Pilgrim Gate.** The western entrance to the city, where the processional road arrives from Landing after its two-hundred-mile passage through Falkrith, is built wide enough to accommodate processions and has been repaired and enlarged several times as the volume of pilgrims and traffic increased. On the feast days of the founding, the gate sees more people pass through it in a single day than most market towns see in a week.

**The Lake Market.** The broad quayside district south of the castle handles the commercial traffic that arrives by barge from the earldom rivers: grain, wool, timber, and fish, offloaded to the merchants and factors who supply the court and the city. It operates at full noise six days a week and at considerably reduced volume on the Pentad's observance days, which the market treats less as holy rest and more as an opportunity to renegotiate prices.

**The Court Quarter.** The cluster of noble residences, chancery buildings, and official apartments that surrounds Tintavel on three sides is referred to collectively as the Court Quarter, though it has no formal name and those who live in it resist the implication that their addresses are defined by their proximity to the castle. During court sessions, this district doubles in population within a week and empties again afterward with equal speed.

**The Old Bridge.** The single bridge across the Aldenmere River, which feeds the lake from the north, is three centuries old and in permanent repair. It carries most of the foot traffic between the northern districts and the rest of the city and has a social life of its own: vendors, arguments, news exchanged between people who run into each other in the middle and are therefore obligated to stop.

## The Wild and Beyond

East of Haven's last border fort, the great forest begins. It has no name in Haven's common usage — it is called the Wild, as if naming it more precisely would imply a familiarity that the kingdom has not earned. The canopy covers the continent's interior in an unbroken spread that extends further than any expedition from Haven has fully mapped. What lies at the far end of the continent is known only from the accounts of those who have crossed it — the old stories, the occasional elven visitor, and the handful of knights who went east on the Sword Quest and were never heard from again.

The peoples who inhabit the Wild are grouped in Haven's common speech under the word *fey*, which covers an extraordinary range of communities and traditions under a single term that the people it describes do not use for themselves. Elves are the most commonly encountered at Haven's borders, more likely than others to venture into the kingdom's margins and, to Haven's people, the most intelligible of the Wild's inhabitants. Gnomes appear somewhat less frequently. The traditional fey — the small and the strange, the creatures that seem made of the forest itself — are encountered more rarely still, mostly in the deep borderlands and, on uncommon occasions, further into the settled interior. These encounters are seldom straightforward: they are curious, magical, and occasionally malicious, and Haven's people fear the fey in the specific way they fear things whose power they cannot match and whose intentions they cannot reliably read.

The goblinoid peoples occupy the Badlands to Haven's south: a rocky and mountainous stretch of country between the kingdom's southern river border and the sea to the east, home to tribes of goblins, hobgoblins, and their kin. Their raids fall most heavily on Umbra, whose river border with the Badlands is the longest and most exposed of any earldom; the communities along the Aven have built their defensible habits across generations of this exposure. The southern forts hold. Elsewhere the goblinoid presence is felt primarily as a distant threat rather than an immediate one.

To Haven's west and north, the sea. To the east, the Wild, and beyond it — somewhere, through a mountain range and whatever lies past it — the land called Aldenmere. Haven's founding stories name it: Cuthbert traveled east, reached Aldenmere, and left the Sword at a shrine there before he died. The knights who have gone east to recover it have not come back. No one from Aldenmere has ever arrived in Haven. Whether Aldenmere is a place that still exists, a name for something that did once and does not now, or a legend that the founding stories elaborated beyond its original basis, Haven cannot say with any certainty. The Shrine in the East is the destination of the quest and, for most of Haven's people, approximately as real as any other destination in a story that happened three centuries ago.

# Section III: Society and Culture

## The Crown

Harold II has been king for twelve years. His reign began with a rebellion by four of his nine Earls — a war that lasted the first decade and ended through exhaustion rather than victory. All four have since sworn and returned to court. He was thirty when the last oath was taken. He has been governing a formally reconciled kingdom ever since, which is a different thing from a healed one.

He was, before he was king, a questing knight. This is well remembered by the common people, whose loyalty to him runs ahead of his actual accomplishments as a monarch. The King is well aware of this, and he regards as an obligation rather than an asset — the kind of goodwill that must be earned through results rather than spent on their absence. His court is described by those who attend it as disciplined and, by those who find discipline uncomfortable, as somewhat colorless. He keeps short counsel and makes decisions in writing. He prays, and is not self-conscious about it.

What marks his reign as distinctive and controversial in equal measure is his appointment of the Magister: a royal magician, the first in Haven's history, whose identity he has not disclosed and who appears in public wearing a deep cowl that makes his identification difficult. The church considers this an act of institutional aggression. Harold II considers it a practical necessity. Beyond the Magister, Harold's relationship with the nobility is the defining unresolved problem of his reign — all four rebel Earls have sworn, the war is over, and no one at court believes the underlying tensions have gone with it.

He is, in the considered opinion of the people who have dealt with him across twelve years, more capable than his position has allowed him to demonstrate.

## The Present Tensions

A kingdom does not reach its current condition all at once, and Haven's tensions are the accumulated product of several distinct pressures whose relationship to each other is not always obvious to those experiencing them.

The most visible is the legacy of the civil war. All four rebel Earls have sworn, and the formal apparatus of the kingdom functions as it should: taxation flows, levies are raised, court is attended. What has not been restored is the kind of easy trust that makes governance less like management and more like cooperation. The Earls who rebelled know that Harold II knows why they did it and has not forgotten; Harold II knows that his authority in their earldoms is observed rather than freely given. The maneuvering that fills the space between these positions — in appointments, in marriages, in the handling of church patronage and trade disputes — is constant, sophisticated, and conducted entirely within the forms of loyalty. It is the kind of politics that looks, from the outside, exactly like peace.

The second pressure is theological. Father Beede, a scholar-monk formerly of the Pentad's monastic tradition, has been circulating a claim that Haven's founding documents describe a different Pentad than the one the church currently teaches — one in which the Trickster occupied a more prominent place that was subsequently diminished without acknowledgment. His movement has significant popular support, particularly in Barstow and in the earldoms with more restive common populations. The church has characterized his position as heresy and is working to suppress the movement through formal process. Whether Beede's documents are genuine, and what their implications would be if they are, is a subject of heated discussion among those who have read them and those who have heard of them secondhand.

The third pressure is older and harder to name. Encounters with the fey in the borderlands have always been part of life near the Wild — curious, magical, and occasionally dangerous in ways that do not follow the logic of ordinary violence. Haven's people greatly fear the fey for their powers, and with reason; the records of what fey encounters have produced, at their worst, are not comforting reading. In the last several years, these encounters have become more frequent, and their character has shifted in ways the Watch reports describe carefully and without consensus interpretation. Iron-nail customs are being practiced further west than anyone living remembers them being necessary. The border communities are not alarmed in any dramatic sense; they are simply paying more attention than usual, which is the way that alarm announces itself in places with three centuries of experience managing it.

## The Pentad

Haven's faith is not a matter of metaphysics. The five gods of the Pentad exist. They take an interest in the affairs of the people who worship them. They appear in visions, speak through their chosen clerics, and have, on documented occasions, manifested in forms that left no room for interpretation. Three centuries of Haven's history have been shaped by this active divine presence, and the result is a population that is devout in the particular way of people who have never had to take the existence of their gods on faith alone.

The five gods were all once mortal. This is known and not widely examined: the official account holds that their apotheosis was a reward for exceptional virtue, that they were elevated to divinity because they deserved to be, and that this origin is a source of pride rather than a complication. The gods choose their clerics by criteria they do not explain, and true clerics — individuals granted genuine divine spellcasting directly by one of these deities — are rare enough that most communities in Haven have never met one. What fills the gap is a combination of parish priests who perform the liturgy without divine power, and the various folk practitioners whose capabilities the church officially condemns but practically tolerates, because the alternative is communities with no access to healing at all.

**Wotan, the Trickster,** (or sometimes "The Stranger") is the god of diplomacy, travelers, rulers, and magic. He is invoked by travelers at crossroads, by diplomats before difficult negotiations, and by the kind of people who regard official channels as a starting point rather than a destination. He is the least formally prominent of the five gods — his name appears rarely in the Pentad's official ceremonies, and where he is referenced, he is referenced by title rather than name. This is not a matter of theological dispute; it is simply how things are. Father Beede would argue otherwise, but Father Beede's position on the matter is currently before the church's inquisitorial process.

**Rana, the Mother,** is the goddess of community and nature. She is the most universally beloved of the five: the goddess most likely to appear in personal prayers, most present in domestic shrines, most associated with the ordinary experience of daily life. Farmers invoke her at planting and harvest. Mothers name daughters after her epithets. Her worship requires no intermediary and no special occasion — the small daily observances that make up most of Haven's religious practice are, in the main, directed at Rana.

**Tor, the Warrior,** is the god of weather, warfare, and physical strength. He is invoked before battle, during storms, and in any undertaking that requires endurance over elegance. His worship has a physical and immediate character that suits its adherents: Tor's observances tend toward exertion rather than contemplation. He is the patron deity of Haven's military tradition and is present in the oaths taken by soldiers, though knights are more likely to swear by Tyr.

**Tyr, the Father,** is the god of rulers and justice, called the far-seeing, said to observe the entire world from a mountain fortress at the edge of the divine realm. He is the primary patron of knights, noble houses, and the institutions of governance. His worship is most concentrated among the military and governing classes and among populations, like Cant's, that take seriously the idea of divine order expressed through social hierarchy. The Pentad's formal hierarchy mirrors Tyr's theology of structured authority, and the church's most conservative faction is the one most committed to his primacy among the five.

**Mara, the Scholar,** is the goddess of knowledge and civilization. She has a warrior aspect, but it concerns strategy and planning rather than force — she is the patron of those who win by knowing more than their opponents rather than hitting harder. Administrators, archivists, physicians, and tacticians are her natural constituency. Her worship tends toward the quiet and the private: a scholar who lights a lamp and begins work is performing a Maran observance whether she names it as one or not.

## The Knightly Code

Knighthood is Haven's highest secular honor and the organizing ideal of its military culture — not because all fighters are knights, or all knights are admirable, but because the code that knights are supposed to embody has shaped how Haven thinks about valor, obligation, and the proper use of force for three centuries.

A knight owes loyalty to the lord who granted the accolade, service to the kingdom, protection to those who cannot protect themselves, and honesty in all dealings. This is the summary version, the one that is taught to children and inscribed on memorial stones. The fuller version, which is the subject of considerable theological and philosophical argument, concerns itself with what happens when these obligations conflict: when loyalty to a lord requires something that justice forbids, or when the protection of the weak requires disobedience to a legitimate authority. Haven's knights have historically resolved these conflicts in ways that the record presents as heroic; those closer to the events often had more complicated views.

There are two kinds of knights in Haven. Crown knights hold their accolade directly from the king and owe their primary loyalty to the throne — they are the king's instrument, able to act with royal authority in the king's name, and they are correspondingly accountable to the Crown in ways that Earl's knights are not. Earl's knights hold their accolade from a noble house and owe their service first to their lord, then to the kingdom. The civil war made clear what happens when these two loyalties diverge; the knightly community has been working through these implications ever since.

The tradition of questing is the aspect of knighthood most alive in Haven's popular imagination. The great quests — undertakings of genuine difficulty for a genuinely important purpose — are the stories the kingdom tells about itself, and the Sword Quest is the grandest of them: the one quest that has been attempted by every generation and completed by none. A knight who rides east on the Sword Quest does so knowing that those who went before did not return, and knowing that the kingdom will remember the attempt regardless of the outcome. This is understood to be the point.

## Father Beede and the Trickster Movement

Father Beede is sixty-three years old, spent most of his adult life as a monk in the Pentad's scholarly tradition, and has recently become the most controversial figure in Haven's religious life. He claims to have found documents from the founding era — a firsthand account by Haven's first Prelate and a prayer book attributed to Cuthbert himself — that describe the Trickster occupying a significantly more prominent theological position than he holds in current Pentad practice. His conclusion, stated without drama and without apparent concern for the consequences, is that the Trickster's diminishment was deliberate and that the present account of the Pentad's theology is incomplete.

The church's position is that Beede's documents are either forgeries or misread, that his interpretation is heterodox, and that the movement his claim has generated represents a genuine threat to the theological order that Haven's stability depends on. The Inquisition has formal proceedings open against him. He has not adjusted his position.

Beede is not a revolutionary. He does not call for the church's overthrow, or the nobility's, or the Crown's. He calls for accuracy, which he regards as a theological obligation and which his opponents regard as the most dangerous kind of radicalism — the kind that cannot be dismissed as ambition. His movement has drawn followers from Haven's common people primarily, and from a portion of the scholarly class that finds his documentary evidence harder to dismiss than his critics have publicly acknowledged.

## The Merchant Guilds of Barstow

Barstow is Haven's largest city and its commercial center, and the guilds that operate there are the most developed mercantile institutions in the kingdom. They predate the current guild law by at least a century, having grown from informal trade associations into organizations with their own courts, their own records, their own enforcement mechanisms, and their own opinions about what constitutes fair dealing — opinions that do not always align with the Crown's.

The major guilds cover the trades that Barstow handles in sufficient volume to require organization: cloth and dye, grain and milling, river and coastal transport, metalwork, and the money-lending and exchange businesses that lubricate the rest. Each guild controls access to its trade within Barstow's walls and maintains correspondent relationships with guild houses in Sanction, Landing, and the larger market towns. A traveler with guild credentials — whether a member's token, a letter of introduction, or a vouched reputation — will find doors open in Barstow that are otherwise closed.

For adventurers and agents who need to move quickly through commercial channels, the guilds offer several useful services that do not require membership. Licensed factors can arrange transport contracts and supply purchases on short notice. The moneylenders operate a letter-of-credit system recognized across Haven's three cities. The transport guild maintains a courier network with reliable reach to most settled parts of the kingdom. None of these services is cheap, and none comes without the guild's expectation of reasonable treatment in return — the guilds have long institutional memories and extend their goodwill to those who have demonstrated it is not wasted.

## Elves and Gnomes in Haven

Elves have been present in Haven since the kingdom's first generation, and their status has not substantially changed in three centuries: they are full citizens in law and something more provisional in practice, associated in the common imagination with the Wild even when their families have lived within Haven's settled borders for generations. An elf in Sanction or Barstow navigates a persistent low-level wariness that is not hostility and not welcome — the sense that their citizenship is a courtesy the kingdom is extending rather than a recognition of belonging.

This wariness has specific roots. The elves who live in Haven's border communities often have genuine connections to the Wild's peoples, by lineage or by maintained relationships, and three centuries of fey incursions have made the distinction between "an elf who lives here" and "a fey who came from the Wild" a matter of active political disagreement. Elves who have served in Haven's military — though few of them have — are accorded more practical trust than those who have not, which tells elves something about what Haven considers legitimate belonging. 

Gnomes are rarer than elves in Haven's settled regions and are regarded with a curiosity that shades quickly into wariness if they display any of the craft abilities associated with fey magical practice. Their reputation is for ingenuity and for a certain unpredictability in the application of it. A gnome artificer in Barstow doing straightforward guild work encounters little friction; a gnome doing something that looks like it might be magic, even if it is not, attracts a different kind of attention. They are Haven's citizens in good standing, which has never quite meant what it says.

## Dealing with the Fey

The border communities of Haven have accumulated three centuries of practical knowledge about fey interaction. Not all of it is accurate — some of the older beliefs have been garbled in transmission, and a few are simply wrong — but the core of what experienced border people know is reliable, and characters who have grown up near the Wild will have absorbed it as a matter of routine. What follows is the version that has proven out across repeated encounters.

**Gifts create obligations.** A gift given by a fey creates a debt unless the giver explicitly releases you with the ritual form: *"I offer this without obligation, let, or lien, and without expectation it will return to me."* Any gift given without this declaration is a claim on you, of a nature and scope the fey will define at a time of their choosing. The obligation does not expire and is recognized by all fey, not only the one who gave the gift. If you receive something from a fey, note it.

**Be polite. But not in the usual way.** Politeness is required in fey interaction and its absence is remembered. However, three common expressions of courtesy are traps. *"Thank you"* places you under obligation to the recipient. *"I'm sorry"* is a formal admission of fault they may use against you later. *"You're welcome"* implies you have performed a service and expect reciprocation. Use other forms: *"I am glad of it," "this is well done," "your house honors us."* The warmth is genuine; only the specific phrases are dangerous.

**Never give your name first.** Names hold power in fey tradition. Giving your name to a fey who has not offered theirs first grants them authority over you. A fey who offers their name first is extending genuine trust — recognize it as such and offer yours in return.

**Follow the rule of three.** Fey gifts are offered three times; accepting on the first or second offer is presumptuous. A fey promise stated only once or twice is an intention, not a commitment. A request made only once has not yet been fully made. Learning to count fey offers will make many interactions considerably more navigable.

**Stay on the path.** In any fey-touched area — particularly within the Wild itself — never leave a defined path. A traveler who departs from a clear route becomes lost in ways that ordinary navigation cannot resolve. The fey realm does not have consistent geography for those who stray from its roads.

**Music and dancing are risks.** Both can produce lost time: minutes experienced as moments, hours as a pleasant evening. The effect is not always intentional, but it is always possible. Stay aware of your surroundings when fey music is playing. Do not dance unless you have specific reason to trust the fey in question.

**Repay exactly.** Repaying a fey debt with more than you borrowed implies their original gesture was insufficient — an extraordinary insult. Repay precisely what you received, in the same spirit it was given.

**Keep your word absolutely.** A promise given to the fey is binding. Breaking it allows them to claim a service of equivalent value without your consent and without recourse. This rule is symmetrical: a fey who breaks their word is subject to the same claim. It is one of the few points of genuine mutual obligation in fey interaction and one of the more reliable tools when dealing with fey whose honesty you cannot otherwise rely on.

# Section IV: Character Creation

## Races

Haven's population is predominantly human, and human characters require no particular social navigation — they are the default inhabitant of every community in the kingdom and carry no outsider's burden regardless of where they travel within its borders. The other races have a presence in Haven, some of it old and settled, some of it more provisional.

**Humans** form the vast majority of Haven's population across all nine Earldoms and the royal lands. The founding fleet was predominantly human, and three centuries of settlement have not substantially diversified the kingdom's demographics. Human characters may come from any earldom, any social class, and any relationship to the Crown's service. The diversity of Haven's political and religious landscape means that two human characters from different earldoms may have meaningfully different assumptions about authority, faith, and the proper relationship between the church and the Crown.

**Dwarves** have been part of Haven since the exile fleet's landing and are concentrated primarily in Kharas, whose hills suit their traditional industries, and in the skilled trades of the larger cities. They are full citizens in every formal sense and are regarded, in general, as reliable — a characterization they have earned and that occasionally obscures the range of individuals it flattens. A dwarf character from Tormund has a different social position than a dwarf craftsman in Barstow, and both are different from a dwarf who has served in the border Watch. Dwarven characters use standard Pathfinder racial traits.

**Halflings** were also a susbstantial portion of the fleet of the exiles. They are the heartland of Goodlund and a visible presence throughout the central and southern earldoms. Haven's halflings have a reputation for warmth, competence, and a pragmatism about the world's workings that other races sometimes misread as simplicity. They are, as a group, thoroughly integrated into Haven's civic and commercial life, and the question of their social position is less one of acceptance than of the particular condescension that comes with being well-liked. Halfling characters use standard Pathfinder racial traits.

**Elves** occupy a liminal position in Haven that three centuries have not resolved. They are citizens. They serve in the Watch, in the court, and in commerce. They are also associated, in the common imagination, with the Wild — a connection that does not disappear simply because a given elf's family has lived in Sanction for four generations. An elf character navigates a persistent low-level wariness that is not hostility and not full welcome, and that intensifies noticeably near the border earldoms or in communities with recent fey encounters. Elf characters use standard Pathfinder racial traits.

**Gnomes** are rarer than elves in Haven's settled regions and are regarded with a curiosity that shades quickly toward suspicion if they display capabilities that read as magical — even if those capabilities are entirely mundane. Their reputation is for ingenuity and a certain unpredictability in its application. Gnome characters are Haven citizens in good standing and encounter no formal barriers; the informal ones are situational and generally navigable with sufficient care. Gnome characters use standard Pathfinder racial traits.

## Classes

**Fighters, Cavaliers, and Rangers** are the most socially legible martial classes in Haven. Fighters and Cavaliers fit naturally into the knightly culture that shapes Haven's military ideal, whether or not a given character holds an accolade. Rangers are the working backbone of the border Watch — not knights, but respected for the practical skills that keep the frontier legible. A martial character will find few doors closed on account of their class.

**Paladins** occupy the most socially elevated position of any class in Haven. A paladin is Tyr's chosen — someone the Father-god selected personally, which carries weight in a kingdom that takes divine election seriously. The Pentad views paladins with a complicated mixture of institutional deference and occasional anxiety: a paladin's authority derives directly from a god, not from the church's hierarchy, and these two lines of authority do not always point the same direction. Paladins are expected to uphold the knightly code at its fullest expression and are held to a standard that the rest of Haven's knights merely aspire to.

**Clerics** are rare. The gods do not grant divine spellcasting in response to petition or formal ordination; they choose their clerics by criteria they do not explain, and the vast majority of Haven's parish priests are not clerics in any mechanical sense. A cleric character is, by definition, someone a god selected — and Haven will treat them accordingly, with a mixture of profound respect, some fear, and the particular weight of expectation that attaches to people understood to speak for powers larger than themselves. A cleric of Wotan, in the current religious climate, would be a conspicuous figure.

**Druids** operate in Haven's grey zone: valued for what they can do, distrusted for how they do it. The Pentad opposes magical practice officially, and druidic work sits close enough to magic that it attracts church attention in the more conservative earldoms. In the borderlands and rural interior, druids who serve communities with skill and discretion are generally tolerated — what the church's official position is and what an individual priest does when the village needs a healer are often different matters. Druids are not persecuted systematically, but they are not comfortable everywhere.

**Wizards, Sorcerers, and Magi** face the most consistent social friction of any class in Haven. The Pentad's condemnation of magical practice is most pointed when directed at the arcane traditions, which the church associates with the usurpation of divine power in its most direct form. A wizard character can practice their craft — the condemnation is social and theological, not backed by formal law in most earldoms — but they will encounter suspicion in conservative communities, occasional Inquisitorial interest if they are insufficiently discreet, and the particular challenge of operating in a kingdom whose founding church regards what they do as fundamentally illegitimate. The Magister's appointment has changed the political calculus somewhat, but it has not resolved the underlying tension.

**Rogues** are neither celebrated nor stigmatized in Haven's cultural framework — they are simply present, particularly in Barstow and other commercial cities where the skills of observation, information, and discrete capability find ready employment. A rogue in Crown service is a legitimate and recognized role. A rogue in independent practice is evaluated by the quality of their judgment and the discretion of their methods.

**Bards** occupy a valued if circumscribed social position. Court culture has a genuine appetite for skilled performers and poets, and a bard with court credentials can move through Haven's upper registers with surprising ease. The folk tradition is equally strong: traveling performers are welcome in most communities and often serve as the primary source of news from outside. The arcane component of bardic magic is generally overlooked in practice, treated as a curiosity of the performance tradition rather than the kind of magical practice the church is interested in pursuing.

**Alchemists and Investigators** are treated similarly to druids — occupying the grey zone between the practically useful and the theologically suspicious, calibrated by earldom and context. Alchemists who produce healing supplies and functional gear for border communities are left alone. Those whose work starts to look like it might be magic attract more scrutiny.

**Inquisitors** in Haven's setting most naturally represent agents of the Pentad's own Inquisition — the institutional mechanism by which the church pursues heresy and suppresses unauthorized magical practice. A player character Inquisitor is an interesting complication: they carry the church's formal authority but answer to an institution that may, over the course of the adventure, give them grounds for serious disagreement with their mandate. An Inquisitor operating outside the Pentad's formal structure — perhaps in service to the Crown directly, or working with the authority of a specific god rather than the institutional church — is entirely plausible and sidesteps some of that tension while introducing others.

**Oracles, Witches, and Summoners** have no strong cultural precedent in Haven and will be treated as exotic by most of the kingdom's population. This is not necessarily a barrier — exotic can read as interesting, as dangerous, or as associated with the fey, depending on context and earldom. Characters of these classes should consider what their origin story looks like from the outside, because Haven's people will.

### Divine Spellcasters: Deity Domains

Clerics, paladins, and inquisitors draw their power from one of the five gods of the Pentad. The domains available to divine spellcasters of each deity are listed below.

**Wotan (The Trickster):** Luck, Magic, Rune, Travel, Trickery

**Rana (The Mother):** Animal, Community, Earth, Plant, Protection

**Tor (The Warrior):** Chaos, Fire, Strength, War, Weather

**Tyr (The Father):** Air, Glory, Law, Nobility, Sun

**Mara (The Scholar):** Artifice, Healing, Knowledge, Liberation, Water

## Campaign Traits

Each player character may select one campaign trait. These traits reflect a specific connection to Haven's institutions or landscape and provide both a mechanical benefit and a social context that shapes how NPCs engage with the character.

---

**Agent of the Crown**

You have served in Harold II's household, delivered dispatches for the chancery, stood guard at a court session, or run an errand that required someone the Crown could trust. You have seen the court from close enough to know the difference between its public face and the way it actually works, and you have made your peace with that difference. Harold II is attempting to build something that previous kings did not attempt, and you have decided it is worth helping.

*Benefit:* You gain a +1 trait bonus on Diplomacy checks, and Diplomacy is a class skill for you. Once per day, when dealing with a representative of a noble house or Crown institution, you may invoke your Crown credentials to treat your attitude result as one step more favorable before you begin rolling.

*Personality suggestion:* You have spent enough time near power to be unsentimental about it without being cynical. You believe in what the Crown is trying to do, which is different from believing the Crown does it perfectly.

---

**Child of the Borderlands**

You grew up in one of the border earldoms where the Wild is not a distant abstraction but a presence visible from the upper windows of your childhood home. Fey encounters were not exotic to you — they were a fact of the calendar, something to be managed with the same careful attention you gave to weather or difficult neighbors. You know the rules. You know what happens when people break them. You know that the polite thing and the safe thing are usually the same thing, and you know why.

*Benefit:* You gain a +1 trait bonus on Knowledge (nature) checks, and Knowledge (nature) is a class skill for you. Additionally, you may reroll one Knowledge (nature) check per day, taking the better result.

*Personality suggestion:* You do not romanticize the fey. You respect them, which is more useful. When other characters approach a fey encounter with either contempt or naive charm, you will have opinions.

---

**Faithful of the Pentad**

Your faith in the five gods is not performed — it is a working part of how you move through the world. You pray at the canonical hours, observe the liturgical calendar, and have found in the Pentad's practice a genuine source of steadiness in difficult circumstances. Whether the faith you were given is the faith you will end this adventure with is a question you have not yet been asked.

*Benefit:* You gain a +1 trait bonus on Will saving throws against fear and despair effects. Once per day, when you fail a saving throw against a mind-affecting effect, you may immediately reroll the save, taking the better result.

*Personality suggestion:* You take theology seriously enough that when Father Beede's arguments begin to make sense, it will cost you something. That cost is not yet apparent to you. It will be.

## Character Hooks

Before play begins, every character should answer the following six questions. The answers need not be shared with the whole group, but they should be specific — a general answer is less useful than a particular one. These questions place your character in Haven's world rather than adjacent to it.

**One.** You were a questing knight, a household guard, a chancery clerk, a traveling merchant, or something else entirely — but the Crown has taken notice of you. What did you do, or what do you have, that brought you to Harold II's attention?

**Two.** You have had one genuine encounter with the fey. It may have been brief or extended, friendly or frightening, in the borderlands or — more unusually — closer to the settled interior. What happened, and what did you bring back from it, materially or otherwise?

**Three.** The Pentad shaped how you understand the world. Which of the five gods do you turn to in a moment of genuine doubt — and which one do you find yourself privately uncertain about?

**Four.** Four Earls raised armies against Harold II a decade ago. All four have since sworn. You have an opinion about whether the reconciliation is real. What is it, and does it affect how you treat the noble houses you will encounter in his service?

**Five.** Father Beede's movement is spreading. Someone in your life — a family member, a priest, a friend, a rival — has an opinion about him and has expressed it to you. What do they believe, and do you find yourself agreeing?

**Six.** You are leaving Haven on a quest that the kingdom has attempted for three centuries without success. You have left something behind — a person, a responsibility, an unresolved situation — that you are not certain will be unchanged when you return. What is it?

# Section V: What Your Character Knows

## Common Knowledge

The following is understood by any reasonably informed citizen of Haven. Characters need not have been told these things explicitly; they are the water in which Haven's people swim.

- Cuthbert the Redeemer founded Haven three centuries ago, leading exile survivors to this peninsula after their home was lost. He carried the Sword of the Redeemer — given to him by the gods — as the symbol of the covenant between Haven and the Pentad. He reigned for nearly thirty years, built the kingdom's foundations, and departed east two centuries ago. He did not return. The Sword went with him.
- The Pentad governs Haven's spiritual life through a single unified church present in virtually every community in the kingdom. Five gods, one institution. Clerics — individuals granted genuine divine power directly by the gods — are rare and personally chosen by the deity; you cannot seek ordination. Most parish priests are not clerics in any magical sense.
- Magic is officially condemned by the Pentad as usurpation of divine power. The condemnation has real social force in the more devout earldoms and is enforced by the church's Inquisition when it chooses to act. Folk practitioners — druids, alchemists, hedge workers — fill the gap left by the scarcity of clerics and are tolerated in practice more than the official position would suggest.
- King Harold II is thirty years old and twelve years on the throne. He spent the first decade of his reign in a civil war against four of his nine Earls; all four have since sworn and returned to court. He was a questing knight before he was a king, which the common people have not forgotten. He recently appointed the kingdom's first royal magician — the Magister, whose identity he has not disclosed — a decision the church considers a provocation and Harold II considers a necessity.
- The border between Haven's settled earldoms and the Wild is managed by the Watch, a tradition of trained scouts and patrols maintained by the border earldoms. The Watch does not stop fey encounters; its function is to know what is happening and summon appropriate response. Encounters with the fey are possible anywhere in the border earldoms and, on rarer occasions, in the interior. Haven's people fear the fey and have practical customs — some accurate, some not — for managing encounters.
- The Badlands to Haven's south are home to goblinoid tribes whose raids fall most heavily on Umbra, the earldom whose river border with the Badlands is longest and most exposed. The southern forts hold. Elsewhere in the kingdom, the goblinoid threat is a distant one.
- Father Beede, a former monk of the Pentad, claims to have recovered founding-era documents proving that Wotan held a more prominent place in the original Pentad theology than the church currently acknowledges. His movement has significant popular support in Barstow and the southern earldoms. The church has opened formal heresy proceedings. Whether Beede's documents are genuine is not a question the church has been willing to settle by examining them publicly.
- Something has changed on the Wild's eastern border in the last several years. Fey encounters have become more frequent and their character has shifted. The Watch reports are careful and inconsistent. Iron-nail customs are being observed further west than living memory places them. No one has formally named what is happening.
