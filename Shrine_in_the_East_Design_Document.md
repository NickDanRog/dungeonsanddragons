# THE SHRINE IN THE EAST
## Design Document

*Pathfinder 1e Adventure Module — Design Blueprint*
*Compiled: March 2026*

---

## Adventure Overview

- **System:** Pathfinder First Edition
- **Level Range:** 1–12
- **Structure:** 4 acts
- **Tone:** Romantic fantasy in the Arthurian tradition — questing knights, fey magic, feudal honor, and heroic destiny. The world feels grand and storied, and the PCs are invited to become part of its legend. Beneath that grandeur, the campaign examines how legends are made and what they conceal: the history the characters were raised to revere is incomplete at best, deliberately constructed at worst, and the quest to recover their kingdom's founding symbol forces them to confront the full weight of that gap.
- **Player Character Type:** Citizens of Haven — humans, dwarves, or halflings — newly elevated to royal service by King Harold II. The campaign begins at the bottom of the world's hierarchy and climbs toward its apex. By the final act, the PCs hold a symbol of divine legitimacy and must decide what kind of kingdom they are willing to fight for.

---

## Central Premise

Three hundred years ago, King Cuthbert the Redeemer led a people in exile across the boundary between worlds, guided by five gods and armed with a sword given to him as a sign of divine favor. He founded Haven on lands he took from their inhabitants, built a kingdom from the wreckage of a refugee fleet, and became the greatest hero in the kingdom's history. The sword he carried — the Sword of the Redeemer — became the symbol of that heroism and the emblem of Haven's founding covenant with the Pentad.

Then he gave up his throne, took the Sword east, and did not return.

The kingdom his heirs built across three centuries has done what kingdoms do: it has written its own history to serve its present interests. The displacement of the goblinoid peoples that caused the war with the Wild is recorded as an inevitable conflict with savages. The fey's resistance to Haven's expansion is attributed to their malign and magical nature. The Trickster — Wotan, the god who chose Cuthbert and led the exile — has been reduced to a footnote in the Pentad's official theology, his influence quietly erased by the four gods who found his values inconvenient once the hard work of survival was done.

Cuthbert knew all of this was coming. He left anyway, because staying would have made it worse.

The PCs will not know any of this at the start. They are agents of the crown, loyal to their king and their gods, dispatched to prove themselves worthy of carrying a sacred quest. What they find at the end of that quest — in the person of Cuthbert's descendants, in the memory of his elven wife, and in the brief presence of his bound spirit — will demand that they choose who they are and what their kingdom is worth.

**What players do not know at the start:**
- The displacement of the goblinoid peoples was the direct cause of the ongoing war with the Wild, not a response to fey aggression
- Wotan (the Trickster) was the leader of the Pentad during Haven's founding and advocated for diplomacy and integration with the continent's peoples — a position the other four gods have since worked to erase from public memory
- The fey are not inherently evil or malign; much of their hostility is a direct response to three centuries of displacement, war, and persecution
- Cuthbert left Haven not because of grief alone but because he foresaw that his continued presence, and the Sword's, would destroy what he had built — and because he was ashamed of what the kingdom was becoming
- The Sword passes only to those worthy of it, and worthiness is defined by ordeals that test values Haven's official culture does not always honor

## The World

### Haven

Haven occupies a sub-continental peninsula on the western coast of a vast and largely unmapped continent. It is bounded to the north, west, and southwest by the sea — the water that carried the exile fleet three centuries ago and still defines the kingdom's western consciousness. To the south lie the Badlands: a broken landscape of ravines and rocky flats inhabited by goblinoid tribes whose ancestors were displaced to make room for the exiles' settlement. To the east, Haven's frontier bleeds into the Wild, the great forested wilderness that covers the vast interior of the continent and is home to the peoples the kingdom calls the fey.

The kingdom is organized around the Crown's royal lands, which surround the capital of Sanction, and nine Earldoms holding the rest of Haven's settled territory. The Earldoms are ruled by hereditary Earls who owe the Crown military service and taxation but exercise significant autonomy within their own borders. Tensions between the Crown and the nobility are chronic and occasionally violent — as recently as a decade ago, four of the nine Earls launched an open rebellion against King Harold II that ended only through mutual exhaustion.

**Capital:** Sanction sits on the shores of a broad freshwater lake at the center of the royal lands. The king's castle — a centuries-old fortress of dark stone — occupies a rise on the western bank, commanding views of the water and the processional road that runs south through Falkrith toward Landing. Sanction is not Haven's largest city, but it is its most important: the royal court, the Crown's administrative apparatus, and what remains of the kingdom's diplomatic function all concentrate here.

**The Nine Earldoms:**

- **Kharas** (northeast): Hill country inhabited largely by dwarves, home to extensive mining operations and some of the kingdom's finest metalwork. Its chief settlement, Tormund, is a dense city of workshops and forges, its skyline broken by the towers of a string of strong border forts facing the Wild.
- **Waldsea** (east-northeast): Wide grasslands prone to magical anomalies and fey incursions, home to many herdsmen. Its chief settlement is Harbow, a livestock trading town that has weathered more fey raids than almost any other settlement in Haven.
- **Cant** (east-southeast): Haven's most productive farming land and one of its most religiously devout regions. Cant's population is deeply pious, deeply suspicious of the fey, and has a long history of witch hunts. Its chief settlement is Aerbra, a small city of shrines, temples, and commercial activity.
- **Umbra** (southeast): Rocky hills to the south, rolling grasslands to the north, and the least-populated of the Earldoms. A great river protects its southern border from the goblinoid-inhabited mountains. Its primary settlement is Dalkeith, a small port.
- **Swansea** (north-central): Mostly swampy, home to an insular population of fishermen and hunters who have little interest in the wider kingdom and receive little interest in return. Its chief settlement is Carrack, a delta town on the northern coast.
- **Goodlund** (central-east): High prairie grasslands home to a substantial halfling population. Productive farms, orchards, and breweries make Goodlund among the more prosperous earldoms. Its chief settlement is Dale, a large market town adjacent to the royal lands.
- **Caerwold** (south-central): Haven's commercial heart — large, prosperous, home to many farms and port towns. Its chief settlement, Barstow, is an important port city and the headquarters of the Pentad's institutional church.
- **Tana** (northwest): Wooded and isolated, home to many small villages and a population that tends toward insularity. Logging and fishing are the primary industries. Its chief settlement is Heathclyde, a small port.
- **Falkrith** (west-central): Well-settled grasslands and coast, home to the ancient processional road leading to the capital. Its chief settlement is Landing, the port city where the exile fleet is said to have first made landfall — the most historically resonant location in the kingdom.

**The Wild:** The great forested wilderness covering most of the continental interior. Haven's people use "the Wild" to refer both to the land itself and to the peoples who inhabit it — primarily elves, gnomes, and the traditional fey races that have lived here since before human memory. The Wild begins where Haven's frontier ends, and that border has been a site of low-level conflict for three centuries.

The Wild is also home to the goblinoid peoples displaced by the exile fleet's landing. After three centuries of friction with Haven and uneasy accommodation with the fey peoples of the interior, the goblinoids have settled into a wary peace with their fey neighbors while maintaining an enduring hatred for Haven. Raids against the kingdom's border settlements are common. The official history attributes this hostility to goblinoid savagery; the reality is that the goblinoids remember who took their land.

**Aldenmere:** On the far side of the continent, beyond the great mountain range that bisects the interior, lie the eastern lands collectively called Aldenmere. What began as the settlement of those who followed Cuthbert east on his final quest has grown over two centuries into a loosely structured society of those followers' descendants and the fey peoples who were already there. Aldenmere has no unified government; its communities govern themselves according to local custom, with tradition, honor, and practical necessity providing more coherence than any formal institution. Magic is practiced openly, and those who use it are neither honored nor feared in excess — it is simply part of how the world works. The people of Aldenmere know who Cuthbert was. They tell a different story about him than Haven does.

### Religion

Haven's official religion is the **Pentad**: five deities worshipped as a combined pantheon, each representing a distinct sphere of divine authority. The Pentad is the state religion in all but formal declaration, deeply beloved by the common people and institutionally entrenched through the church headquartered in Barstow. The nobility and Crown pay appropriate respect to the faith while exercising their own judgment about its precepts.

The Pentad's most visible institutional position is opposition to magical practice, which the church regards as an encroachment on divine prerogative. This creates persistent tension, particularly along the Wild's frontier, where practical survival sometimes requires what the church condemns. True clerics — those granted divine spellcasting by the gods themselves — are rare, because the gods choose their clerics personally and do not grant divine power casually. Folk practitioners (druids, alchemists, and hedge practitioners) are more common and provide most of the practical magical support that ordinary people rely on.

The gods of the Pentad are of intermediate divine strength. They were all once mortals — a deliberate criterion of Wotan's in assembling a pantheon for the exiles. They take an active hand in the world, appearing in visions and communicating with their chosen, in part to prevent their nature from shifting too far in directions they did not choose. A god's character is shaped by the understanding of their believers; they manage this actively to preserve their own sense of self.

**The Five Gods:**

- **Wotan (The Trickster):** God of diplomacy, travelers, rulers, and magic. He selected Cuthbert as his champion and was the primary architect of the exodus from the dying world. Among the Pentad, he led during the founding years — and has been systematically sidelined by the other four ever since. His name is virtually unknown to ordinary worshippers; he is referred to only as the Trickster in Pentad ceremony. Domains: Luck, Magic, Rune, Travel, Trickery.
- **Rana (The Mother):** Goddess of community and nature. Recruited by Wotan to help keep the exiles together across their differences. She remains the most universally beloved of the five. Domains: Animal, Community, Earth, Plant, Protection.
- **Tor (The Warrior):** God of weather, warfare, and great physical strength. Mercurial in temperament; associated with battle's chaos as much as its glory. His relationship with Wotan goes back to their mortal lives as adventurers together. Domains: Chaos, Fire, Strength, War, Weather.
- **Tyr (The Father):** God of rulers and justice. The far-seeing, said to observe the entire world from his mountain fortress. The primary patron of knights and noble houses. Domains: Air, Glory, Law, Nobility, Sun.
- **Mara (The Scholar):** Goddess of knowledge and civilization, with a warrior aspect more concerned with strategy than force. Domains: Artifice, Healing, Knowledge, Liberation, Water.

## Key NPCs

### King Harold II (The Young King)
*Current ruler of Haven. The PCs' patron and employer.*

Harold II inherited the throne from his grandfather Harold I a decade ago, at an age that prompted immediate questions from the nobility about whether the Crown could hold. Four of the nine Earls tested that proposition directly — the resulting rebellion settled into a stalemate that attrition forced both sides to abandon. Harold II is not naive about what that settlement means: the bad blood with the nobility is structural, not personal, and no diplomacy will resolve it without shifting the underlying balance of power.

He is a better king than his reign suggests. He knows Haven's history well enough to recognize the pattern: crisis follows crisis, settlement follows settlement, and nothing improves. He is attempting something different. The PCs represent the first iteration of a new idea — a corps of heroes answering directly to the Crown, independent of the nobility and the church, whose deeds and reputation belong to the throne. If they succeed, he gains a political asset no previous Harold thought to build. If they fail, the Sword Quest will at least have captured the public's attention. He is aware that he is being strategic. He is not entirely cynical about it.

### King Cuthbert the Redeemer
*Founder of Haven. Dead two centuries. Still present.*

In legend, Cuthbert the Redeemer is Haven's perfect hero: a man chosen by the gods, hardened by catastrophe, who led a broken people to safety and built something worth preserving from the ruins of everything they had lost. He is depicted in every public building, named in every oath, and held up as the standard against which all subsequent rulers fall short.

The truth is more human and more interesting. He was genuinely exceptional — capable, compassionate, and remarkably clear-eyed about the limits of his own judgment. He made decisions in the founding years he spent the rest of his life regretting. He chose to leave not because grief broke him — though his first wife Aleswith's death was part of it — but because he could see the kingdom's trajectory and could not change it from within.

Aleswith died in Haven after years of building the kingdom alongside him. They had several children; his daughter **Mathilde** he selected as his successor, hoping to establish merit-based succession as a lasting tradition. The tradition did not outlast her. His departure came two decades after Aleswith's death, when the last of his reasons for staying had been exhausted.

In the eastern lands, he married **Reinja**, an elven sorceress, and together they had one child. He died approximately two centuries ago. His tomb is the Shrine in the East, in Aldenmere. His spirit is bound to the Sword of the Redeemer and will speak briefly to whoever completes the ordeals and takes up the weapon. He does not offer judgment. He offers truth, and advice if asked.

### Reinja
*Elven sorceress. Cuthbert's second wife. Creator of the five ordeals.*

She has been in Aldenmere since before Cuthbert arrived and has outlived him by two centuries with no apparent sign of diminishment. She is not sentimental about this. She created the five ordeals at Cuthbert's request as a means of ensuring the Sword could only pass to those genuinely worthy of it — an attempt to prevent exactly the kind of political manipulation of the weapon's symbolism that drove him east in the first place. She has tested every claimant since.

She is not warm. She is not cold either. She will speak plainly to the PCs about what is required and has no patience for attempts to charm or argue around the conditions. Whether she finds the party worthy is a matter of what they do, not what they say.

### Signi
*Current Redeemer. Direct descendant of Cuthbert. Guide.*

Signi is one of only two of Cuthbert's physical descendants to have passed the ordeals and claimed the title of Redeemer. She has held the Sword long enough to understand its weight and to have spent considerable time thinking about what Cuthbert actually intended when he brought it east.

Her conclusion, on hearing the PCs' account of Haven, is that the time has come. She believes the Sword's purpose was never to remain in Aldenmere — it was to return when Haven had people capable of using it properly. She will send the PCs to Reinja with a riddle, and she will tell them as much of the true history as she knows, which is considerable.

### Father Beede
*Former monk. Leader of the Trickster movement.*

A self-educated man of common birth who spent decades in a monastery copying texts before discovering, buried in the archive, a collection of documents from the founding era that showed Wotan's true role among the Pentad. The discovery remade him. He left the monastery and began preaching, drawing a following primarily from the common people — those whose relationship with the Pentad has always been more practical than theological.

His movement is theologically sincere and politically inconvenient. The church's official position is that the Trickster is a minor deity, not the Pentad's former leader; Father Beede's documents threaten that position directly. The powerful lay leader working to suppress him is doing so at the intersection of religious orthodoxy and class politics. Beede's followers are common people, and common people who feel newly legitimate are a management problem for those currently doing the managing.

If the PCs kill or capture Father Beede, they will return to a kingdom in social crisis.

## Act I Antagonists

### The Countess
A powerful noblewoman with designs on the throne and the patience to pursue them through conspiracy rather than open rebellion. She is connected, through threads the PCs may or may not discover, to the Priest — the alliance of convenience between an ambitious noble and a theologically motivated church figure provides each with resources the other lacks. Her specific earldom, household, and personal history to be detailed in the GM Guide.

### The Priest
A powerful lay leader of the Pentad who has identified Father Beede's movement as a threat — to theological orthodoxy, to institutional authority, and to the social order his church helps maintain. His intelligence network is extensive and has information useful to the PCs on more than one front. He is not a villain in the conventional sense; he genuinely believes what he is defending is worth defending. He is wrong about the history. The adventure will make that clear.

### The Dragon
A young dragon in the terms of dragonkind, formidable by any other measure, pillaging outlying settlements for reasons of territory and appetite that have nothing to do with the kingdom's political struggles. A straightforward problem with a dangerous solution.

### The Sorceress
An elven sorceress using her powers to bend minds and pursue a revenge that has consumed her. Her lover was killed, and the trail of that death leads back to actions taken by someone within Haven's power structure. She is not evil by nature; she is grief-driven and dangerous, which in practice amounts to the same thing from the receiving end. Whether the PCs engage her through force or negotiation will shape what resources they carry into later quests.

### The Dark Knight
A knight who ran afoul of a fey magician he had defeated through trickery. The fey's response was a curse of terrible elegance: the knight was given great physical strength and freed from any need to eat, drink, or sleep, while his memories were taken entirely. He does not know who he is, what he did, or why he wanders. He kills knights sent to stop him — not from malice, but because that is how each encounter resolves.

He can only die permanently in an honorable one-on-one duel fought without magic or trickery. If defeated by any other means, he will rise again within a month and hunt down those who bested him. There is tragedy in this, and the PCs may choose to see it.

## Adventure Structure

### Act I — Agents of the Crown *(Levels 1–5)*
*In which the PCs are elevated to royal service, given names and a mission, and spend the early months of their careers proving that the Crown's investment was not misplaced — learning, as they do, that the problems they are sent to solve have solutions that leave marks.*

Act I is structured as a **quest pool of five interrelated missions.** All five problems exist simultaneously. All five worsen over time as the party devotes attention to some and not others. The order in which the PCs address them determines what resources and complications they carry forward; the outcomes of each affect the conditions of those that follow.

**Quest 1 — The Countess:** Investigate and neutralize a conspiracy by a powerful noblewoman targeting the Crown's stability. Evidence gathered here may reveal her connection to the Priest.

**Quest 2 — The Priest:** Address the suppression of Father Beede's Trickster movement by a senior Pentad official. The Priest's intelligence network has information that may help the party locate the dragon. The deeper question — whether the movement is a cult or a legitimate theological claim — is for the PCs to investigate and decide.

**Quest 3 — The Dragon:** Hunt and defeat a young dragon raiding the kingdom's outlying settlements. The Priest may have information on the lair's location. Success here shifts the Sorceress's posture.

**Quest 4 — The Sorceress:** Deal with an elven sorceress pursuing revenge through magical coercion. A defeated dragon makes her more wary of direct conflict and more open to negotiation. A negotiated resolution may yield a magic item useful against the Dark Knight.

**Quest 5 — The Dark Knight:** End the threat posed by a cursed knight who cannot die except in specific conditions. Defeating him by any means other than honorable one-on-one combat — no magic, no dirty tricks — results in resurrection within a month and pursuit of those who bested him.

**Quest interconnections:**
- Countess → Priest: Evidence in the Countess's conspiracy connects to the Priest
- Priest → Dragon: The Priest's network holds information on the dragon's lair
- Dragon → Sorceress: Defeating the dragon makes the Sorceress more cautious or negotiable
- Sorceress → Dark Knight: A negotiated Sorceress provides a magic item aiding the duel

**Act I closes** when the five quests are resolved and Harold II, satisfied with what the PCs have demonstrated, formally commissions the Sword Quest and names the heroes' order.

### Act II — The Sword Quest *(Levels 6–9)*
*In which the PCs cross a continent they have never seen, confront the wildness of a world larger and stranger than Haven prepared them for, and are forced to survive a winter on its terms before they can reach what they came for.*

The party travels east from Haven, through the Wild's frontier and into the unmapped interior of the continent. The journey is mostly linear. What the party encounters en route — specific monsters, fey interactions, and magical anomalies — is to be designed in the GM Guide.

**Midpoint: The Mountain Pass Downtime.** At approximately the halfway point, early-setting weather closes the mountain passes and pins the party on the western side of the range. They must find winter shelter. Three options are available:

- **The Dwarven Settlement:** A community of Haven dwarves — loggers and miners operating in the frontier — welcomes the party readily. Wintering with them provides better supplies and equipment for the crossing ahead.
- **The Fey Settlement:** A fey community initially hostile to a party from Haven, though one that can be won over with patience and good faith. Wintering here grants access to magic: spells, magic item crafting, and relationships that may carry forward.
- **Their Own Camp:** The party establishes an independent camp. Harder than either alternative but fully under their control.

The choice of shelter is a small-scale expression of the campaign's larger themes. What the party discovers during the winter about the Wild, its peoples, and Haven's relationship with them will begin the process of historical revision that culminates in Act III.

### Act III — The Far East *(Levels 9–11)*
*In which the party arrives in Aldenmere, meets people who remember the same founder but tell an entirely different story, undergoes the ordeals created to test the Sword's worthiness, and stands briefly in the presence of a man who has been dead for two centuries.*

The party crosses the mountains and enters Aldenmere. The historical revelations accumulate from the moment of arrival — the society's structure, its relationship with the fey, its memory of Cuthbert — and the PCs cannot avoid confronting the gap between what they were taught and what they find.

**Meeting Signi.** The current Redeemer receives the party and hears their story. She believes the time has come to pass the Sword on. She sends the PCs to Reinja with a riddle.

**Reinja's Test.** The elven sorceress evaluates the party's worthiness. If satisfied, she grants them access to the five ordeals.

**The Five Ordeals.** Reached through a passage in the lower reaches of Reinja's castle, the ordeals were designed by Reinja at Cuthbert's direction to test qualities he considered essential in a Redeemer. Each occupies its own impossible space — a self-contained world of magical construction, linked to the others by short corridors of plain stone. The five trials test: **Courage, Wisdom, Insight, Piety, and Sacrifice.** Specific mechanics and structure to be designed in the GM Guide.

**Cuthbert's Spirit.** After the ordeals, the Sword passes to the party. Cuthbert's spirit appears — briefly, without theater. He is not triumphant. He is a man who has had two centuries to think about what he did and why. He tells the truth about his reasons for leaving. He will answer questions if asked. He will not tell the party what to do.

### Act IV — The Return *(Level 12)*
*In which the PCs come home to a kingdom that has not stood still in their absence, armed with an object of immense symbolic power and a knowledge of their homeland's history that no one in Haven possesses. What they do with both is theirs to decide.*

The party returns to Haven. The crises of Act I have escalated in proportion to the choices made there — some problems resolved, others allowed to fester, all of them now several years further along their own trajectories. The precise state of the kingdom on the party's return is determined by Act I outcomes; the range runs from strained but holding to genuinely precarious.

The Sword's return is not a secret. Whatever the party does with the knowledge of its recovery, the symbol matters enormously to Haven's people — and to everyone competing for power over them.

## Possible Endings

The endings of this campaign are not determined by a single choice but by the accumulated decisions of Acts I through IV, culminating in the party's disposition of the Sword. The instrument of power belongs to them. The question is what they do with it.

### Ending 1: The Crown Redeemed
*The party gives the Sword to Harold II.*

Harold II, wielding the Sword of the Redeemer, possesses divine legitimacy no noble house can credibly challenge. He brings the rebellious nobility to heel, pursues meaningful peace with the fey — a peace that will require acknowledging what Haven did to begin the conflict — and works to address the historical injustices his kingdom has spent three centuries accumulating. This is not a clean ending; it is the beginning of a long and difficult process. The PCs become the founding knights of a new royal chivalric order, sworn to protect the kingdom and hold its ruler accountable to the values the Sword represents.

Whether Harold II succeeds in these ambitions depends in part on whether the PCs believe he will, and whether they are willing to hold him to it.

### Ending 2: A Different Path
*The party pursues a resolution of their own devising.*

The adventure does not foreclose player creativity. If the party arrives at a disposition of the Sword not covered by Ending 1, the GM and players work together to determine what the party actually intends and what the kingdom's response will be. The five virtue questions and the Act IV crisis states remain the structural framework for this ending; the GM should use the Ending 1 treatment of each question as a model and adapt the specific events to fit the party's path. The five gods are watching. Cuthbert's ghost spoke. What the party does with that knowledge is theirs to determine.

## Key Mechanics to Design

1. **Quest Pool Framework (Act I):** Mechanism for tracking simultaneous quest progress and deterioration. Problems worsen while unaddressed. Order of completion affects available resources for subsequent quests. Specific deterioration tracks and interconnection triggers to be designed in the GM Guide.

2. **Magic Economy:** No magic shops. The party acquires magical resources through personal crafting, quest rewards from Harold II, gifts from Pentad members who take interest, fey gifts (particularly from the Sorceress if negotiated with and from the fey winter settlement), and items from Reinja or the eastern lands. Magic is significant and costs something — in time, risk, or obligation — every time it enters play.

3. **Historical Revelation Tracker:** A framework for tracking how much of the true history the party has learned. Each major revelation (the goblinoid displacement, Wotan's true role, the Sorceress's legitimate grievance, Cuthbert's actual reasons for leaving) has implications for how the party approaches the endings. The GM should track what the party knows and let it inform NPC interactions.

4. **The Five Ordeals System:** Mechanical framework for the five trials, accessed through a passage within Reinja's castle. Each ordeal tests a specific quality (Courage, Wisdom, Insight, Piety, Sacrifice) through scenario design rather than skill checks alone. Specific mechanics to be designed in the GM Guide.

5. **Winter Downtime Framework (Act II):** Structured downtime period during the mountain pass winter. Activities available vary by choice of shelter. Resources and relationships gained here carry forward into Act III.

6. **Act IV Crisis Framework:** System for determining the state of Haven on the party's return based on Act I outcomes. Each quest resolution creates specific consequences that compound across the years of absence. The GM should be able to describe Haven's condition clearly and trace its causes to choices the party made.

7. **Milestone Leveling:** Advancement tied to act completion and significant plot beats. Characters begin at Level 1.

## Institutions and Factions

### Internal to Haven

**The Crown (Harold II)**
The party's patron and the institution they are initially loyal to. Harold II is not an enemy. He is a man trying to hold something together with inadequate tools, attempting to build something better, and not entirely sure he will succeed. His interests and the party's will align for most of the campaign and may diverge sharply at its end.

**The Pentad**
Haven's established church is not a monolith. The institution headquartered in Barstow represents the dominant theological current — one that has systematically reduced Wotan to a minor role and supports the social order that the nobility and Crown find useful. Father Beede's movement represents a genuine alternative rooted in the founding documents. By the campaign's end, the PCs will have enough information to understand how the Pentad's theology diverged from its origins, and what restoring Wotan's proper place would actually mean.

**The Nobility**
Nine Earls with their own interests, histories, and relationships to the Crown. Four of them rebelled against Harold II within living memory. Each represents a potential ally, a potential obstacle, or a potential recipient of the Sword, depending on who they are and what the party has done in their territory during Act I.

**The Common People**
Haven's serfs, craftspeople, and free farmers have little formal political power and considerable latent energy. Father Beede's movement is the most visible expression of their interests. How the party treats this population during Acts I and II shapes the conditions of Act IV regardless of which ending the party pursues.

### External

**The Wild's Peoples**
The fey of the Wild are not a single faction. They are a collection of peoples, communities, and traditions with varying relationships to Haven's borders. Some have reached grudging accommodation with the kingdom's frontier settlements. Many have not. What they share is three centuries of accumulated grievance against a kingdom that took their neighbors' land, blamed the resulting war on those it wronged, and then blamed the fey for defending them.

**The Goblinoids**
Pushed into the Wild by the exile fleet's arrival, the goblinoid peoples have spent three centuries rebuilding. They have made a wary peace with the fey peoples they were forced among and maintained an enduring hatred for Haven. Their raids against the kingdom's borders are not savagery; they are the persistent expression of a wrong that has never been acknowledged, let alone addressed.

**Aldenmere**
Not a formal faction but a collection of communities in the eastern lands. Loosely governed, magically practiced, and possessed of the complete history that Haven's official account is missing. Their disposition toward the party begins as cautious — a westerner bearing Haven's symbols is not a recommendation — and depends entirely on how the party conducts itself.

## Calendar and Significant Events

The campaign does not use a fixed-event calendar in the sense that a single pivotal night drives the adventure's hinge. Instead, time pressure is created through the deterioration of unaddressed problems in Act I and the approach of winter in Act II.

**Act I Timeline:** The five quests run simultaneously from the party's commissioning. Each passing month without resolution allows problems to worsen. The party should feel the calendar as pressure on their decisions, not as a countdown to a fixed event.

**The Winter Crossing (Act II):** The mountain passes close seasonally. The party must cross before they do, winter on one side of the range, and cross when they reopen. The downtime period spans several months. Time spent here is not time wasted — it is the campaign's only built-in breathing room.

**The Years of Absence:** The party is gone from Haven for a significant period — the journey east, the winter, the time in Aldenmere for the ordeals, and the return journey. Several years pass. The crises of Act I have had time to compound. The kingdom the party returns to has changed.

## Chapter 7: The Five Ordeals — Design Notes

### The Questing Beast Interlude — STATUS: WRITTEN

Available only when the party fails an ordeal. One use only. Reinja reveals the option only if asked three times; she names Halsin (her son by Cuthbert) as the only prior finder.

**The Beast:** Body of a great hunting cat (black with white spots), legs and hooves of a great hind, fox's tail, serpentine neck, doe's head with twisting antlers. Sound emanates from within its body — distant hounds in full cry, no louder at close range than at a mile's distance. Iron key on a braided cord around its neck. Unearthly beauty; cannot be mistaken for any other creature.

**The Journey:** Northern wilderness of Aldenmere — dangerous mountains, deep lakes, hidden valleys; stranger and more hazardous than anything the party has crossed. Minimum six months (party found fully worthy, no challenge failures). Each failed wilderness challenge adds one month; unworthy baseline is seven months.

**Dream tests:** Five visits across the journey, one per virtue, beast never speaks. Fully worthy party: wilderness challenges at standard difficulty, Beast yields key willingly at the High Mere at dusk. Unworthy party: elevated DCs throughout, Beast must be subdued (nonlethal only; stat block in Appendix A).

**Five wilderness challenges** (virtue/location):
1. Sacrifice — Rope Bridge (Climb DC 20, Reflex DC 22 on failure; +4 DC if unworthy)
2. Wisdom — Drowned Valley (Survival or Knowledge [Nature] DC 22 to read route; +4 DC if unworthy)
3. Insight — Deep Lake (Knowledge DC 20 to identify gesture of passage; spirit becomes aggressive if unworthy)
4. Piety — Trickster's Shrine (honest answers to old figure's questions earn Beast's location; figure absent for unworthy party)
5. Courage — Ice Cathedral (Acrobatics or Escape Artist DC 22 through unstable overhang; DC 28 if unworthy)

**On success:** Party resumes ordeals from the point of failure with all resources recovered. Journey time adds normally to their absence from Haven.

## Chapter 10: The Reckoning — Design Notes

### Structure

Act IV is structured around five virtue questions drawn from the five ordeals: **Sacrifice, Wisdom, Insight, Piety, Courage.** The first four are roleplay challenges; the fifth (Courage) is a combat challenge. Together they constitute the full dramatic arc of the final act. The virtue questions are not scored — they are evaluated, and the way the party answers shapes the specific events and conditions of their ending.

The two primary tracks running through all endings are determined by the Countess quest outcome: **Harold holds the throne (peace)** or **Harold has been driven to Landing (civil war).**

### NPC Additions (Act IV)

- **Sorcha:** The Magister's chief apprentice. Human Arcanist 12 (CR 11). Introduced at the pre-Test of Five council; volunteers to support the king when the Magister declines. The Magister accepts but cuts her loose — no more teaching after this. Fights in the Test of Five or joins the Sorella rescue depending on party composition. Stat block in Appendix A.
- **Father Marne (expanded):** Inquisitor of Barstow; antagonist from Act I Quest 2. Returns in Ending 1 (peace track) as the mystery White Knight who wins the tournament, reveals himself, and calls the Test of Five. Human Paladin 14 (CR 13). Stat block in Appendix A.
